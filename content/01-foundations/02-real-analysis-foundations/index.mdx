You likely already know that $\lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} = 0$. You probably wrote arguments like "as $n$ gets very large, $1/n$ gets very small and approaches zero." That reasoning is correct, but it is not mathematics. "Very large," "very small," "approaches" — these are physical intuitions dressed up as definitions. The great achievement of nineteenth-century analysis was replacing them with something watertight.

This is not pedantry. The history of mathematics is littered with results that seemed obviously true from intuition but were false, and with objects — continuous nowhere-differentiable functions, space-filling curves — that could not even be conceived without precise definitions. You need the precision before you can trust the intuition.

## What is a real number?

Before sequences, you need to know what the real numbers are. You have been using them since school, but what exactly is $\pi$, or $\sqrt{2}$, or $e$? They are not fractions. They are limits of sequences of fractions — and to define limits, you need to already have the real numbers. This circularity troubled mathematicians for much of the nineteenth century.

The resolution came from [Dedekind](/mathematicians/dedekind) and [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) in the 1870s: the real numbers are constructed from the rationals by completing them — adding all the limit points of sequences that "ought to converge" but whose limit is not rational. The key property that distinguishes $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ is the **completeness axiom**: every non-empty set of real numbers that is bounded above has a least upper bound (supremum). This is not a theorem; it is a definition of what the reals are.

You will use completeness constantly. The Intermediate Value Theorem, the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem, the existence of the Riemann integral — all depend on it, usually via the supremum axiom or one of its equivalent formulations.

## Sequences and convergence

A **sequence** of real numbers is a function $a : \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{R}$, written $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ or simply $(a_n)$. The formal definition of convergence, due to [Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy) and polished by [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass), is:

> The sequence $(a_n)$ **converges** to the limit $L \in \mathbb{R}$ if: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ such that for all $n > N$, $|a_n - L| < \varepsilon$.

Written in symbols: $\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists N \in \mathbb{N},\ \forall n > N: |a_n - L| < \varepsilon$.

The logical structure matters enormously. The quantifiers are in the order $\forall\varepsilon, \exists N, \forall n$: you (the challenger) pick $\varepsilon$ first; only after seeing your $\varepsilon$ does the sequence (the defendant) produce an $N$; and then the condition must hold for all $n$ beyond that $N$. Swapping the quantifiers — $\exists N, \forall \varepsilon, \forall n$ — is a completely different statement.

This is the bit that catches students out. Spend time with a few examples before you believe you understand the definition.

**Example.** Prove that $a_n = \frac{1}{n} \to 0$.

Given $\varepsilon > 0$, we need $|1/n - 0| = 1/n < \varepsilon$ for all sufficiently large $n$. Choose $N = \lceil 1/\varepsilon \rceil$ (the smallest integer $\ge 1/\varepsilon$). Then for any $n > N$, we have $n > 1/\varepsilon$, so $1/n < \varepsilon$. Done.

Every epsilon-delta proof has this shape: guess the right $N$ or $\delta$, then verify it works.

## Key results about sequences

Several theorems follow from the definition and the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$:

**Monotone Convergence Theorem.** Every bounded monotone sequence converges. (A sequence is *monotone increasing* if $a_{n+1} \ge a_n$ for all $n$.) This is used to define $e = \lim_{n \to \infty}(1 + 1/n)^n$.

**Bolzano-Weierstrass Theorem.** Every bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence. This is proved using the supremum axiom. [Bolzano](/mathematicians/bolzano) established versions of this result decades before [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) made it a cornerstone of rigorous analysis.

**Cauchy sequences.** A sequence is **Cauchy** if its terms eventually get arbitrarily close to each other: $\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists N: |a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$ whenever $m, n > N$. The critical theorem: in $\mathbb{R}$, every Cauchy sequence converges. (This fails in $\mathbb{Q}$, which is why the rationals are "incomplete.")

## Series

A **series** $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$ is defined as the limit of its partial sums $S_N = \sum_{n=1}^N a_n$. The series converges if $(S_N)$ converges as a sequence.

Testing convergence without computing the sum explicitly is a central skill. Among the tests you will meet:

- **Comparison test**: if $0 \le a_n \le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges, so does $\sum a_n$.
- **Ratio test**: if $|a_{n+1}/a_n| \to L < 1$, the series converges absolutely.
- **Alternating series test**: an alternating series $\sum (-1)^n b_n$ with $b_n \searrow 0$ converges.

The distinction between **absolute convergence** ($\sum |a_n| < \infty$) and **conditional convergence** is subtle and important. The Riemann rearrangement theorem states that any conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any real number you like — or to diverge. This sounds absurd; it is a theorem.

## Continuity

A function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is **continuous at $a$** if $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$. The epsilon-delta version: $\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists \delta > 0: |x - a| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon$.

The **Intermediate Value Theorem** says: if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$, then there exists $c \in (a,b)$ with $f(c) = 0$. Intuitively obvious; proving it rigorously requires the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$.

The **Extreme Value Theorem** says: if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, it attains its maximum and minimum. Again, obvious from a picture; the proof uses the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: the precise notion of convergence you develop here underpins every iterative algorithm in computation — [robotics and control systems](/applications/robotics-and-control) rely on fixed-point iteration (via the Banach contraction mapping theorem) to prove that their update rules converge to a stable solution. The continuity theory for functions is the foundation on which [numerical methods](/03-specialist-areas/05-numerical-analysis) base their error guarantees. Even [deep learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) depends on Lipschitz conditions derived from the epsilon-delta framework to guarantee that gradient descent converges.

## Why this matters

Real analysis is the foundation everything else rests on. When you differentiate a function, integrate it, or claim that a series converges, you are implicitly using results proved in real analysis. In core mathematics, analysis extends to metric spaces (replacing $|x - y|$ with a general "distance function") and then to functional analysis (infinite-dimensional spaces). Every step of that staircase has the epsilon-delta language of foundational topics at its base.

There is also something philosophically important happening here. Mathematics is the one subject where you do not have to take anyone's word for anything. Every statement has a proof, and every proof can be checked from first principles. Real analysis is the first place a degree student is really asked to live by that standard — and it changes how you think about every claim you will ever encounter, mathematical or otherwise.
