Coming in, you may already know how to manipulate matrices, solve simultaneous equations, and find eigenvalues of small matrices. What you may not yet know is *why* any of it works, or what happens when the familiar $2 \times 2$ case generalises to $n$ dimensions, or to abstract vector spaces where the "vectors" are functions rather than arrows. That is what the algebra strand of the foundations gives you.

## Sets, functions, and the grammar of mathematics

Everything in modern mathematics is built on sets. A set is simply a collection of objects: $\{1, 2, 3\}$, the set of all real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, the set of all continuous functions from $[0,1]$ to $\mathbb{R}$. The power of sets is not in this definition — it is in the precise language they provide for talking about everything else.

A **function** $f : A \to B$ assigns to each element of the set $A$ exactly one element of the set $B$. The set $A$ is the *domain* and $B$ is the *codomain*. Functions are classified by their behaviour:

- **Injective** (one-to-one): $f(a_1) = f(a_2) \Rightarrow a_1 = a_2$. Distinct inputs give distinct outputs.
- **Surjective** (onto): every element of $B$ is the image of at least one element of $A$.
- **Bijective**: both injective and surjective. A bijection between $A$ and $B$ establishes a perfect pairing — every element of $A$ corresponds to exactly one element of $B$, and vice versa.

You will use these terms constantly throughout the degree. A bijection from $A$ to itself is a *permutation* — and permutations, it turns out, are the heart of group theory.

**Relations** generalise functions. An **equivalence relation** on a set partitions it into classes of "equivalent" elements. The classic example is arithmetic modulo $n$: declare two integers equivalent if they have the same remainder on division by $n$. The resulting equivalence classes $\{[0], [1], \ldots, [n-1]\}$ form the integers modulo $n$, written $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Z}_n$. You can add and multiply these classes, and you get a perfectly consistent arithmetic. This is the foundation of cryptography.

## The integers and the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

[Euclid's](/mathematicians/euclid) algorithm is one of the oldest pieces of mathematics still in everyday use: given two positive integers $a$ and $b$, repeatedly replace the larger by the remainder after dividing by the smaller, until you reach zero. The last non-zero remainder is the greatest common divisor, $\gcd(a, b)$.

$$\gcd(48, 18): \quad 48 = 2 \cdot 18 + 12, \quad 18 = 1 \cdot 12 + 6, \quad 12 = 2 \cdot 6. \quad \gcd = 6.$$

The Euclidean algorithm does more than compute a gcd. It gives you the **extended Euclidean algorithm**, which finds integers $x, y$ such that $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$. This identity is the key to almost everything in elementary number theory.

The **Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic** states that every integer greater than $1$ has a unique factorisation as a product of primes (up to the order of factors). This seems obvious — try proving it rigorously and you will discover it requires careful work.

## Complex numbers revisited

You already know that $i^2 = -1$ defines the imaginary unit. What university mathematics adds is [Euler's](/mathematicians/euler) formula:

$$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.$$

This is not a definition; it is a theorem, proved using the power series for $e^x$, $\cos\theta$, and $\sin\theta$. Setting $\theta = \pi$ gives $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ — Euler's identity, which connects the five most fundamental constants in mathematics.

The formula transforms complex arithmetic. Multiplying $re^{i\theta} \cdot se^{i\phi} = rse^{i(\theta+\phi)}$ shows that multiplication in $\mathbb{C}$ simultaneously scales by moduli and adds arguments. **De Moivre's theorem** — $(cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)$ — follows immediately and gives you the $n$th roots of any complex number.

## Matrices and linear algebra

You can solve a system of linear equations $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ by **Gaussian elimination**: write down the augmented matrix $[A \mid \mathbf{b}]$ and apply row operations to reduce it to echelon form. This process, systematised by [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss), is the foundation of all computational linear algebra.

foundational topics goes much further. A **vector space** $V$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ is a set with two operations (addition and scalar multiplication) satisfying eight axioms. The vectors do not have to be arrows: the set of all polynomials of degree $\le n$, the set of all $m \times n$ matrices, and the set of all continuous functions on $[0,1]$ are all vector spaces.

A **subspace** is a subset of $V$ that is itself a vector space. A set of vectors $\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\}$ is **linearly independent** if the only solution to $\alpha_1 v_1 + \cdots + \alpha_k v_k = 0$ is $\alpha_1 = \cdots = \alpha_k = 0$. A **basis** is a linearly independent set that spans the whole space; the number of basis vectors is the **dimension**. Every finite-dimensional vector space has a basis (this requires proof), and any two bases have the same number of elements.

The **rank-nullity theorem** says that for any linear map $T : V \to W$:
$$\dim(\ker T) + \dim(\text{im}\, T) = \dim V.$$
This is one of those beautiful equations that says something profound in very few symbols: the dimensions of the "things $T$ destroys" and the "things $T$ creates" add up to the dimension of the whole space.

## Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

A vector $\mathbf{v} \ne 0$ is an **eigenvector** of a matrix $A$ with **eigenvalue** $\lambda$ if $A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v}$. The matrix does not rotate $\mathbf{v}$; it only stretches (or compresses, or reverses) it.

To find eigenvalues, solve the **characteristic equation**:
$$\det(A - \lambda I) = 0.$$
This gives a polynomial in $\lambda$ — the characteristic polynomial — whose roots are the eigenvalues. The eigenvectors for each eigenvalue form the corresponding **eigenspace** (the kernel of $A - \lambda I$).

Eigenvalues are everywhere: Google's PageRank algorithm is an eigenvalue computation on the web-link matrix; quantum mechanics expresses observable quantities as eigenvalues of Hermitian operators; principal component analysis in statistics finds the eigenvectors of a covariance matrix. The concept is worth understanding deeply.

In foundational topics you will meet the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices (real symmetric matrices have orthonormal eigenbases of real eigenvalues) and get the first hints of the general Jordan Normal Form theory that appears in core mathematics.

## Polynomials and a first taste of algebra

Polynomial arithmetic has a striking parallel with integer arithmetic. Both support a division algorithm, a notion of "gcd" (via Euclid's algorithm again, now for polynomials), and a concept of prime/irreducible elements. The **factor theorem** says $a$ is a root of polynomial $p(x)$ if and only if $(x - a)$ divides $p(x)$. The **coprimality criterion** for polynomials mirrors the criterion for integers.

This parallel is not a coincidence: both $\mathbb{Z}$ and the polynomial ring $\mathbb{F}[x]$ are examples of **Euclidean domains** — an abstract algebraic structure that appears in core mathematics. Recognising structural parallels is one of the great skills that mathematics teaches.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [RSA and Public-Key Cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) secures every HTTPS connection using modular arithmetic and Euclid's algorithm — the very tools introduced here. [PageRank](/applications/pagerank-google) computes web-page importance as the dominant eigenvector of the link matrix, a billion-dimensional version of the eigenvalue problem you learn in this course. [GPS positioning](/applications/gps-positioning) solves an over-determined linear system to locate your phone; [machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) trains neural networks whose core arithmetic is batched matrix multiplication at enormous scale.

## Where this leads

The algebra of foundational topics feeds into almost every subsequent part of the degree. Abstract algebra in core mathematics ([Groups and Rings](/02-core-mathematics/01-abstract-algebra)) takes the structures hinted at here and builds a general theory. Linear algebra deepens in core mathematics with the Jordan Normal Form theorem. The eigenvalue language turns up in differential equations (the matrix exponential, stability analysis), numerical analysis (QR algorithm), and statistics (principal components, covariance).

The habit of proof that algebra requires — precise definitions, step-by-step logical deductions, quantifiers in the right order — is the habit the whole degree runs on.
