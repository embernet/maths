## Before You Arrive: The Essential Eight

These eight books are worth your time before your first lecture. Not all of them are content books — some are about how to think and how to work. University mathematics makes demands on both.

---

### 1. Martin Liebeck — *A Concise Introduction to Pure Mathematics* (CRC Press, 4th ed.)

The single best pre-arrival book for a new mathematics undergraduate. Liebeck covers exactly what Year One demands: number theory, the real number system, complex numbers, sets, functions, and proof by induction and contradiction. The chapters are short and the style is clear. If you read only one book from this list before arriving, read this one.

---

### 2. G. Pólya — *How to Solve It* (Princeton University Press)

Not a content book but a thinking book. Pólya's four-step framework — understand the problem, devise a plan, carry it out, look back — and his catalogue of heuristic strategies are as close as mathematics has to a systematic guide to problem-solving. Indispensable for the tutorial culture of a maths degree, where you are expected to attack unseen problems.

---

### 3. Timothy Gowers — *Mathematics: A Very Short Introduction* (Oxford University Press)

Written by a Fields Medallist in 150 pages. Covers what university mathematics actually is, why abstraction is not an affectation, and why the move from calculation to proof is the right one. Read this on the train to university on arrival day.

---

### 4. Kevin Houston — *How to Think Like a Mathematician* (Cambridge University Press)

Explicitly teaches proof-writing, mathematical reading, and how to engage with definitions and theorems. Bridges the gap between A-level "doing maths" and university "understanding maths." Particularly recommended for anyone anxious about the proof transition — which includes most new students, regardless of their A-level grade.

---

### 5. G. H. Hardy — *A Course of Pure Mathematics* (Cambridge University Press, 10th ed.)

The classic British pure mathematics text, published in 1908 and still in print. Chapters on real and complex numbers, limits, sequences, series, and differentiation. Harder than Liebeck; rewarding for students who want to go deep before arriving. Hardy's prose is superb.

---

### 6. G. Smith — *Introductory Mathematics: Algebra and Analysis* (Springer SUMS series)

Covers the algebra and analysis material of a typical first-year foundations unit. If you want to prepare for a specific strand of Year One, this is the most targeted option.

---

### 7. Richard Courant & Herbert Robbins — *What is Mathematics?* (Oxford University Press)

A panoramic overview, first published in 1941 and still outstanding. Covers number theory, geometry, topology, and analysis in a style that is rigorous without being dry. Good for students who want a broad sense of the mathematical landscape before specialising.

---

### 8. Lara Alcock — *How to Study for a Mathematics Degree* (Oxford University Press)

Practical, honest advice on attending lectures, engaging with definitions, working through proofs, and revising for examinations. Particularly useful for students who achieved A\* at A-level without needing to work very hard: university mathematics will be different, and this book explains why and what to do about it.

## Free Online Resources

### Video and Visual

- **3Blue1Brown** — [3b1b.co](https://www.3b1b.co). Grant Sanderson's "Essence of Linear Algebra" and "Essence of Calculus" series build spatial intuition in a way that written texts rarely achieve. Watch them before or alongside Year One. Everything he makes is worth your time.

- **MIT OpenCourseWare** — [ocw.mit.edu](https://ocw.mit.edu). Lecture notes, problem sets, and examinations for 18.01 (single-variable calculus), 18.02 (multivariable calculus), and 18.03 (ordinary differential equations) are freely available and of excellent quality. A useful parallel track alongside your own lecture course.

### Reference and Computation

- **Paul's Online Math Notes** — [tutorial.math.lamar.edu](https://tutorial.math.lamar.edu). Comprehensive, clearly written worked examples for calculus, differential equations, and linear algebra. Useful when you need to see a technique in action before attempting it yourself.

- **Desmos** — [desmos.com](https://www.desmos.com). Free graphing calculator in your browser. Invaluable for visualising functions, level curves, vector fields, and phase planes. If you are unsure what a function looks like, draw it.

- **Wolfram Alpha** — [wolframalpha.com](https://www.wolframalpha.com). Symbolic computation, integration, differentiation, series expansion. Useful for checking answers and exploring examples. Not a substitute for understanding — a wrong method that produces the right Wolfram Alpha output is still wrong.

### History

- **MacTutor History of Mathematics Archive** — [mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk). Biographical essays on every mathematician mentioned in this guide, and several hundred more. Written by historians of mathematics and carefully sourced. When a theorem is named after someone, it is worth five minutes reading who they were.

## A-Level Revision Checklist

The following topics should be revision-ready before your first lecture. This is not a list of things that will be re-taught; it is a list of things the degree assumes you already know fluently.

### Algebra

- Binomial theorem (including the general binomial expansion)
- Partial fractions; completing the square
- Arithmetic and geometric series, including infinite geometric series and convergence condition
- Polynomial long division; factor theorem; remainder theorem
- Indices and logarithm rules, including natural logarithm and $e^x$

### Calculus

- Differentiation: product rule, quotient rule, chain rule, implicit differentiation
- Integration: by substitution, by parts, using partial fractions
- First-order differential equations: separation of variables, integrating factor
- Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (both parts, stated clearly)

### Functions and Graphs

- Composition of functions; inverse functions
- Even, odd, and periodic functions; their graph symmetries
- Transformations of graphs ($y = af(bx + c) + d$ etc.)
- Modulus function; solving equations and inequalities involving $|x|$

### Trigonometry

- All standard identities: addition formulae, double angle, $R\sin(x + \alpha)$ form
- Inverse trigonometric functions; their domains and ranges
- Radians; arc length; sector area

### Further Mathematics: Complex Numbers

- Cartesian form $a + bi$ and modulus-argument form $r e^{i\theta}$
- De Moivre's theorem and its applications
- Loci in the Argand diagram

### Further Mathematics: Matrices

- $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ determinants
- Inverse of a $2 \times 2$ matrix
- Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $2 \times 2$ matrices; geometric interpretation

### Statistics (Awareness Level)

- Discrete and continuous probability distributions: Binomial, Poisson, Normal
- Hypothesis testing: null hypothesis, significance level, $p$-value, Type I and Type II errors

### Programming (Desirable, Not Required)

- Basic Python: variables, loops, functions, plotting with `matplotlib`
- If you have no Python experience, 10 hours on a free Python tutorial (Codecademy, Python.org, CS50P) before arriving will pay dividends in Year One computing units.

## What to Read After Year One

The pre-arrival books are for the bridge. Once you are inside the degree, the following texts are worth knowing about.

**Analysis:** Walter Rudin's *Principles of Mathematical Analysis* (affectionately known as "Baby Rudin") is the gold standard for a concise, rigorous treatment of real analysis. Stephen Abbott's *Understanding Analysis* is gentler and better for building intuition first. Mary Hart's *Guide to Analysis* is readable and accessible, particularly for Sequences and Series.

**Linear Algebra:** Sheldon Axler's *Linear Algebra Done Right* deliberately avoids determinants until late, organising the subject around linear maps and eigenvalues in a way that many students find more illuminating than the determinant-first approach. Gilbert Strang's *Introduction to Linear Algebra* is computational and geometric; his lecture videos on MIT OCW are excellent.

**Abstract Algebra:** Dummit and Foote's *Abstract Algebra* is the standard graduate-level reference; Michael Artin's *Algebra* is more accessible and has a geometric flavour. Herstein's *Topics in Algebra* is the classic undergraduate text.

**Complex Analysis:** Needham's *Visual Complex Analysis* uses geometric intuition to make the subject feel as concrete as possible before it becomes abstract. Stein and Shakarchi's *Complex Analysis* (Princeton Lectures in Analysis, Vol. 2) is more rigorous and builds to the Riemann zeta function.

**Topology:** Munkres's *Topology* is the standard reference. Armstrong's *Basic Topology* is shorter and more intuitive.

**Number Theory:** Hardy and Wright's *An Introduction to the Theory of Numbers* is the classic text; Ireland and Rosen's *A Classical Introduction to Modern Number Theory* is more modern and algebraic.

## A Final Note

Mathematics is a subject you do, not one you watch. The books and videos here will give you language, context, and intuition — but the understanding comes from working through problems, getting stuck, and then seeing why the argument works. The mathematicians in this guide spent their careers in that process. So will you.
