The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ enjoy unique prime factorisation: every integer factors into primes in exactly one way. When you pass to the integers of a number field — the natural analogues of $\mathbb{Z}$ for algebraic extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$ — this can fail. Algebraic number theory is the story of what breaks, and how the theory of ideals repairs it.

## Number fields and the ring of integers

A **number field** $K$ is a finite extension $[\mathbb{Q}: K]$ of $\mathbb{Q}$. The simplest examples are quadratic fields $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ for square-free integers $d$, and cyclotomic fields $K = \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ where $\zeta_n = e^{2\pi i/n}$ is a primitive $n$-th root of unity.

The **ring of integers** $\mathcal{O}_K$ consists of all elements of $K$ that satisfy a monic polynomial with coefficients in $\mathbb{Z}$:

$$\mathcal{O}_K = \{\alpha \in K : \exists\, x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0 = 0,\ a_i \in \mathbb{Z}\}.$$

For $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$, the ring of integers is $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}] = \{a + b\sqrt{-5} : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. This ring has a surprising failure: $6 = 2 \cdot 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, and these two factorisations are genuinely different — none of the four factors is a unit multiple of any other. Unique factorisation breaks down.

## Ideals and Dedekind domains

The repair comes from working with **ideals** rather than elements. An ideal $\mathfrak{a} \subset \mathcal{O}_K$ is a subgroup closed under multiplication by elements of $\mathcal{O}_K$. The **product** of two ideals $\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}$ is the ideal generated by all products $ab$ with $a \in \mathfrak{a}$, $b \in \mathfrak{b}$.

$\mathcal{O}_K$ is a **Dedekind domain**: an integral domain that is Noetherian, integrally closed in its fraction field, and has Krull dimension 1 (every non-zero prime ideal is maximal). The fundamental theorem for Dedekind domains states: every non-zero ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals. The failure of unique element factorisation is perfectly compensated — ideals factor uniquely even when elements do not.

In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, the ideal $(6) = \mathfrak{p}_2 \cdot \mathfrak{p}_3 \cdot \overline{\mathfrak{p}}_3$ where $\mathfrak{p}_2 = (2, 1+\sqrt{-5})$ and $\mathfrak{p}_3 = (3, 1 + \sqrt{-5})$ — and this factorisation is unique. The two element-factorisations of $6$ are simply two ways of grouping ideal factors together.

## The norm, the trace, and ramification

The **norm** $N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha) = \prod_{\sigma} \sigma(\alpha)$ (product over all embeddings $\sigma : K \hookrightarrow \mathbb{C}$) and the **trace** $\mathrm{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha) = \sum_{\sigma} \sigma(\alpha)$ are fundamental invariants: they are rational integers when $\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K$. The norm of an ideal $\mathfrak{a}$ is the index $[\mathcal{O}_K : \mathfrak{a}]$, a positive integer.

A rational prime $p \in \mathbb{Z}$ **ramifies** in $K$ if the ideal $(p) \subset \mathcal{O}_K$ contains a repeated prime ideal factor: $(p) = \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1} \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r^{e_r}$ with some $e_i \ge 2$. Ramification is intimately connected to the discriminant of $K$ — a finite set of primes that reflects the arithmetic complexity of the extension.

## The ideal class group and class number

The set of fractional ideals (formal products of ideals and their inverses) of $\mathcal{O}_K$ forms a group under multiplication. The **principal fractional ideals** $(\alpha) = \alpha \mathcal{O}_K$ form a subgroup. The quotient

$$\mathrm{Cl}(K) = \{\text{fractional ideals}\} / \{\text{principal ideals}\}$$

is the **ideal class group**. Its order $h_K = |\mathrm{Cl}(K)|$ is the **class number**. The class number measures exactly how far $\mathcal{O}_K$ is from having unique element factorisation: $h_K = 1$ if and only if $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a principal ideal domain, in which case unique factorisation of elements holds.

For $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, one can show $h_K = 2$: there are two ideal classes. The connection to Fermat's Last Theorem is deep — Kummer attempted a proof using cyclotomic fields $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$, but his argument required $h_{\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)} = 1$, which fails for the prime $p = 23$. Understanding this failure led directly to the development of ideal theory.

## Dirichlet's unit theorem

The group $\mathcal{O}_K^\times$ of units (invertible elements) of $\mathcal{O}_K$ has a precise structure. **Dirichlet's unit theorem** states:

$$\mathcal{O}_K^\times \cong \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^{r_1 + r_2 - 1},$$

where $\mu_K$ is the finite cyclic group of roots of unity in $K$, $r_1$ is the number of real embeddings of $K$, and $r_2$ is the number of pairs of complex embeddings. For $K = \mathbb{Q}$ this gives $\mathbb{Z}^\times = \{\pm 1\}$ (the units $1$ and $-1$); for a real quadratic field like $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$, the unit group is $\{\pm 1\} \times \mathbb{Z}$, generated by the fundamental unit $1 + \sqrt{2}$.

## Real-world applications

The arithmetic of number fields is foundational to modern cryptography. [RSA public-key cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) relies on the difficulty of factoring large integers, and its security analysis uses the theory of prime ideals in rings of integers. [Digital signatures](/applications/digital-signatures) built on lattice-based cryptography — the leading post-quantum standard — are described entirely in the language of algebraic number theory: the security hardness assumptions involve ideal lattices in cyclotomic rings $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{2^k}]$.

## Why this matters

Algebraic number theory is not just a repair job on the integers — it is a vast and beautiful theory with connections running in every direction. The class number formula connects $h_K$, the regulator, and the Dedekind zeta function $\zeta_K(s)$ in a single identity, which in turn leads to the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (one of the Clay Millennium Problems). The Langlands programme — arguably the broadest framework in modern mathematics — begins with Galois representations on the $p$-adic cohomology of algebraic varieties, which is algebraic number theory in a deep disguise. Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem is, at its core, a theorem about the arithmetic of elliptic curves over number fields, proved by showing that a certain Galois representation is modular.
