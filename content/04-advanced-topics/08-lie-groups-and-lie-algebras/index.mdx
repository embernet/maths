A Lie group is both a group and a smooth manifold, with the group operations smooth. This small combination — symmetry plus smoothness — produces an extraordinarily rich theory. Every continuous symmetry in physics is a Lie group action; the standard model of particle physics is essentially a list of the irreducible representations of certain Lie groups.

## Lie groups: definition and examples

A **Lie group** $G$ is a smooth manifold equipped with a group structure such that the multiplication map $G \times G \to G$, $(g, h) \mapsto gh$, and the inversion map $G \to G$, $g \mapsto g^{-1}$, are both smooth. The compatibility between the smooth and algebraic structures is the key.

The most important examples are **matrix groups**: subgroups of $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$ or $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{C})$ defined by smooth equations.

- $\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb{R})$: all invertible real $n \times n$ matrices (an open subset of $\mathbb{R}^{n^2}$, hence a manifold).
- $\mathrm{SO}(n)$: orthogonal matrices with determinant $1$ — the rotation group in $n$ dimensions. $\mathrm{SO}(2) \cong S^1$ (circle), $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ is the group of rotations of 3-space.
- $\mathrm{SU}(n)$: unitary matrices with determinant $1$. $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ is diffeomorphic to $S^3$ and is the double cover of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$; $\mathrm{SU}(3)$ governs the strong nuclear force in the standard model.
- $\mathrm{U}(n) = \{A \in M_n(\mathbb{C}) : A^\dagger A = I\}$: unitary matrices. $\mathrm{U}(1) \cong S^1$ is the gauge group of electromagnetism.

## The Lie algebra: tangent space at the identity

For a Lie group $G$, the **Lie algebra** $\mathfrak{g} = T_e G$ is the tangent space at the identity element $e$. As a vector space it has dimension $\dim G$. What makes it more than a vector space is the **Lie bracket** $[\cdot, \cdot] : \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g}$, defined by

$$[X, Y] = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{d}{dt}\bigg|_{t=0} \mathrm{Ad}_{e^{tX}}(Y),$$

where $\mathrm{Ad}_g(Y)$ is the differential of conjugation by $g$. The bracket is bilinear, anti-symmetric ($[X, Y] = -[Y, X]$), and satisfies the **Jacobi identity**:

$$[X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0.$$

For matrix groups, the bracket is simply the commutator: $[X, Y] = XY - YX$. The Lie algebras of the main examples are: $\mathfrak{so}(n)$ (skew-symmetric matrices), $\mathfrak{su}(n)$ (skew-Hermitian traceless matrices), $\mathfrak{gl}_n$ (all $n\times n$ matrices).

## The exponential map

The **exponential map** $\exp : \mathfrak{g} \to G$ connects the Lie algebra back to the Lie group. For matrix groups, it is the matrix exponential:

$$\exp(X) = I + X + \frac{X^2}{2!} + \frac{X^3}{3!} + \cdots$$

This converges for all $X \in M_n(\mathbb{R})$ and satisfies $\exp(tX)\exp(sX) = \exp((t+s)X)$: the one-parameter subgroup generated by $X$. In general, $\exp$ is a local diffeomorphism near the identity, and every element of a connected Lie group is a product of elements of the form $\exp(X)$.

The exponential map linearises the group structure near the identity: two elements $\exp(X)$ and $\exp(Y)$ satisfy $\exp(X)\exp(Y) = \exp(X + Y + \tfrac{1}{2}[X,Y] + \cdots)$ (the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula), where all subsequent terms involve iterated brackets.

## Root systems and Cartan subalgebras

A **semisimple** Lie algebra is one with no non-zero solvable ideals — all the "simple" building blocks. A **Cartan subalgebra** $\mathfrak{h} \subset \mathfrak{g}$ is a maximal abelian subalgebra of semisimple elements. The adjoint action of $\mathfrak{h}$ on $\mathfrak{g}$ diagonalises simultaneously, giving a decomposition

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{h} \oplus \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Phi} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha,$$

where each $\mathfrak{g}_\alpha = \{X \in \mathfrak{g} : [H, X] = \alpha(H)X\ \forall H \in \mathfrak{h}\}$ is a one-dimensional eigenspace and $\alpha \in \mathfrak{h}^*$ is a **root**. The set $\Phi$ of roots has a very rigid geometric structure: it lies in a Euclidean space and is invariant under reflections in the hyperplanes perpendicular to each root.

This root system can be encoded in a **Dynkin diagram** — a graph with one node per simple root and edges indicating the angles between them. The complete list of connected Dynkin diagrams gives the complete classification of simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$: four infinite families $A_n$ ($\mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}$), $B_n$ ($\mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}$), $C_n$ ($\mathfrak{sp}_{2n}$), $D_n$ ($\mathfrak{so}_{2n}$), and five exceptional cases $G_2, F_4, E_6, E_7, E_8$.

## Lie theory and representation theory

Every representation of a compact Lie group $G$ on a complex vector space $V$ restricts to a representation of its Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. For simply connected groups, the correspondence is exact: representations of $G$ and representations of $\mathfrak{g}$ are in bijection. This reduces the study of continuous group representations to purely algebraic computations with bracket relations and root systems.

The **Peter–Weyl theorem** for compact Lie groups states that $L^2(G)$ decomposes as a direct sum of finite-dimensional irreducible representations, each appearing with multiplicity equal to its dimension. The irreducible representations are classified by **highest weights** — dominant integral elements of $\mathfrak{h}^*$ — and their characters are given by the Weyl character formula.

## Real-world applications

Lie theory is the mathematical language of [quantum mechanics and quantum computing](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms): the symmetry groups $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ (spin) and $\mathrm{SU}(3) \times \mathrm{SU}(2) \times \mathrm{U}(1)$ (the gauge group of the standard model) classify all elementary particles via their irreducible representations. In [computer graphics](/applications/computer-graphics-3d), rotations in 3D are elements of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$, but computations use the double cover $\mathrm{SU}(2)$ via quaternions to avoid gimbal lock. The gyroscopes in [gravitational wave detectors](/applications/gravitational-wave-detection) and spacecraft attitude control systems navigate the Lie group $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ continuously.

## Why this matters

Lie theory sits at the confluence of algebra, geometry, analysis, and physics. Its most striking achievement is the complete classification of all simple Lie algebras — a finite list that governs the symmetries of quantum field theories, crystallographic structures, and exceptional mathematical objects such as the octonions and the 248-dimensional group $E_8$. Noether's theorem, one of the deepest results in mathematical physics, states that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity — and the statement is a theorem about the representation theory of the symmetry's Lie group acting on the phase space.
