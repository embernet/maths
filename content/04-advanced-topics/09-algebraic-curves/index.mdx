An algebraic curve is the set of solutions to a polynomial equation $f(x, y) = 0$. The circle, the conic sections, and the cubic $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ are all plane algebraic curves. This page develops the tools — smooth and singular points, projective closure, genus, and the Riemann–Roch theorem — that transform these elementary objects into one of the richest areas of mathematics, with direct applications to cryptography and number theory.

## Plane algebraic curves and smooth points

Fix an algebraically closed field $k$ (take $k = \mathbb{C}$ for intuition). An **affine plane curve** is the zero set $C = \{(x, y) \in k^2 : f(x, y) = 0\}$ for a polynomial $f \in k[x, y]$.

A point $p \in C$ is **smooth** if $\nabla f(p) = (\partial f/\partial x, \partial f/\partial y)|_p \neq (0, 0)$. At a smooth point, the implicit function theorem gives $C$ locally the structure of a smooth curve (a 1-dimensional manifold over $k$). A **singular point** is one where $\nabla f(p) = (0, 0)$; the curve fails to have a well-defined tangent line there.

Singularities come in recognisable types. A **node** is a singularity where two smooth branches cross transversally — the simplest example is the origin on $y^2 = x^2(x+1)$. A **cusp** is a more degenerate singularity where the branches are tangent, like the origin on $y^2 = x^3$. The local structure is captured by the **tangent cone**: the lowest-degree terms of $f$ at $p$, which determine the directions from which the curve approaches $p$.

## Projective closure and Bézout's theorem

Affine curves omit behaviour "at infinity". The **projective closure** $\bar{C} \subset \mathbb{P}^2_k$ is obtained by homogenising $f$: if $f(x, y) = \sum a_{ij} x^i y^j$, set $F(X, Y, Z) = Z^{\deg f} f(X/Z, Y/Z)$ and define $\bar{C} = \{[X:Y:Z] : F(X, Y, Z) = 0\}$. The affine curve $C$ is recovered by setting $Z = 1$.

**Bézout's theorem**: two projective plane curves $C$ and $D$ of degrees $m$ and $n$ over an algebraically closed field, with no common component, intersect in exactly $mn$ points counted with multiplicity. This is the fundamental counting theorem of plane algebraic geometry. Examples: two distinct lines ($m = n = 1$) meet in exactly 1 point; a line and a conic ($m = 1, n = 2$) meet in 2 points (possibly at infinity or complex, but always 2 counted with multiplicity).

Bézout's theorem explains why the cubic $y^2 = x^3 + ax + b$ (genus 1, degree 3) has the property that any line meets it in exactly 3 points — the group law on an elliptic curve exploits precisely this.

## Smooth projective curves and genus

For a **smooth projective curve** $C$ over $\mathbb{C}$, the Riemann surface structure gives it a topological type: it is homeomorphic to a compact orientable surface of some **genus** $g \ge 0$ (a sphere with $g$ handles). The genus is the fundamental topological invariant.

For a smooth plane curve of degree $d$, the genus is given by the degree-genus formula:

$$g = \frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}.$$

So degree-1 curves (lines) have genus 0; conics (degree 2) have genus 0; cubics (degree 3) have genus 1; quartics (degree 4) have genus 3. Singular curves have lower genus than the formula predicts — each node reduces $g$ by 1.

The genus also measures the space of holomorphic differentials on $C$: $\dim H^0(C, \Omega_C) = g$.

## Divisors and the Riemann–Roch theorem

A **divisor** on a smooth projective curve $C$ is a formal finite sum of points $D = \sum_{p \in C} n_p \cdot p$ with $n_p \in \mathbb{Z}$. The **degree** of $D$ is $\deg D = \sum n_p$. A function $f \in k(C)^*$ determines a divisor $\mathrm{div}(f) = \sum_p v_p(f) \cdot p$ where $v_p(f)$ is the order of vanishing (or pole) at $p$; principal divisors have degree 0.

The **Picard group** $\mathrm{Pic}(C) = \{\text{divisors}\}/\{\text{principal divisors}\}$ classifies line bundles on $C$. The degree-0 part $\mathrm{Pic}^0(C)$ is the **Jacobian** of $C$ — itself a complex torus of dimension $g$ (an abelian variety).

The **Riemann–Roch theorem** is the central result. For a divisor $D$ on a smooth projective curve of genus $g$, let $\ell(D) = \dim H^0(C, \mathcal{O}(D))$ be the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions with poles bounded by $D$, and let $K$ be the canonical divisor (divisor of a holomorphic 1-form). Then:

$$\ell(D) - \ell(K - D) = \deg(D) + 1 - g.$$

This theorem controls which functions exist on a curve. For $\deg D \ge 2g - 1$, the term $\ell(K - D) = 0$ and the formula gives $\ell(D) = \deg(D) + 1 - g$ directly. Consequence: every smooth curve of genus 0 embeds in $\mathbb{P}^1$; every genus-1 curve (elliptic curve) can be written as a plane cubic.

## Elliptic curves: the genus-1 case

An **elliptic curve** over a field $k$ is a smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a distinguished base point $O$. By Riemann–Roch, it can always be brought into **Weierstrass form**:

$$y^2 = x^3 + ax + b,$$

where $4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0$ (the smoothness condition). The key feature is the **group law**: three collinear points on the curve always sum to zero with respect to $O$. This makes the set of points $E(k)$ into an abelian group, classified by the Mordell–Weil theorem over $\mathbb{Q}$.

Elliptic curves over finite fields $\mathbb{F}_p$ are the objects in modern elliptic-curve cryptography. Their group structure is computationally hard to invert (the discrete logarithm problem), making them ideal for public-key cryptography with smaller keys than RSA.

## Real-world applications

The group law on elliptic curves is the foundation of [elliptic-curve cryptography](/applications/cryptocurrency-blockchain), which secures Bitcoin, Ethereum, and most modern TLS connections. [Digital signatures](/applications/digital-signatures) using ECDSA and EdDSA are computed in the group $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ using the Weierstrass model. The security of [RSA and related systems](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) is analysed using the arithmetic of algebraic curves over number fields, and the Wiles–Taylor proof of Fermat's Last Theorem is a theorem about elliptic curves — specifically, that semi-stable elliptic curves over $\mathbb{Q}$ are modular.

## Why this matters

Algebraic curves are the meeting point of algebraic geometry, complex analysis, number theory, and arithmetic. The Riemann–Roch theorem is one of the great results in all of mathematics — it controls spaces of functions via a formula that depends only on the topology (genus) and combinatorics (degree) of the curve. The theory of elliptic curves is the subject of several millennium problems: the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture predicts the rank of $E(\mathbb{Q})$ from the behaviour of the $L$-function $L(E, s)$ at $s = 1$, connecting arithmetic directly to analysis. Modern cryptographic security depends on the difficulty of discrete logarithm problems in $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$ — problems that have no known efficient classical algorithm.
