Algebraic topology answers topological questions by translating them into algebra. The idea is to assign invariants — groups, rings, vector spaces — to topological spaces in such a way that homeomorphic spaces get the same invariant. A topological question then becomes an algebraic one, and algebra is far better equipped to produce decisive answers.

## The fundamental group

Fix a topological space $X$ and a basepoint $x_0 \in X$. A **loop** based at $x_0$ is a continuous map $\gamma : [0,1] \to X$ with $\gamma(0) = \gamma(1) = x_0$. Two loops are **homotopic** if one can be continuously deformed into the other through a family of loops all based at $x_0$.

The set of homotopy classes of loops, with composition given by concatenation and identity given by the constant loop, forms the **fundamental group** $\pi_1(X, x_0)$. It measures how many essentially different loops $X$ contains.

The canonical example: $\pi_1(S^1) \cong \mathbb{Z}$. A loop on the circle is classified entirely by its winding number — an integer that counts how many times it winds around, with sign. The plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ has $\pi_1 = 0$ (every loop contracts to a point); the torus has $\pi_1 \cong \mathbb{Z}^2$ (two independent winding directions). Simply connected spaces — those with $\pi_1 = 0$ — include spheres $S^n$ for $n \ge 2$, and this is precisely what the Poincaré conjecture asserts characterises $S^3$ among closed 3-manifolds.

## Covering spaces and deck transformations

A **covering space** of $X$ is a space $\tilde{X}$ together with a continuous surjection $p : \tilde{X} \to X$ that is locally a homeomorphism. The universal cover $\tilde{X}$ of a path-connected, locally path-connected space is the covering space in which every loop lifts to a path — it has trivial fundamental group.

The **deck transformation group** of the universal cover is the group of homeomorphisms $\tilde{X} \to \tilde{X}$ that commute with $p$. A theorem asserts it is isomorphic to $\pi_1(X, x_0)$, giving a concrete way to recover the fundamental group from covering space symmetry. This is the precise analogue of the Galois correspondence: subgroups of $\pi_1$ correspond to intermediate covering spaces, mirroring subgroups of a Galois group corresponding to intermediate field extensions.

Higher homotopy groups $\pi_n(X, x_0)$ are defined analogously using maps from the $n$-sphere $S^n$ into $X$. They are always abelian for $n \ge 2$, but notoriously hard to compute — even for spheres, $\pi_n(S^m)$ contains surprises.

## Singular homology and chain complexes

Homology provides a family of abelian group invariants $H_n(X)$, one in each dimension, that are easier to compute than homotopy groups. The construction begins with **singular $n$-simplices**: continuous maps from the standard $n$-simplex $\Delta^n$ into $X$. The free abelian group generated by all singular $n$-simplices is the chain group $C_n(X)$.

The **boundary map** $\partial_n : C_n(X) \to C_{n-1}(X)$ takes an $n$-simplex to the alternating sum of its $(n-1)$-dimensional faces. The key identity: $\partial_{n-1} \circ \partial_n = 0$ — the boundary of a boundary is empty. This defines a **chain complex**

$$\cdots \to C_n(X) \xrightarrow{\partial_n} C_{n-1}(X) \to \cdots \to C_0(X) \to 0.$$

The $n$-th homology group is $H_n(X) = \ker \partial_n / \operatorname{im} \partial_{n+1}$: cycles (things with no boundary) modulo boundaries (things that are themselves boundary of something). Intuitively, $H_1$ counts loops that cannot be filled in; $H_2$ counts enclosed voids; and so on.

## Mayer–Vietoris and computation

The **Mayer–Vietoris sequence** is the main computational tool. If $X = A \cup B$ with $A$, $B$ and $A \cap B$ all open and path-connected, there is a long exact sequence

$$\cdots \to H_n(A \cap B) \to H_n(A) \oplus H_n(B) \to H_n(X) \to H_{n-1}(A \cap B) \to \cdots$$

This reduces the homology of a complicated space to those of simpler pieces, much as the Seifert–van Kampen theorem does for the fundamental group. Together they reduce many computations to standard building blocks: $H_n(S^k) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ for $n = 0$ and $n = k$, and $0$ otherwise.

**Cohomology** is the dual theory: define $C^n(X) = \mathrm{Hom}(C_n(X), \mathbb{Z})$ and take the cohomology $H^n(X)$. For smooth manifolds, de Rham's theorem identifies $H^n_{\mathrm{dR}}(M; \mathbb{R})$ — cohomology computed from differential forms — with singular cohomology tensored with $\mathbb{R}$. This bridge between analysis and topology is one of the most fruitful in all of mathematics.

## Brouwer's fixed-point theorem and the hairy-ball theorem

Two celebrated applications. **Brouwer's fixed-point theorem**: every continuous map $f : D^n \to D^n$ from the closed $n$-disc to itself has at least one fixed point. The proof uses the fact that $H_{n-1}(S^{n-1}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ is non-trivial — if there were no fixed point, one could retract $D^n$ onto its boundary, which is impossible by homology.

The **hairy-ball theorem**: there is no nowhere-vanishing continuous tangent vector field on $S^2$. Equivalently, you cannot comb a ball of hair flat. The proof relies on the Euler characteristic $\chi(S^2) = 2 \neq 0$; the Poincaré–Hopf theorem states that the sum of indices of the zeros of any vector field on a compact manifold equals the Euler characteristic.

## Real-world applications

Algebraic topology underpins [computer graphics and 3D modelling](/applications/computer-graphics-3d): the Euler characteristic and genus of a mesh determine its topological type and influence remeshing and hole-filling algorithms. Topological data analysis (TDA) uses persistent homology — tracking which homology classes appear and disappear as a scale parameter varies — to detect structure in high-dimensional data, with applications ranging from [GPS and spatial analysis](/applications/gps-positioning) to protein folding and materials science.

## Why this matters

Algebraic topology is the art of asking topological questions algebraically and algebraic questions topologically. The fundamental group, homology, and cohomology are not just tools for distinguishing spaces — they appear in differential geometry (de Rham cohomology), algebraic geometry (étale cohomology, the Weil conjectures), number theory (Galois cohomology), and physics (topological quantum field theory, the classification of phases of matter). The Poincaré conjecture — proved by Perelman in 2003 using Ricci flow — is simultaneously a statement about $\pi_1$ and about the global geometry of 3-manifolds. The tools built here sit at the foundation of almost every area of modern pure mathematics.
