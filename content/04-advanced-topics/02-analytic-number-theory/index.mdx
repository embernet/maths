Why are there infinitely many primes? Euclid answered that. But how are they distributed? How many primes are there less than $x$? Why does the pattern of primes look almost random? These questions are the domain of **analytic number theory** — which, remarkably, uses complex analysis to prove results about integers.

## The Riemann zeta function

The **Riemann zeta function** is defined for $\text{Re}(s) > 1$ by:

$$\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_p \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}},$$

where the product runs over all primes $p$. The **Euler product** (the second expression) — proved by [Euler](/mathematicians/euler) in 1737 — is the analytic version of the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic: it encodes the unique prime factorisation of integers.

[Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann) showed in 1859 that $\zeta(s)$ extends to a meromorphic function on all of $\mathbb{C}$ with a single simple pole at $s = 1$, and satisfies a **functional equation** relating $\zeta(s)$ to $\zeta(1-s)$.

The **zeros** of $\zeta(s)$ control the error term in the prime counting function. The trivial zeros are at $s = -2, -4, -6, \ldots$. All known non-trivial zeros lie on the **critical line** $\text{Re}(s) = 1/2$.

## The Riemann hypothesis

Riemann conjectured that all non-trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$ lie on the critical line $\text{Re}(s) = 1/2$. This remains unproved after over 150 years; it is one of the Clay Millennium Problems, with a $1,000,000 prize for a proof.

Why does it matter? The **explicit formula** for the prime-counting function $\pi(x) = \#\{p \le x : p \text{ prime}\}$ expresses $\pi(x)$ as a sum involving the zeros of $\zeta$. If the Riemann hypothesis is true, the error in the approximation $\pi(x) \approx \text{Li}(x) = \int_2^x 1/\log t\, dt$ is at most $O(\sqrt{x} \log x)$ — the best possible.

Numerically, over $10^{13}$ zeros have been verified to lie on the critical line. This is compelling evidence but not a proof.

## The prime number theorem

The **prime number theorem** (proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin in 1896):

$$\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x} \quad \text{as } x \to \infty.$$

Equivalently, the $n$th prime is approximately $n \log n$. The proof uses the Hadamard-Cartwright theorem on zero-free regions of $\zeta(s)$ near $\text{Re}(s) = 1$, together with a Tauberian theorem connecting $\zeta(s)$ to $\pi(x)$ via the Mellin transform. [Hardy](/mathematicians/hardy), one of the leading analytic number theorists of the early twentieth century, called the prime number theorem "one of the most remarkable achievements of pure mathematics."

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [RSA public-key cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) depends on the computational hardness of factoring large integers — a difficulty that is intimately tied to the distribution of primes studied in this course; the prime number theorem and its error estimates tell us how many candidate primes of a given size exist, which determines how fast key generation runs. [Cryptocurrency and blockchain protocols](/applications/cryptocurrency-blockchain) use discrete logarithm problems in groups whose order is a large prime; the analytic number theory behind Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions ensures that suitable prime moduli are plentiful. More broadly, the same Euler product structure that appears in the zeta function underlies the generating functions used in modern primality testing.

## Dirichlet characters and L-functions

Dirichlet generalised Euler's argument to prove there are infinitely many primes in any arithmetic progression $\{a, a+d, a+2d, \ldots\}$ with $\gcd(a, d) = 1$. The key tool is **Dirichlet L-functions**:

$$L(s, \chi) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s} = \prod_p \frac{1}{1 - \chi(p)p^{-s}},$$

where $\chi$ is a **Dirichlet character** (a multiplicative function periodic modulo $d$). Dirichlet's theorem reduces to showing $L(1, \chi) \ne 0$ for non-principal characters — a beautiful analytic argument.

The generalised Riemann hypothesis conjectures that all non-trivial zeros of all Dirichlet $L$-functions lie on the critical line. This would have far-reaching consequences for the distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions.

The Langlands programme — one of the most ambitious projects in modern mathematics — seeks to generalise this framework to connect L-functions (arising from number theory) to representations of algebraic groups, with potential applications to every corner of pure mathematics.
