Take an atlas. Measure the coastline of Britain with a ruler set to 100 kilometres. You get about 2,800 km. Now reset the ruler to 50 km and measure again; now you can trace in peninsulas and bays you had previously smoothed over, and you get about 3,400 km. Reset to 10 km and you get 5,000 km. Reset to 1 km and you get over 17,000 km. The coastline is growing without bound as your measuring scale shrinks.

This observation, which Benoît Mandelbrot published in 1967 under the title 'How Long Is the Coast of Britain?', is not a failure of measurement. It is a fundamental property of the geometric object. Coastlines are *fractal*: they have structure at every scale, and the conventional concept of length does not apply to them.

## Fractal dimension

For a smooth curve, doubling the resolution of your measurement roughly doubles the number of segments you need to cover it, and the total measured length stays approximately constant. For a fractal, doubling the resolution increases the count of segments by a factor greater than 2, and the measured length keeps growing.

Specifically, if covering the object with segments of length $\epsilon$ requires $N(\epsilon)$ segments, then for a fractal

$$N(\epsilon) \sim \epsilon^{-D}$$

where $D$ is the *fractal dimension* (also called the Hausdorff or box-counting dimension). For a smooth curve, $D = 1$. For a flat region, $D = 2$. For a fractal, $D$ is typically not an integer.

The coastline of Britain has $D \approx 1.25$. Norway's more jagged coastline has $D \approx 1.52$. South Africa's smoother coastline has $D \approx 1.02$. These are not vague estimates; they are measurable from maps.

You can compute the box-counting dimension directly: cover the coastline with a grid of boxes of side $\epsilon$, count how many boxes contain part of the coastline, and plot $\log N(\epsilon)$ against $\log \epsilon$. The slope of the resulting line is $-D$. This is a genuine measurement of a genuine property.

## Mathematical fractals

The natural setting for understanding fractal dimension is the study of self-similar sets — sets that look the same at every scale.

The **Cantor set** is the simplest example. Start with $[0,1]$. Remove the middle third. Remove the middle third of each remaining interval. Repeat infinitely. What you are left with is uncountable, has zero length, and has fractal dimension $D = \log 2 / \log 3 \approx 0.631$. It is the foundational example in fractal geometry, and it was constructed by [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) in the 1880s as a counterexample in analysis — a set that was everywhere totally disconnected but still uncountable.

The **Koch snowflake** starts from an equilateral triangle, replaces the middle third of each edge by two sides of a smaller equilateral triangle, and repeats. The resulting curve has infinite length and fractal dimension $D = \log 4 / \log 3 \approx 1.26$ — remarkably close to the coastline of Britain.

The [Weierstrass function](/theorems/weierstrass-function-continuous-everywhere-differentiable-nowhere) — continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere — is a fractal curve in the time domain. When [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) presented it in 1872, it was considered pathological, a monster constructed to refute an intuition. From the fractal geometry perspective, it is simply a curve whose dimension is greater than 1.

## Fractals in the living world

**Lungs.** The human lung has a surface area of about 70 square metres, contained in a volume of about 4 litres. This extraordinary ratio is achieved by fractal branching: the bronchial tree divides roughly 23 times from trachea to alveolus, with each division reducing the diameter by a constant factor. The total number of alveoli is about 480 million. The fractal dimension of the bronchial tree surface is approximately 2.97 — close to the theoretical maximum of 3 for a surface filling a volume. Evolution has used fractal architecture to pack a gymnasium-sized surface into a chest cavity.

**River networks.** River basins show fractal structure in their branching, obeying a relation known as Hack's law: the length $L$ of the main stream in a basin of area $A$ scales as $L \propto A^{0.6}$. The fractal dimension of river networks, measured by box-counting, is typically around 1.8 to 2.0. The geometry of drainage networks is now understood to arise from the process of erosion optimising flow gradients — another case where an optimisation problem produces a fractal structure.

**Lightning.** Electrical discharge in air propagates along the path of least resistance, which at a microscopic level is determined by the random distribution of ionised molecules. The resulting discharge path is a fractal with dimension approximately 1.7 to 1.8. Dielectric breakdown — the same process that creates Lichtenberg figures when a high-voltage discharge propagates through a solid insulator — produces similar fractal structures that can be preserved in perspex blocks and are sold as ornaments.

**Romanesco broccoli.** This is perhaps the most visually dramatic example. Each head of Romanesco is composed of spiralling florets, each of which is itself a miniature version of the whole head, which is itself a miniature version of the larger structure. The self-similarity is visible to the naked eye across three or four scales. The fractal dimension is approximately 2.66.

## Power laws

Fractal dimension is intimately related to power laws. If a quantity $Y$ scales with a size $X$ as $Y \propto X^{\alpha}$, and if this relation holds across multiple orders of magnitude, then the underlying structure is likely fractal or self-similar. Many biological and physical quantities obey power laws of this type: the sizes of cities, the energies of earthquakes, the frequencies of words in a text, the surface areas of organs. The Gaussian distribution (the bell curve; see the [normal distribution](/theorems/gaussian-distribution-normal-distribution)) does not describe these phenomena well; power-law distributions do, and fractal geometry provides the mathematical framework for understanding them.

## What is true and what is overstated

Not every irregular shape is a fractal in the technical sense. For a fractal dimension to be meaningful, the power-law scaling must hold over at least two or three orders of magnitude. Many natural objects are self-similar over a limited range of scales and then become smooth or random at other scales. Biologists who claim "the lung is a fractal" are using "fractal" as a shorthand for "has more structure at smaller scales than classical geometry would predict" — which is true and useful, but is not the same as having a well-defined fractal dimension over all scales.

Also: fractal dimension is not a uniquely determined property. Box-counting dimension, Hausdorff dimension, correlation dimension, and information dimension can give different values for the same object. For a genuine mathematical fractal (Cantor set, Koch snowflake), they all agree. For a noisy natural object, they differ, and the choice of which to use depends on what question you are asking.
