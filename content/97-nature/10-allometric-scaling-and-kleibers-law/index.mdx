If you scale up an animal, how do its physiological quantities change? The naive expectation is straightforward: double the mass, double everything. A 20-gram mouse needs $X$ calories per day; a 40-gram mouse needs $2X$. In practice, this is wrong for almost every physiological quantity, and understanding *why* it is wrong leads into one of the most remarkable discoveries in twentieth-century biology.

## Kleiber's law

In 1932, Max Kleiber of the University of California Davis published a paper analysing the metabolic rates of animals ranging from rats to horses. He found that basal metabolic rate $B$ scales with body mass $M$ as

$$B \propto M^{3/4}.$$

This is Kleiber's law, and the exponent $3/4$ has since been confirmed across an extraordinary range of organisms: bacteria, protozoa, insects, fish, reptiles, birds, and mammals, spanning 27 orders of magnitude in body mass.

Think about what this means. A mouse (20 g) has a mass about $2 \times 10^5$ times smaller than an elephant (4,000 kg). If metabolic rate scaled linearly, the elephant would need $2 \times 10^5$ times as many calories. But Kleiber's law predicts a ratio of only $(2 \times 10^5)^{3/4} \approx 10^{3.9} \approx 8 \times 10^3$ — about 40 times less than the linear expectation. In fact, the elephant's metabolic rate per unit mass is much lower than a mouse's: large animals run more efficiently, calorie for calorie, than small ones.

The exponent $3/4$ is also not the only allometric scaling exponent. Heart rate scales as $M^{-1/4}$; lifespan scales as $M^{1/4}$; the time for a heartbeat scales as $M^{1/4}$. All of these exponents are multiples of $1/4$. This is not a coincidence.

## The West–Brown–Enquist model

Why $3/4$, rather than $2/3$ (which would follow from a simple surface-area argument) or $1$ (linear scaling)?

In 1997, Geoffrey West, James Brown, and Brian Enquist published a derivation in *Science* that answered this question. Their argument starts from three assumptions:

1. The organism's metabolic resources are delivered by a branching network (circulatory system, bronchial tree, vascular system in plants).
2. The network is self-similar (fractal), with the same branching geometry repeating at each level.
3. The network has evolved to minimise the energy cost of delivery while filling the volume of the organism.

From these three assumptions — and the mathematical structure of optimal fractal networks, which is a direct descendant of Murray's law (see the [tree branching page](/97-nature/06-tree-branching-and-leonardos-rule)) — they derive that metabolic rate must scale as $M^{3/4}$. The factor of $1/4$ in all the allometric exponents follows from the fact that a self-similar network in a three-dimensional space introduces one extra dimension of parameter freedom compared to the bulk material it supplies.

More precisely: a fractal network filling a 3-dimensional volume has an effective dimension of 4 for the purposes of transport scaling. The metabolic rate — which is determined by transport to the terminal units (cells) of the network — scales with the number of terminal units, which scales with $M^{1} = M$. But the rate per unit is constrained by the network geometry, which introduces a $M^{-1/4}$ correction, giving $B \propto M^{3/4}$.

## A specific calculation

The terminal units of the circulatory system are capillaries. Capillaries are essentially invariant across mammals: they have approximately the same diameter (about $5\,\mu\text{m}$) and the same length (about $1\,\text{mm}$) in a mouse and an elephant. The number of capillaries scales with the total number of cells, which scales approximately with $M$.

The total volume of blood in the circulatory system scales with the number of capillaries, hence with $M$. The heart-rate scales as $M^{-1/4}$ (as observed). The heart pumps one stroke volume per beat; the stroke volume scales as $M^{5/4}/M^{1} = M^{1/4}$ (roughly); so cardiac output (volume per unit time) scales as $M^{1/4} \times M^{-1/4} \times M^{1/4} \times \ldots$ — the precise argument requires following the network geometry through the hierarchy, which West et al. do carefully.

## What the law says about lifespan

One of the most striking corollaries: since heart rate scales as $M^{-1/4}$ and metabolic rate as $M^{3/4}$, the number of heartbeats per unit of energy consumed is approximately constant across mammals. Put differently, a shrew and an elephant each get roughly the same number of heartbeats in a lifetime — about $1.5 \times 10^9$. The shrew lives for about 2 years and has a heart rate of 1,000 beats per minute; the elephant lives for about 70 years and has a heart rate of about 30 beats per minute.

Humans are a notable exception: we get about three times as many heartbeats as the law predicts for our mass, probably because of reduced predation pressure and evolved longevity.

## Plants

The same $3/4$ scaling applies to plants. Tree basal metabolic rate, leaf nitrogen content, and phloem transport rate all scale as $M^{3/4}$ or related quarter-power laws. The West–Brown–Enquist model was originally derived from animal circulatory networks but extends to plant vascular systems with the same predictions, because the same mathematical structure — space-filling fractal branching with invariant terminal units — applies.

The confirmation across both animals and plants, organisms with completely different evolutionary histories and body plans, is powerful evidence that the $3/4$ exponent reflects a deep geometric constraint rather than a specific biological mechanism.

## Controversies and caveats

Kleiber's law has been contested. The main dispute is over the exact value of the exponent: some meta-analyses favour $2/3$ rather than $3/4$, particularly within taxonomic groups (mammals only, or birds only). The $3/4$ exponent appears more clearly when the full range of 27 orders of magnitude is used; within narrower ranges, both $2/3$ and $3/4$ fit the data roughly equally well.

The West–Brown–Enquist model has also been criticised on the grounds that its specific assumptions (fully space-filling network, invariant terminal units) are not precisely satisfied in real organisms. The model's defenders argue that the $3/4$ exponent is robust to these deviations, and that the key structural insight — fractal network geometry introduces a quarter-power scaling — is correct even if the precise derivation is not.

This debate is genuinely unresolved. What is not disputed is that allometric scaling with approximately quarter-power exponents is real and widespread, and that fractal network geometry is the most compelling explanation on offer.
