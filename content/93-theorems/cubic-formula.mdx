        For the depressed cubic $x^3 + px + q = 0$ the real solutions are given by

$$
x = \sqrt[3]{-\tfrac{q}{2} + \sqrt{\tfrac{q^2}{4} + \tfrac{p^3}{27}}} + \sqrt[3]{-\tfrac{q}{2} - \sqrt{\tfrac{q^2}{4} + \tfrac{p^3}{27}}}.
$$

When the discriminant $\tfrac{q^2}{4} + \tfrac{p^3}{27}$ is negative, the formula passes through complex numbers even when all three solutions are real — the *casus irreducibilis*.

## History

The story is one of the most colourful in mathematics. Scipione del Ferro found a solution around 1500, kept it secret for use in mathematical duels. Niccolò Tartaglia rediscovered it independently. [Cardano](/mathematicians/cardano) extracted the method from Tartaglia under a vow of secrecy, then published it anyway in *Ars Magna* (1545) — sparking a feud Tartaglia never recovered from.

## Why it matters

It was the first solution of an equation harder than the quadratic. The need to manipulate $\sqrt{-1}$ to recover real solutions forced mathematicians to take complex numbers seriously, two centuries before they had a proper meaning.

## Where you'll meet it

As a historical case study in any algebra course; the methods recur in Galois theory.
