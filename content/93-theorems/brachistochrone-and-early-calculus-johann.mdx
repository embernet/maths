## What it says

The brachistochrone problem: given two points $A$ and $B$ at different heights, find the curve along which a bead sliding under gravity (without friction) travels from $A$ to $B$ in the shortest time. The answer is a **cycloid** — the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling circle.

The travel time is the functional $T[y] = \int_0^{x_1} \sqrt{\frac{1 + (y')^2}{2gy}}\,dx$, and the problem is to minimise $T[y]$ over all curves $y(x)$ with the appropriate boundary conditions. This is a problem from the **calculus of variations** — optimising a functional, not a function.

## History

Johann [Bernoulli](/mathematicians/bernoulli-family) posed the problem in 1696 as a challenge to mathematicians. Correct solutions were received from Newton, Leibniz, l'Hôpital, and Jacob Bernoulli. Johann's own solution used a beautiful analogy with Snell's law of optics. The problem marked the beginning of the calculus of variations — a major new branch of mathematics.

## Why it matters

The brachistochrone problem opened the calculus of variations: instead of minimising a function of a real variable, you minimise a functional — a function whose argument is itself a function. This framework underlies Lagrangian mechanics, optimal control theory, and the path-integral formulation of quantum mechanics.

## Real-world applications

[Robotics](/applications/robotics-and-control) uses optimal control (a generalisation of the calculus of variations) for motion planning. [Aerodynamics](/applications/airfoil-and-aerodynamics) uses variational methods to optimise wing shapes.
