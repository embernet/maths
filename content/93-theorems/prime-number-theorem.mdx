Primes look chaotic up close. Zoom out, and a pattern appears. Write $\pi(N)$ for the number of primes less than or equal to $N$. The **prime number theorem** says:

$$
\pi(N) \sim \frac{N}{\ln N} \qquad \text{as } N \to \infty.
$$

The squiggle $\sim$ means the ratio tends to 1: as $N$ grows, $\pi(N)$ and $N/\ln N$ become arbitrarily close in relative terms. Primes thin out, but they do so smoothly — about one in every $\ln N$ numbers near $N$ is prime.

## History

A fifteen-year-old [Carl Friedrich Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) tabulated primes for fun and noticed, around 1792, that they appeared to thin out logarithmically. He never published the conjecture but recorded it in his notebooks. Adrien-Marie Legendre stated something very close to it independently in 1797. The hard work — actually proving it — took another century. [Bernhard Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann)'s extraordinary 1859 paper transformed the question into one about a complex-analytic object, the zeta function, and revealed that the precise distribution of primes is governed by the locations of the [zeros of $\zeta(s)$](/theorems/riemann-zeta-function). In 1896, Jacques Hadamard and Charles-Jean de la Vallée Poussin closed the loop, independently proving the theorem using complex analysis. An "elementary" proof — one avoiding complex analysis — eluded mathematicians until Atle Selberg and Paul Erdős found one in 1948 (a collaboration that ended in a famous feud over credit).

## Why it matters

The PNT is the great success of analytic number theory. It connects two worlds — the discrete primes and the continuous logarithm — and shows that even the most stubborn-looking integer sequences have analytic shape. Sharper estimates of the error term $\pi(N) - N/\ln N$ remain an active area: the [Riemann hypothesis](/theorems/riemann-hypothesis), still unproven, would give the sharpest possible bound.

## Real-world applications

Modern cryptography depends on the fact that primes are dense enough to find, but factoring is hard. The PNT tells you roughly how many random tries it takes to find a prime of any given size — directly informing key generation in [RSA and TLS](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) and the elliptic-curve schemes underpinning [cryptocurrency](/applications/cryptocurrency-blockchain).
