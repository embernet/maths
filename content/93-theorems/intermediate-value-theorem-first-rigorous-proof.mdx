## What it says

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$ (or vice versa), then there exists $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = 0$.

More generally: if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $k$ is any value between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, then $f$ takes the value $k$ somewhere on $(a, b)$.

In plain language: you cannot get from one height to another without passing through every height in between, provided you travel continuously. This seems obvious — but proving it requires understanding what "continuous" and "real number" actually mean.

## History

[Bolzano](/mathematicians/bolzano) gave the first rigorous proof in his 1817 paper *Rein analytischer Beweis* ("Pure analytical proof"). He was dismayed that the theorem — which his contemporaries considered self-evident — had no proper proof, and that proofs of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra relied on geometric intuition rather than analysis. His paper was a direct challenge to that sloppiness. Cauchy gave a cleaner proof in 1821, and it is often attributed to him, but Bolzano's 1817 paper was first.

## Why it matters

The IVT is one of the deepest properties of the real number line. It fails for the rationals: $f(x) = x^2 - 2$ is continuous on $[1, 2]$ with $f(1) < 0 < f(2)$, but the zero at $\sqrt{2}$ is not in $\mathbb{Q}$. So the IVT is, in a sense, what distinguishes $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$: the reals are complete, meaning there are no "gaps" for the function to jump over.

The IVT is also the simplest existence theorem in analysis — it tells you a root exists without constructing it. Bisection search then finds it numerically.

## Real-world applications

Bisection root-finding, which relies on the IVT, underpins many numerical solvers. [Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) models use iterative root-finding to solve implicit equations at each grid cell and time step.

## Where you'll meet it

The IVT appears in Year 1 calculus courses and is revisited carefully in Year 2 real analysis, where its proof reveals what completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ really means. See also [Dedekind cuts](/theorems/dedekind-cuts-rigorous-construction-of-real-numbers) for the construction that makes the proof work.
