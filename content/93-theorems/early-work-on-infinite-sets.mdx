## What it says

In *Paradoxien des Unendlichen* (Paradoxes of the Infinite, published 1851), [Bolzano](/mathematicians/bolzano) argued that infinite sets can be put into one-to-one correspondence with proper subsets of themselves — e.g., $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ can be matched with $\{2, 4, 6, \ldots\}$ by $n \mapsto 2n$. He noted this as a property of infinite sets (rather than a contradiction), but did not develop the full theory of cardinality.

He also made the important observation that the set of points on a line segment is "as numerous" as the set of points on the whole plane — a claim that would be made rigorous by Cantor.

## History

Bolzano wrote the text in the 1840s, but it was published only after his death in 1851. It remained largely unread. [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) developed the rigorous theory of cardinality independently in the 1870s–90s, and it was only then that Bolzano's anticipations were recognised.

## Why it matters

Bolzano was ahead of his time: he saw that infinite sets behave paradoxically by the standards of finite sets, and rather than retreating from this, he tried to understand it systematically. That willingness to accept counterintuitive results as mathematical facts — not puzzles to be avoided — is the prerequisite for the rigorous theory that Cantor built.

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but the set-theoretic foundations that this work anticipated are the basis of all of modern mathematics.
