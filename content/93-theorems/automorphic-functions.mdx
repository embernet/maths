## What it says

An **automorphic function** is a meromorphic function $f$ on the upper half-plane $\mathbb{H}$ that satisfies:

$$f\!\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right) = f(z) \quad \text{for all } \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} \in \Gamma$$

where $\Gamma$ is a discrete subgroup of $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ (a Fuchsian group). The function is invariant under a group of Möbius transformations.

**Modular forms** (a special case): functions on $\mathbb{H}$ satisfying $f\!\left(\frac{az+b}{cz+d}\right) = (cz+d)^k f(z)$ for $\Gamma = \text{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$. These are central in modern number theory.

## History

[Poincaré](/mathematicians/poincare) developed automorphic functions in a remarkable burst of creativity in 1880–81, reportedly while boarding an omnibus and having a sudden illumination about the connection between Fuchsian functions (his name) and non-Euclidean geometry. He later recalled: "At the moment when I put my foot on the step, the idea came to me, without anything in my former thoughts seeming to have paved the way for it."

## Why it matters

Automorphic forms and modular forms are central to modern number theory. The proof of Fermat's Last Theorem by Wiles used the modularity of elliptic curves — a deep connection between modular forms and elliptic curves. The Langlands programme seeks to unify all of number theory through a vast generalisation of automorphic forms.

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but modular forms connect to [RSA cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) via elliptic curves.
