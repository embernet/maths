## What it says

Given observations $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ and model predictions $\hat{y}_i(\theta)$ depending on parameters $\theta$, the **least squares** estimate minimises:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat{y}_i(\theta))^2$$

For the linear model $\mathbf{y} = X\boldsymbol{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, the least squares solution is:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top \mathbf{y}$$

**Gauss–Markov theorem:** Under the assumptions of zero-mean errors with equal variance, the least squares estimator is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator (BLUE) — minimum variance among all linear unbiased estimators.

If errors are Gaussian, least squares is equivalent to [maximum likelihood estimation](/theorems/maximum-likelihood-estimation).

## History

[Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) used the method in 1801 to predict the return of the asteroid Ceres from only 40 days of observations, to the astonishment of astronomers. He published the method in *Theoria Motus* (1809). Legendre published the method independently in 1805 and claimed priority; the dispute was unpleasant. The probabilistic justification — that least squares is optimal when errors are Gaussian — is Gauss's contribution.

## Why it matters

Least squares is the oldest and most widely used fitting method in science. Every linear regression, every fit of a curve to data, every calibration of a measurement instrument uses least squares or a direct extension. Understanding why it works — the Gauss–Markov theorem and the connection to MLE — is essential for any quantitative scientist.

## Real-world applications

[GPS positioning](/applications/gps-positioning) uses weighted least squares to solve the overdetermined position system. [Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) fits model parameters to observations by least squares. [Machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) linear regression is OLS. [Survey statistics](/applications/census-and-survey-statistics) uses least squares throughout.
