## What it says

A **formal axiomatic system** consists of:
1. A formal language (symbols, formation rules for well-formed formulae)
2. A set of axioms (specific formulae taken as given)
3. Rules of inference (allowed steps from formulae to formulae)

A **proof** is a finite sequence of formulae where each is either an axiom or follows from earlier formulae by a rule of inference. A system is **consistent** if $\bot$ (a contradiction) is not provable. It is **complete** if every sentence or its negation is provable.

Hilbert's *Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1899) presented Euclidean geometry as a formal system, completing Euclid's programme by replacing intuitive geometric notions with purely formal axioms.

## History

[Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert) developed the programme of formalism: mathematics is the manipulation of symbols according to rules, and the content of those symbols is irrelevant. "Instead of points, straight lines, and planes, one must always be able to say tables, chairs, and beer mugs," he reportedly said. This abstraction was powerful but, as Gödel showed, had inherent limitations.

## Why it matters

Formal axiomatic systems are the foundation of mathematical logic, proof theory, and computer science. Every programming language type system, every automated theorem prover, every formal verification tool is a formal system. The incompleteness theorems showed that no consistent formal system strong enough to express arithmetic can be complete.

## Real-world applications

Formal methods in software engineering use formal axiomatic systems to verify program correctness — critical in avionics, medical devices, and financial systems.
