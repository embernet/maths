## What it says

[Newton](/mathematicians/newton) conceived of quantities as *fluents* — things that flow over time — and their rates of change as *fluxions*. If $x$ is a fluent, its fluxion $\dot{x}$ is the instantaneous rate at which $x$ changes. This is what we now call the derivative $dx/dt$. Newton's notation used overdots: $\dot{x}$, $\ddot{x}$, still standard in physics for time derivatives.

The core idea: for a function $y = f(x)$, the fluxion $\dot{y}/\dot{x}$ gives the ratio of infinitesimal changes — the gradient of the curve at a point. Combining this with the idea of summing infinitesimal increments (fluents as sums of their fluxions) is what Newton recognised as the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.

## History

Newton developed the method of fluxions between roughly 1664 and 1666, the famous "plague years" during which Cambridge was closed and he retreated to Lincolnshire. He used it immediately to derive his law of gravitation and verify it against the Moon's orbit. He did not publish the method for decades, and [Leibniz](/mathematicians/leibniz) independently developed calculus in the 1670s using his own notation. The resulting priority dispute between their supporters consumed much of the early eighteenth century and damaged British mathematics — which clung to Newton's dot notation while the Continent used Leibniz's far more flexible $dy/dx$.

## Why it matters

Fluxions were the first systematic account of instantaneous rates of change, unlocking the mathematics of motion, planetary orbits, fluid flow, and heat. Every differential equation you will write is a descendant of this idea. Newton's mechanics became the paradigm of scientific explanation for over two centuries.

## Real-world applications

[Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) and [weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) are built on differential equations that trace directly to Newton's fluxional calculus. [Gravitational-wave detection](/applications/gravitational-wave-detection) requires solving coupled differential equations of motion at extraordinary precision.
