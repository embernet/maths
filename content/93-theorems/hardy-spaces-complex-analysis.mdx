## What it says

The **Hardy space** $H^p(\mathbb{D})$ ($1 \leq p < \infty$) consists of holomorphic functions $f$ on the unit disc $\mathbb{D}$ satisfying:

$$\sup_{0 < r < 1} \int_0^{2\pi} |f(re^{i\theta})|^p \frac{d\theta}{2\pi} < \infty$$

Key results: Every $H^p$ function has boundary values (limits as $r \to 1$) almost everywhere. The boundary values lie in $L^p$ of the circle. The Fourier coefficients of $H^p$ functions are one-sided (only $e^{in\theta}$ with $n \geq 0$). The factorisation theorem (Blaschke products, inner and outer functions) classifies $H^p$ functions by their zeros.

## History

[Hardy](/mathematicians/hardy) introduced the spaces in 1915 while studying integral means of holomorphic functions. He was interested in the boundary behaviour of complex functions — a question in pure analysis. The spaces are now fundamental to several areas: operator theory, interpolation, prediction theory for stationary processes.

## Why it matters

Hardy spaces provide the correct framework for many questions in analysis: which sequences of values can be achieved by a holomorphic function on the disc? What are the analytic functions with a given boundary behaviour? In control theory, Hardy spaces appear as the natural function spaces for input-output systems.

## Real-world applications

[Signal processing](/applications/signal-processing-jpeg-mp3) uses Hardy space theory for Wiener filter analysis and spectral factorisation. [Option pricing](/applications/option-pricing-finance) uses Hardy space methods in the Heston model.
