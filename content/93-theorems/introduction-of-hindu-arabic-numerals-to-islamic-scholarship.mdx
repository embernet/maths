## What it says

The Hindu-Arabic numeral system uses ten symbols (0–9) and positional notation: the value of a digit depends on its position (units, tens, hundreds, …). This makes arithmetic operations (addition, multiplication, long division) vastly more efficient than additive systems like Roman numerals.

The system includes **zero** as a number and as a positional placeholder — an innovation that was absent from Greek and Roman mathematics and that makes the whole positional system work.

## History

The positional decimal system was developed in India (Brahmagupta formalised arithmetic with zero in 628 CE) and reached the Islamic world via translations. [Al-Khwarizmi](/mathematicians/al-khwarizmi) wrote his arithmetic treatise around 820 CE systematically introducing the system. His work was translated into Latin in the twelfth century, and [Fibonacci](/mathematicians/fibonacci) popularised the system in Europe through *Liber Abaci* (1202). Roman numerals persisted in some European contexts until the fifteenth century, but the Hindu-Arabic system was simply far superior for calculation.

## Why it matters

Zero and positional notation are not minor conveniences — they are the prerequisites for all systematic arithmetic, algebra, and eventually computing. Without zero as a number, you cannot formulate the concept of a remainder cleanly. Without positional notation, long multiplication requires a counting board or abacus. The adoption of the Hindu-Arabic system in Europe was a genuine intellectual revolution.

## Real-world applications

Every number in every formula in this guide is written in the Hindu-Arabic system. Every digital computer represents numbers in positional notation (base 2).
