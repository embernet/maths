## What it says

In 1654, Pascal and Fermat exchanged letters solving the **problem of points**: two players are playing a fair game; each round one player wins; the first to reach $n$ wins gets the prize. If the game is interrupted, how should the prize be divided fairly?

Solution: calculate, for each player, the probability that they would have won if the game had continued. Divide the prize proportionally.

For example, if Player A needs 2 more wins and Player B needs 3: the game would last at most $2 + 3 - 1 = 4$ more rounds. Player A wins if she gets $\geq 2$ of the remaining rounds. The probability of each outcome is computed using binomial coefficients from [Pascal's triangle](/theorems/pascal-s-triangle).

## History

[Pascal](/mathematicians/pascal) and Fermat solved the problem of points in a correspondence that effectively launched probability theory as a mathematical discipline. Pascal combined the result with work on expected value and combinatorics in his *Traité du triangle arithmétique* (1654). Huygens published the first textbook on probability in 1657.

## Why it matters

Pascal and Fermat invented the framework for computing probabilities and expectations — the foundation of all subsequent probability theory. Their key insight was that a fair division depends on *future probabilities*, not on past outcomes. This was a new way of thinking about fairness, risk, and uncertainty.
