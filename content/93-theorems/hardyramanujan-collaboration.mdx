## What it says

The Hardy–Ramanujan collaboration produced results including: the asymptotic formula for the partition function $p(n)$ (the number of ways to write $n$ as a sum of positive integers), highly composite numbers, and results on the distribution of prime factors.

The **Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic formula** (1918):

$$p(n) \sim \frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}} e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$$

This was the first asymptotic formula for $p(n)$, obtained using the **circle method** — an analytic technique for extracting coefficients of generating functions that became a cornerstone of analytic number theory.

## History

Ramanujan wrote to [Hardy](/mathematicians/hardy) in 1913 from Madras, enclosing a list of remarkable formulas. Hardy and Littlewood studied the letter: some results were well-known, some were wrong, but some were entirely unknown and clearly the work of a mathematician of the first order. Hardy brought Ramanujan to Cambridge in 1914. Their five-year collaboration produced fundamental results despite Ramanujan's lack of formal training in modern analysis.

## Why it matters

The circle method they developed is still a primary tool for problems about integer representations — how many ways can $n$ be written as a sum of $k$ squares, for instance. Ramanujan's modular forms and mock theta functions, understood only decades later, have connections to string theory and the Langlands programme.

## Real-world applications

Partition theory has connections to [quantum computing algorithms](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms) via quantum state counting.
