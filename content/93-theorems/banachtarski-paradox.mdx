## What it says

The Banach–Tarski theorem states that a ball $B \subset \mathbb{R}^3$ can be partitioned into a finite number of (non-measurable) pieces $P_1, \ldots, P_k$ and these pieces can be reassembled — using only rotations and translations — into two balls, each congruent to the original.

The key word is **non-measurable**: the pieces cannot be assigned a sensible volume (they are not Lebesgue measurable). The "paradox" does not violate conservation of mass because no physical object can be decomposed into such pieces — they require the axiom of choice to construct.

## History

Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski proved the result in 1924. It relies on the axiom of choice, which permits the construction of non-measurable sets. The result was shocking — it showed that the axiom of choice had deeply counterintuitive consequences — and contributed to ongoing debates about which axioms should be accepted in mathematics.

## Why it matters

The Banach–Tarski paradox is a striking illustration of the difference between mathematical and physical intuition. It shows that "geometric decomposition" is far more subtle than it seems, and that measure theory (volume, area, probability) requires careful foundational work to avoid contradictions. It also shows that the axiom of choice — used routinely in analysis — implies results with no physical realisation.

## Real-world applications

No direct applications — but the measure theory that Banach–Tarski demarcates is the foundation of [Kolmogorov's probability axioms](/theorems/axiomatic-foundations-of-probability) and all of modern integration theory.

## Where you'll meet it

The Banach–Tarski paradox is discussed in advanced topics set theory and measure theory courses as a motivation for restricting to measurable sets. See the [Modern Mathematics era](/90-history/09-modern-mathematics).
