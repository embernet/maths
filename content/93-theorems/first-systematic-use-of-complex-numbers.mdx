## What it says

[Cardano](/mathematicians/cardano) encountered expressions like $\sqrt{-15}$ while applying his cubic formula. He called them "sophistic" or "fictitious" quantities but showed they could be manipulated algebraically, demonstrating that $(5 + \sqrt{-15})(5 - \sqrt{-15}) = 40$. The negative square root appears as a computational intermediary even when the final answer is a real number.

Over the following two centuries, complex numbers were gradually accepted: Bombelli gave rules for computing with them in 1572; Wallis attempted geometric interpretation in 1685; Wessel (1797) and Argand (1806) introduced the complex plane; Gauss used them extensively and gave complex analysis a rigorous basis; [Euler](/mathematicians/euler) unified them with $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$.

## History

The *casus irreducibilis* — the case where a cubic with three real roots requires complex intermediates in the formula — forced Cardano to confront complex numbers. He could not avoid them if he wanted the formula to work. This "forcing" by algebra is a recurring theme: mathematics discovers new kinds of numbers when existing ones prove insufficient.

## Why it matters

Complex numbers completed the number system in a sense: the [Fundamental Theorem of Algebra](/theorems/fundamental-theorem-of-algebra) says $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed, so no further extension is needed to solve polynomial equations. Complex numbers also have a beautiful geometric interpretation as rotations and scalings in the plane, making them indispensable in physics and engineering.

## Real-world applications

[Signal processing](/applications/signal-processing-jpeg-mp3) uses complex exponentials at every step. [Quantum computing](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms) is inherently complex-valued — quantum states are vectors in $\mathbb{C}^n$.
