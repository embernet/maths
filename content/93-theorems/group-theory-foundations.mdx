## What it says

**Lagrange's theorem:** If $H$ is a subgroup of a finite group $G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

Proof: the cosets $gH$ partition $G$ into equal-sized pieces, each of size $|H|$. The number of pieces is $[G:H] = |G|/|H|$, which must be an integer.

Consequence: the order of any element $g \in G$ divides $|G|$ (since $\langle g \rangle$ is a subgroup of order equal to the element's order). This immediately implies Fermat's little theorem: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ for prime $p$ and $\gcd(a, p) = 1$, since $(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^*$ has order $p-1$.

## History

[Lagrange](/mathematicians/lagrange) proved a precursor of this theorem in 1770 while studying permutation groups and the roots of polynomials. He noticed that the number of values a function of $n$ variables takes under permutations of the variables always divides $n!$. This was the seed of group theory, sixty years before Cauchy and Galois formalised the group concept.

## Why it matters

Lagrange's theorem is the first structural result in group theory. It immediately gives non-trivial information: groups of prime order $p$ have no subgroups other than $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself, so they are cyclic. The theorem is used in every group theory proof that involves "counting."

## Real-world applications

Lagrange's theorem underlies the correctness of [RSA cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) (via the group structure of $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^*$) and [error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes).
