## What it says

Analytic geometry studies geometric objects using algebraic equations via a coordinate system. In the plane, curves are sets of points satisfying equations $f(x, y) = 0$. The standard conic sections — circle, ellipse, parabola, hyperbola — are the curves of degree-2 equations. Properties like tangency, intersection, and area become algebraic computations.

Key techniques: parametric representation $(x(t), y(t))$; vector methods; the distance formula; the equation of a line through two points.

In three dimensions, surfaces are defined by $f(x, y, z) = 0$, and curves by pairs of equations.

## History

[Descartes](/mathematicians/descartes) and [Fermat](/mathematicians/fermat) both developed analytic geometry independently around 1637. Descartes published in *La Géométrie*; Fermat circulated manuscripts. Fermat's approach was in some ways closer to the modern one — he explicitly introduced two-dimensional coordinates and studied general curves — while Descartes emphasised the use of a single variable. Newton and Leibniz used analytic geometry as the foundation for their calculus.

## Why it matters

Analytic geometry is the language in which calculus is formulated: derivatives are slopes of tangent lines to curves, integrals are areas under curves. Without coordinates, these concepts have no algebraic expression. Analytic geometry also makes higher-dimensional geometry possible: you can study curves and surfaces in $\mathbb{R}^n$ without geometric intuition, purely through equations.

[Computer graphics](/applications/computer-graphics-3d) represents all geometry algebraically. [GPS](/applications/gps-positioning) and [robotics](/applications/robotics-and-control) use coordinate geometry throughout.
