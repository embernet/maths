## What it says

A sequence $(a_n)$ is a **Cauchy sequence** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that for all $m, n > N$:

$$|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$$

The key point: you never mention the limit. You only require that terms cluster together. In the real numbers (and any complete metric space), every Cauchy sequence converges — this is the defining property of completeness.

The contrapositive is equally useful: if a sequence is not Cauchy, it cannot converge, regardless of how you extend the space.

## History

[Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy) introduced this characterisation of convergence in *Cours d'analyse* (1821) as part of his project to put calculus on rigorous foundations. Before Cauchy, convergence was intuitive — a sequence "tends to a limit" if it seems to approach something. Cauchy made it precise. The deeper question — why do Cauchy sequences in $\mathbb{R}$ converge? — was answered only when [Dedekind](/mathematicians/dedekind) and [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) constructed the reals rigorously from the rationals in the 1870s.

## Why it matters

Cauchy sequences are the bridge between algebra and analysis. They let you construct $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ by "completing" — adding in all the missing limits. The same construction works for any metric space: complete it by adjoining the limits of all Cauchy sequences. Banach spaces (complete normed vector spaces) are defined this way. This is not just abstract machinery; numerical methods for differential equations produce Cauchy sequences and rely on completeness to guarantee convergence to a solution.

## Real-world applications

Iterative numerical solvers used in [weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) and [climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) converge because the underlying update procedures produce Cauchy sequences in appropriate function spaces.
