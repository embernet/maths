## What it says

Analytic number theory uses complex analysis — particularly the theory of the [Riemann zeta function](/theorems/riemann-zeta-function) and its generalisations — to study the distribution of prime numbers and the values of arithmetic functions.

Key results:
- **Prime Number Theorem:** $\pi(x) \sim x / \ln x$ as $x \to \infty$ (proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, 1896, using $\zeta(s)$ near the line $\text{Re}(s) = 1$)
- **Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions:** There are infinitely many primes of the form $an + b$ when $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, proved using Dirichlet $L$-functions
- **Circle method (Hardy–Ramanujan–Vinogradov):** Extracts asymptotics for representation problems

## History

The subject was founded by Dirichlet and Riemann in the nineteenth century. [Hardy](/mathematicians/hardy) and Littlewood developed it extensively in the early twentieth century, particularly the circle method and Waring's problem (can every sufficiently large integer be written as the sum of $k$ perfect $n$th powers?).

## Why it matters

Analytic number theory connects the most elementary-seeming objects (prime numbers, their counting function) to the deepest tools of complex analysis. The Riemann hypothesis, if proved, would give the sharpest possible result on prime distribution. The field continues to be active, with connections to random matrix theory and the Langlands programme.

## Real-world applications

Understanding prime distribution underpins the security of [RSA cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography).
