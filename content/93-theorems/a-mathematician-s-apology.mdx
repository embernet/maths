## What it says

*A Mathematician's Apology* (1940) is not a theorem but an essay in which [Hardy](/mathematicians/hardy) argues:
- Mathematics is a creative art, and the mathematician's patterns, like the painter's or poet's, must be beautiful.
- Mathematical beauty cannot be defined precisely but involves a quality of unexpectedness, inevitability, and economy.
- A mathematical proof is "beautiful" if its ideas are surprising, its statement concise, and its argument elegant.
- Pure mathematics is justifiably useless — Hardy proudly noted that number theory had no military applications, a claim that has not survived to the present day.
- The young mathematician who can no longer create mathematical ideas should not pretend otherwise; old age is a tragedy for a mathematician.

## History

Hardy wrote the essay in 1940 near the end of his mathematical career, after a serious heart attack. It is shot through with melancholy. [C.P. Snow](/mathematicians/hardy)'s preface in the 1967 edition provides biographical context. The essay remains the most personal and candid account ever written by a major mathematician of what it feels like to do mathematics.

## Why it matters

*A Mathematician's Apology* is one of the few accounts of the inner life of mathematics written by a practitioner at the height of their powers (or just past it). It raises questions — what is mathematical beauty? Is pure mathematics justified? — that are relevant to every mathematics student.

## Real-world applications

None — the point of the essay is to defend mathematics that has no applications.
