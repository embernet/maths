## What it says

If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then there exists $c \in (a, b)$ such that:

$$f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$$

In plain language: the slope of the tangent at $c$ equals the slope of the chord from $a$ to $b$. Geometrically, somewhere on the curve the tangent is parallel to the secant joining the endpoints.

The special case $f(a) = f(b)$ is **Rolle's theorem**: if a differentiable function returns to its starting value, it must have zero derivative somewhere in between.

## History

Lagrange stated a version of the theorem in 1797, but [Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy) gave the first rigorous proof in his 1823 *Résumé des leçons*. He needed it to prove that functions with identically zero derivative are constant — without the MVT, you cannot make that step, and calculus rests on a gap. Bolzano had earlier proved the Intermediate Value Theorem, and both results are part of the same programme of filling in the logical foundations of analysis.

## Why it matters

The MVT is perhaps the most-used tool in theoretical calculus. Almost every proof that requires "going from a local condition (derivative) to a global conclusion (function values)" passes through the MVT. It implies:
- A function with positive derivative on an interval is increasing.
- If two functions have the same derivative, they differ by a constant.
- Taylor's theorem (in one of its forms).
- Error bounds for numerical differentiation.

These are not curiosities — they are the scaffolding that holds rigorous analysis together.

## Real-world applications

Numerical solvers for differential equations use the MVT to establish error bounds. [Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) and [climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) rely on such solvers at every timestep.
