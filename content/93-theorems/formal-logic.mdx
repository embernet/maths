## What it says

[Leibniz](/mathematicians/leibniz) dreamed of a **characteristica universalis** — a universal language in which all concepts could be precisely expressed — and a **calculus ratiocinator** — a mechanical procedure for determining the truth or falsehood of any statement expressed in that language. Disputes would be settled not by argument but by calculation: "Let us calculate!"

He developed several prototypical logical calculi, including a primitive predicate logic and rules for syllogism. He also worked on modal logic (the logic of possibility and necessity).

## History

Leibniz's logical work was not systematically published and had little direct influence on mathematics. It was largely rediscovered and superseded by Boole (1854), Frege (1879), and Russell and Whitehead (1910–13). In retrospect, Leibniz's programme was remarkably prescient — the characteristica universalis foreshadows both formal logic and programming languages, and the calculus ratiocinator foreshadows automated theorem proving.

## Why it matters

Leibniz's vision was centuries ahead of its realisation. The programme he sketched — universal formal language, mechanical reasoning — is exactly what mathematical logic and computer science eventually achieved. Turing machines and formal proof systems are the realisation of Leibniz's dream.

## Real-world applications

Formal verification tools and automated theorem provers implement Leibniz's calculus ratiocinator programme in modern form.
