## What it says

[Euler](/mathematicians/euler) introduced or popularised key symbols that are now universal:

- **$e$** (Euler's number, base of natural logarithm): first used in 1727, published in *Mechanica* (1736). Euler did not name it after himself — that convention followed naturally.
- **$i = \sqrt{-1}$** (imaginary unit): Euler used this notation extensively, though Descartes and others had earlier used $\sqrt{-1}$ without a dedicated symbol.
- **$f(x)$** (function notation): introduced in 1734, codifying the idea of a function as a mapping.
- **$\Sigma$** (summation): introduced in 1755 in *Institutiones calculi differentialis*.
- **$\pi$**: the ratio $\pi = C/d$ had been studied for millennia, but the symbol was popularised by Euler from 1736 (used by William Jones in 1706 — Euler made it universal). Euler did not invent $\pi$ or the concept.

## History

Euler was the most prolific mathematician in history, and his notational innovations reflect his gift for systematic exposition. He wrote with such clarity and such range that his textbooks became the standard European reference for a century. A theorem without Eulerian notation often had to be re-expressed when applied.

## Why it matters

Good notation is not decoration. $f(x)$ makes the idea of a function as a mapping immediately legible. $\Sigma$ compresses infinite series into single symbols. $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ (Euler's formula) is legible in his notation in a way it would not be in any earlier notation system. Mathematical progress is partly notational progress.

## Real-world applications

Every formula in every field of quantitative science uses at least one of Euler's notational conventions.
