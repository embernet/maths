## What it says

**Pascal's theorem:** If $ABCDEF$ is a hexagon inscribed in a conic (circle, ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola), then the points where opposite sides meet — $(AB \cap DE)$, $(BC \cap EF)$, $(CD \cap FA)$ — are collinear (lie on a single line called the **Pascal line**).

This is a theorem of projective geometry: it holds for any conic and any hexagon inscribed in it, regardless of the shape. The dual theorem (Brianchon's theorem, 1806) states that if a hexagon is circumscribed about a conic, the three main diagonals are concurrent.

## History

[Pascal](/mathematicians/pascal) proved this at age 16 in 1640, in an essay he called his "Essay on Conics." Leibniz saw a copy and was reportedly impressed. The theorem is a consequence of the cross-ratio being preserved under projection, which is why it holds for all conics (they are all projectively equivalent to a circle).

## Why it matters

Pascal's theorem is an early example of projective geometry: theorems about incidence, concurrence, and collinearity that hold without any notion of distance or angle. Projective geometry underlies computer vision, perspective drawing, and the geometry of [3D rendering](/applications/computer-graphics-3d).

## Real-world applications

[Computer graphics](/applications/computer-graphics-3d) uses projective geometry for perspective transformations and homogeneous coordinates.
