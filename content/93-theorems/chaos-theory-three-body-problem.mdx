## What it says

The **three-body problem** asks: given the initial positions and velocities of three mutually gravitating bodies, predict their future motion. Unlike the two-body problem (which has a complete analytical solution), the three-body problem generally does not.

[Poincaré](/mathematicians/poincare) showed in 1890 that generic orbits of three gravitating bodies do not follow quasi-periodic paths but can exhibit wild, chaotic behaviour: trajectories that are highly sensitive to initial conditions. He introduced the **Poincaré section** (cross-section of a flow), **stable and unstable manifolds**, and the **homoclinic tangle** — the tangled intersection of stable and unstable manifolds that generates chaos.

**Sensitive dependence on initial conditions:** small differences in initial state grow exponentially (the Lyapunov exponent is positive).

## History

Poincaré won a prize competition in 1888 with what he thought was a proof of stability; he then found an error and his correction became the discovery of chaos. He published it in *Les Méthodes nouvelles de la mécanique céleste* (1892–99). The field lay dormant until Lorenz (1963) rediscovered chaos in weather models and Smale (1965) formalised the horseshoe map.

## Why it matters

Chaos theory shows that deterministic systems can produce unpredictable behaviour. It is not randomness — the system follows exact differential equations — but sensitivity to initial conditions makes long-term prediction impossible in principle. This sets fundamental limits on weather forecasting and explains why the solar system, though deterministic, has an unpredictable long-term future.

## Real-world applications

[Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) operates in a chaotic system — predictability is limited to about 10–14 days. [Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) uses ensemble methods to handle chaos. [Population dynamics](/applications/population-dynamics-ecology) exhibits chaos in simple models (the logistic map).
