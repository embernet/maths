## What it says

**Gödel's completeness theorem (1929):** A sentence $\phi$ is a logical consequence of a set of axioms $\Sigma$ (i.e., true in every model of $\Sigma$) if and only if there is a formal proof of $\phi$ from $\Sigma$ using the inference rules of first-order logic.

In short: **semantic entailment $\Leftrightarrow$ syntactic provability** for first-order logic.

This is completeness of the *proof system*, not completeness of a specific *theory*. First-order logic's proof system is complete. Specific theories (like Peano arithmetic) may be incomplete — which is what the later [incompleteness theorems](/theorems/incompleteness-theorems-1931) show.

## History

[Gödel](/mathematicians/godel) proved this in his 1929 doctoral dissertation at the University of Vienna, under Hans Hahn. It was his first major result. The theorem was expected but had not been formally proved; Gödel's proof established that first-order logic has a "complete" proof system in the sense that no valid argument is unprovable.

## Why it matters

The completeness theorem establishes that first-order logic is a good foundation for mathematics: anything that is true in all models can in principle be proved. Combined with the compactness theorem (a consequence), it gives powerful tools for constructing models and studying theories. It also sets the stage for Gödel's incompleteness theorems — which show that specific powerful theories are *incomplete* despite logic being *complete*.

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but formal methods for software verification and automated theorem proving use these logical foundations.
