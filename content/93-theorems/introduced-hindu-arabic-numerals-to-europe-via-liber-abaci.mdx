## What it says

[Fibonacci](/mathematicians/fibonacci) (Leonardo of Pisa) opened *Liber Abaci* (1202) with the words: "The nine Indian figures are: 9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2 1. With these nine figures, and with the sign 0 which the Arabs call zephyr, any number whatsoever is written."

The book then demonstrated arithmetic using these symbols across hundreds of practical problems — currency exchange, profit calculations, weights and measures, geometric progressions, and the famous rabbit problem (giving rise to [Fibonacci numbers](/theorems/fibonacci-sequence)). It showed that positional arithmetic with zero was enormously more efficient for commercial calculation than Roman numerals and the abacus.

## History

Fibonacci had grown up in North Africa, where his father was a Pisan customs official, and had learnt arithmetic from Arab teachers. *Liber Abaci* was a work of mathematical advocacy as much as instruction: Fibonacci wanted to persuade Italian merchants that the new system was better. The book was revised in 1228. It circulated widely among merchants and scholars, and over the following two centuries Hindu-Arabic numerals gradually displaced Roman ones in European bookkeeping.

## Why it matters

The Hindu-Arabic numeral system, especially zero, made possible the mathematical development of Europe from the late medieval period onwards. It is a necessary prerequisite for algebraic notation, calculus, and the scientific revolution. That it was introduced to Europe through a practical commercial text is a reminder that mathematical tools often spread through economic usefulness.

## Real-world applications

The entire numeral system used in all modern science and computing traces to this transmission.
