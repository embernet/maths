## What it says

[Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert) presented 23 problems at the 1900 International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris (he actually presented 10 in the talk; the full list was published later). They ranged across all of mathematics:

- Problem 1: Cantor's continuum hypothesis
- Problem 2: Consistency of arithmetic (resolved negatively by Gödel)
- Problem 8: Riemann hypothesis (still open)
- Problem 10: Does every Diophantine equation have a computable solution? (resolved negatively by Matiyasevich, 1970)
- Problem 16: Topology of algebraic curves and surfaces (partly open)
- Problem 23: Development of the calculus of variations

As of 2025, some are resolved (6 fully, 8 partially), some remain open.

## History

Hilbert was the dominant mathematician of his era; his choice of problems was an act of intellectual direction-setting. Many of the problems were solved by his students and followers. The contrast between his optimism ("there is no ignorabimus in mathematics — we must know, we shall know") and Gödel's incompleteness results is one of the dramatic intellectual reversals of the twentieth century.

## Why it matters

Hilbert's problems defined the research agenda of twentieth-century mathematics. Problems from his list drove the development of logic, set theory, analysis, algebraic geometry, and number theory. The [Millennium Prize Problems](https://www.claymath.org/) (2000) are the modern equivalent — seven problems each with a \$1 million prize.

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but several problems (Riemann hypothesis, complexity theory adjacent to Problem 10) have indirect consequences for [cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography).
