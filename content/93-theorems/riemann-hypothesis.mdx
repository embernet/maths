## What it says

The [Riemann zeta function](/theorems/riemann-zeta-function) $\zeta(s)$ has "trivial" zeros at $s = -2, -4, -6, \ldots$ and infinitely many "non-trivial" zeros in the critical strip $0 < \text{Re}(s) < 1$.

**The hypothesis:** All non-trivial zeros lie on the critical line $\text{Re}(s) = \frac{1}{2}$.

Over $10^{13}$ zeros have been numerically verified to lie on the critical line. No counterexample has been found. No proof exists. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a \$1 million prize for a proof or disproof.

## History

[Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann) stated the hypothesis in an 1859 paper of eight pages — one of the most influential papers in mathematics. He introduced the zeta function as a meromorphic function on $\mathbb{C}$ and noted, almost in passing, that it "is very probable" that all non-trivial zeros have real part $\frac{1}{2}$. In 165 years since, the hypothesis has neither been proved nor disproved, but enormous amounts of mathematics have been created in the attempt — random matrix theory, explicit formulas for prime counting, the theory of $L$-functions.

## Why it matters

The Riemann hypothesis is the key to understanding how prime numbers are distributed. The Prime Number Theorem ($\pi(x) \sim x / \ln x$) was proved unconditionally in 1896, but the *error* in the approximation depends on the zeros of $\zeta(s)$. If the hypothesis is true, primes are "as regularly distributed as they possibly could be." Many theorems in number theory are stated "assuming RH" and would become unconditional theorems the day a proof appears.

## Real-world applications

The security of [RSA cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) and much of modern [cybersecurity](/applications/cybersecurity-anomaly-detection) depends on the difficulty of factoring large numbers, which is related (indirectly) to the distribution of primes that the Riemann hypothesis governs.
