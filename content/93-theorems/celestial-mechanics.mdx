## What it says

[Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace)'s *Mécanique céleste* (1799–1825) applied Newton's gravitational theory to the full complexity of the solar system:

- **Perturbation theory:** how small gravitational forces from other planets slowly alter an orbit
- **Secular stability:** he showed (to leading order) that the orbital eccentricities and inclinations of the planets oscillate but do not grow unboundedly — the solar system appeared stable
- **Laplace transform** and operator methods for solving the resulting differential equations
- **Tidal theory:** explaining the tides via gravitational forcing by Moon and Sun

He also introduced **potential theory** — the study of functions satisfying the [Laplace equation](/theorems/laplace-equation) $\nabla^2 V = 0$ — to describe gravitational fields in space.

## History

Laplace worked on celestial mechanics throughout his long career. He famously reportedly told Napoleon (who asked why God was not mentioned in the *Mécanique*) "I have no need of that hypothesis." His mathematical contributions — especially potential theory and the Laplace transform — outlasted the specific astronomical applications.

## Why it matters

Celestial mechanics is the first great triumph of differential equations as a tool for prediction. Laplace's perturbation theory is still used to compute spacecraft trajectories. The stability question — does the solar system remain stable? — is deeply connected to [chaos theory](/theorems/chaos-theory-three-body-problem) and is not fully resolved.

## Real-world applications

[GPS positioning](/applications/gps-positioning) uses precise orbital mechanics. [Gravitational-wave detection](/applications/gravitational-wave-detection) requires accurate orbital dynamics for template generation.
