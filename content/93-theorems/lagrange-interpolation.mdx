## What it says

Given $n+1$ distinct points $(x_0, y_0), \ldots, (x_n, y_n)$, the **Lagrange interpolating polynomial** is:

$$p(x) = \sum_{j=0}^n y_j \prod_{\substack{k=0 \\ k \neq j}}^n \frac{x - x_k}{x_j - x_k}$$

This is the unique polynomial of degree $\leq n$ satisfying $p(x_j) = y_j$ for all $j$. The basis polynomials $\ell_j(x) = \prod_{k \neq j} \frac{x - x_k}{x_j - x_k}$ satisfy $\ell_j(x_k) = \delta_{jk}$ (1 if $j = k$, 0 otherwise).

**Warning:** Runge's phenomenon — interpolating at equally spaced points leads to large oscillations near the endpoints for high-degree polynomials. Chebyshev points eliminate this.

## History

[Lagrange](/mathematicians/lagrange) published the formula in 1795, though Newton and Gregory had earlier related forms. Lagrange's clean formulation became standard.

## Why it matters

Lagrange interpolation is the foundation of numerical analysis: every quadrature rule (numerical integration), every finite element method, and many ODE solvers use polynomial approximation at their core. Understanding interpolation error — how well a polynomial fits the underlying function between data points — is essential for assessing the accuracy of these methods.

## Real-world applications

[Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) interpolates meteorological data from discrete observation stations. [GPS](/applications/gps-positioning) receivers interpolate satellite positions from tabulated ephemeris data. [Machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) kernel methods can be viewed as interpolation in feature spaces.
