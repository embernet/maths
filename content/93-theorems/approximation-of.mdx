## What it says

[Archimedes](/mathematicians/archimedes) showed that:

$$\frac{223}{71} < \pi < \frac{22}{7}$$

His method: inscribe a regular polygon inside a circle and circumscribe one outside. The circle's circumference is bounded between the two polygons' perimeters. Starting with hexagons (6 sides) and doubling to 96 sides, he computed the perimeters using square root approximations, obtaining a bound correct to two decimal places.

## History

Archimedes presented this in his treatise *Measurement of a Circle* (c. 250 BCE). The bound $\pi < 22/7$ is still taught as a useful approximation. The method of successively doubling polygon sides was used to compute $\pi$ to increasing accuracy for centuries: Ptolemy (150 CE) got 4 decimal places; Zu Chongzhi (480 CE) got 7. Note: $\pi$ was not named $\pi$ by Archimedes — that notation was popularised by [Euler](/mathematicians/euler) in 1736.

## Why it matters

Archimedes' computation is the prototype for algorithmic numerical approximation: iterating a procedure to get a better estimate at each step. The method of exhaustion guarantees that the approximation converges to the true value. This combination — an iterative algorithm with provable convergence — is the template for numerical analysis.

## Real-world applications

[GPS positioning](/applications/gps-positioning) uses $\pi$ in geodesic calculations on an oblate spheroid. [Gravitational-wave detection](/applications/gravitational-wave-detection) uses $\pi$ in the waveform templates of the matched filter. In practice, modern computers use $\pi$ to millions of decimal places, computed by algorithms that are the descendants of Archimedes' polygon method.
