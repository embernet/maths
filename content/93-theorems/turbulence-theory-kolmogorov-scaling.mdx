## What it says

[Kolmogorov](/mathematicians/kolmogorov)'s 1941 (K41) theory assumes that at sufficiently high Reynolds number, turbulent flow has a region of length scales (the **inertial subrange**) where:
- Energy is transferred from large to small eddies at a constant rate $\varepsilon$ (the dissipation rate)
- The statistics of velocity differences depend only on $\varepsilon$ and the scale $r$

By dimensional analysis, the energy spectrum scales as:

$$E(k) \sim \varepsilon^{2/3} k^{-5/3}$$

where $k$ is the wavenumber. This **Kolmogorov $-5/3$ spectrum** has been confirmed in wind tunnels, atmospheric measurements, and ocean data.

## History

Kolmogorov published three papers in 1941 developing this theory. It was a remarkable achievement: turbulence is one of the hardest problems in physics (one of the Millennium Prize Problems concerns the Navier–Stokes equations). Kolmogorov's scaling law gives a statistical description that holds empirically without solving the Navier–Stokes equations.

## Why it matters

Turbulence governs weather, ocean currents, aircraft drag, combustion in engines, and blood flow in vessels. Kolmogorov's theory is the foundation of turbulence modelling — the practical engineering approach to turbulent flows. Direct numerical simulation of turbulence requires resolving scales from the integral length down to the Kolmogorov microscale $\eta = (\nu^3/\varepsilon)^{1/4}$.

## Real-world applications

[Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) and [climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) use turbulence parametrisations based on Kolmogorov's theory. [Aerodynamics](/applications/airfoil-and-aerodynamics) wing drag calculations require turbulence models.
