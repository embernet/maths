## What it says

The **limit** $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L$ means: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

A function $f$ is **continuous** at $a$ if $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$ — the limit exists and equals the function value.

A function $f$ is **uniformly continuous** on $[a, b]$ if the $\delta$ can be chosen independently of $a$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $|x - y| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x, y$.

## History

Before the nineteenth century, calculus used "infinitesimals" — quantities that were smaller than any positive number but not zero. This was effective in practice but logically murky; Berkeley had ridiculed the concept in 1734 as "ghosts of departed quantities." [Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy) in *Cours d'analyse* (1821) replaced infinitesimals with the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ framework for the first time in print. [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) sharpened and formalised the approach in his Berlin lectures from the 1860s, removing all lingering imprecision.

## Why it matters

The $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ language is not just pedantry — it is what makes proofs possible. With it, you can prove that $\sqrt{x}$ is uniformly continuous on $[0, 1]$. Without it, you are waving your hands. The framework also revealed new phenomena: functions continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere ([Weierstrass's function](/theorems/weierstrass-function-continuous-everywhere-differentiable-nowhere)), space-filling curves, and the crucial distinction between pointwise and uniform convergence.

## Real-world applications

Every numerical analysis result about error bounds and convergence rates uses $\varepsilon$-$\delta$-style reasoning.
