## What it says

Let $(X, d)$ be a complete metric space and $T: X \to X$ a **contraction**: there exists $0 \leq k < 1$ such that $d(T(x), T(y)) \leq k\, d(x, y)$ for all $x, y \in X$.

Then:
1. $T$ has a **unique fixed point** $x^* \in X$ with $T(x^*) = x^*$.
2. For any starting point $x_0$, the iteration $x_{n+1} = T(x_n)$ converges to $x^*$.
3. The error bound: $d(x_n, x^*) \leq \frac{k^n}{1-k}\, d(x_1, x_0)$.

The sequence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy (since $T$ is a contraction), and by completeness it converges.

## History

[Banach](/mathematicians/banach) proved the theorem in his 1922 doctoral thesis, as a clean unifying result for many iterative procedures that mathematicians had been using without a general framework. The proof is constructive — it tells you how to find the fixed point and how fast you converge.

## Why it matters

The Banach fixed-point theorem is the universal justification for iterative numerical methods. Solve $f(x) = 0$ by writing it as $x = T(x)$ and iterating. Solve an ODE by Picard iteration. Verify that Newton's method converges near a simple root. All these rest on the same underlying theorem. It also proves the existence and uniqueness theorem for ODEs (Picard–Lindelöf) — arguably the most important existence theorem in applied mathematics.

## Real-world applications

[Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) and [climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) solve large ODE/PDE systems iteratively — the contraction mapping theorem guarantees convergence. [Robotics](/applications/robotics-and-control) uses fixed-point iterations for inverse kinematics. [Machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) gradient descent analyses often use contraction-mapping style arguments.
