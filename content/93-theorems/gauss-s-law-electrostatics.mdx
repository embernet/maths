## What it says

**Gauss's law (integral form):**

$$\oint_S \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$$

The net electric flux through any closed surface $S$ equals the total enclosed charge $Q_{\text{enc}}$ divided by the permittivity of free space $\varepsilon_0$.

**Differential form** (using the divergence theorem):

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$

where $\rho$ is the charge density. This is one of Maxwell's four equations.

For a point charge $Q$, Gauss's law immediately gives the inverse-square law: $\|\mathbf{E}\| = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$, recovered by taking a spherical surface of radius $r$.

## History

[Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) stated this law in 1835 (unpublished at the time; published 1867). It was incorporated by Maxwell into his unified theory of electromagnetism in the 1860s. The divergence theorem itself — which connects the integral and differential forms — is also attributed to Gauss (and independently to Green and Ostrogradsky).

## Why it matters

Gauss's law is one of the most powerful tools in electrostatics: for symmetric charge distributions (spherical, cylindrical, planar), it allows the electric field to be computed almost by inspection. The same mathematical structure — a divergence equation — appears in fluid mechanics, gravity, and heat flow.

## Real-world applications

[MRI](/applications/medical-imaging-mri-ct) uses electromagnetic field calculations governed by Gauss's law. [Radar and sonar](/applications/radar-and-sonar) rely on Maxwell's equations, of which Gauss's law is one. [Seismic imaging](/applications/seismic-imaging-oil-exploration) uses analogous divergence equations for acoustic waves.
