## What it says

The Cartesian coordinate system assigns to each point in the plane a pair of real numbers $(x, y)$ — its signed distances from two perpendicular reference axes. This creates a bijection between geometric objects (points, lines, curves) and algebraic objects (number pairs, equations).

A line is an equation $ax + by = c$. A circle is $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$. The intersection of two curves corresponds to the common solutions of their equations. Geometric problems become algebraic calculations, and algebraic relationships become geometric pictures.

The system extends naturally to three or more dimensions: $(x, y, z)$, $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$.

## History

[Descartes](/mathematicians/descartes) introduced coordinates in *La Géométrie* (1637), an appendix to his *Discours de la méthode*. [Fermat](/mathematicians/fermat) developed related ideas at about the same time, also in 1637. The modern coordinate axis notation is actually due to later mathematicians; Descartes typically placed his axis at the bottom of a figure and used a single horizontal reference. But the conceptual move — algebraising geometry — is fully Descartes's.

## Why it matters

Cartesian coordinates are so universal that they are easy to take for granted. Before them, geometry and algebra were separate subjects. After them, every algebraic equation has a geometric meaning, and every geometric problem can be attacked with algebraic tools. This merger created analytic geometry, which led directly to calculus (Newton and Leibniz both used coordinate geometry) and ultimately to the whole of modern mathematical physics.

## Real-world applications

Every coordinate system in [GPS positioning](/applications/gps-positioning), [robotics](/applications/robotics-and-control), [3D graphics](/applications/computer-graphics-3d), and map-making is a form of Cartesian coordinates.
