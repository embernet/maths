## What it says

Descartes's central insight in *La Géométrie* (1637) was the correspondence:

- **Algebraic equation** $\leftrightarrow$ **Geometric curve**
- **Algebraic solution** $\leftrightarrow$ **Geometric intersection**
- **Algebraic degree** $\leftrightarrow$ **Geometric complexity of curve**

A quadratic equation in $x, y$ gives a conic section. A cubic equation gives a cubic curve. The order of the algebra corresponds precisely to the complexity of the geometry. Solving a system of algebraic equations is the same as finding intersections of curves.

This is not merely a notational convenience: the correspondence is deep and bilateral. Geometric intuition can guide algebraic calculations; algebraic methods can resolve geometric questions that resist direct geometric proof.

## History

[Descartes](/mathematicians/descartes) presented this in *La Géométrie*, written in a deliberately obscure style (he wanted readers to struggle). The insight built on Viète's symbolic algebra and Greek geometry but went far beyond both. It was clarified and popularised by subsequent commentators. The algebraic geometry of the twentieth century — algebraic varieties, schemes, sheaves — is the full modern realisation of Descartes's programme.

## Why it matters

Every branch of geometry from calculus onwards uses coordinates. The correspondence between algebra and geometry is what makes geometric problems tractable by computation. In modern algebraic geometry, this correspondence is elevated to a profound duality: rings correspond to geometric spaces, and geometric properties translate into algebraic properties.
