## What it says

Ramanujan's notebooks contain thousands of results, including:

- **Series for 1/π:** $\frac{1}{\pi} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{9801}\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(4n)!(1103+26390n)}{(n!)^4 396^{4n}}$. One of his series for $1/\pi$ provides 14 decimal digits per term and was used in 1985 by Gosper to compute $\pi$ to 17 million digits.

- **Continued fractions** with highly non-obvious closed forms
- **Identities for hypergeometric functions**, mock theta functions, and modular forms
- **Formulas for sums of integer powers**, partition congruences, and highly composite numbers

Many of these were written without proof; the task of verifying and contextualising them has occupied mathematicians for a century.

## History

[Ramanujan](/mathematicians/ramanujan) left school at 16 and worked as a clerk in Madras. He kept notebooks of his mathematical discoveries, taught himself from Carr's *Synopsis of Elementary Results* (a collection of formulas), and arrived at sophisticated results by a combination of intuition and pattern recognition. His notebooks were entrusted to the University of Madras and eventually published; the "Lost Notebook" (found by Andrews in 1976) contained mock theta functions.

## Why it matters

Ramanujan's work represents a singular case of mathematical genius developing in near-isolation. His formulas have connections — often unexpected and understood only decades later — to deep areas of modern mathematics: string theory, black hole entropy, the Monster group, the Langlands programme.

## Real-world applications

Ramanujan's $\pi$ series are used in high-precision computation of $\pi$.
