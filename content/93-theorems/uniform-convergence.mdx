## What it says

A sequence of functions $f_n: [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ **converges pointwise** to $f$ if for each fixed $x$, $f_n(x) \to f(x)$. The $N$ that makes $|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$ may depend on both $\varepsilon$ and $x$.

The sequence **converges uniformly** to $f$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ (depending only on $\varepsilon$, not on $x$) such that $n > N$ implies $|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x \in [a,b]$.

Key theorems:
- **Continuity preserved:** The uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. (Pointwise limit need not be.)
- **Integration:** $\int_a^b f_n \to \int_a^b f$ under uniform convergence.
- **Differentiation:** If $f_n \to f$ pointwise and $f_n' \to g$ uniformly, then $f$ is differentiable and $f' = g$.

Without uniform convergence these fail: Fourier's original treatment of the heat equation (1807) involved interchanging limits and integrals in ways that are only justified by uniform convergence, and early analysts did not always check the condition.

## History

The distinction between pointwise and uniform convergence was first made precise by [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) in unpublished lectures around 1841, though he published it later. Abel (1826) had noticed anomalies in the convergence of Fourier series; Seidel (1847) identified the issue as the non-uniformity of convergence near points of discontinuity. Weierstrass's systematic treatment, and his [nowhere-differentiable function](/theorems/weierstrass-function-continuous-everywhere-differentiable-nowhere) as a demonstration of what can go wrong, made the concept indispensable.

## Why it matters

Uniform convergence is the correct condition for swapping limits with integrals and derivatives — operations performed constantly in analysis. Without it, arguments that "look right" can fail. It is one of the core concepts in real analysis, alongside Cauchy sequences and $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ limits, that every analyst must internalise.

## Real-world applications

Uniform convergence underpins the validity of truncating infinite series in numerical computation: it guarantees that the partial sum approximates the limit uniformly, giving computable error bounds.
