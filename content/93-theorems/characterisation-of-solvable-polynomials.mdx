## What it says

**Galois's theorem:** A polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group $\text{Gal}(f)$ is a solvable group.

A group $G$ is **solvable** if it has a composition series in which every factor is abelian. The symmetric groups $S_n$ are solvable for $n \leq 4$ but not for $n \geq 5$, because $A_5$ (the alternating group on 5 elements) is simple and non-abelian.

Consequence: the general polynomial of degree $n \geq 5$ has Galois group $S_n$, which is not solvable, so no general radical formula exists. For specific polynomials of degree 5 or higher, the Galois group may be smaller and solvable — those polynomials do have radical solutions.

## History

This is the core theorem that [Galois](/mathematicians/galois) developed in 1830–32. It unified and explained what [Abel](/mathematicians/abel) had shown in 1826 (no general quintic formula) by identifying exactly which polynomials are solvable. Galois's manuscripts lay unread until Joseph Liouville published them in 1846. The theorem required not just permutation group theory but the concept of a normal subgroup — also introduced by Galois.

## Why it matters

Before Galois, the question "is this polynomial solvable?" had no systematic answer. Galois provided an algorithm: compute the Galois group, check if it is solvable. The deeper importance is the structural template: a question about one kind of object (polynomial equations) is answered by studying a different kind of object (groups). This dictionary — algebraic structures correspond to symmetry groups — is the founding idea of modern algebra and appears throughout mathematics in the form of functors, representations, and correspondences.

## Real-world applications

Galois fields — the finite fields that arise from this theory — are the algebraic foundation of [error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes) used in data storage and communication.
