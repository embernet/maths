## What it says

There is no formula involving only $+, -, \times, \div$, and $n$th roots ($\sqrt[n]{\phantom{x}}$) that expresses the roots of a general degree-5 polynomial $x^5 + a_4 x^4 + a_3 x^3 + a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0 = 0$ in terms of its coefficients.

This does not mean quintic equations have no roots — the [Fundamental Theorem of Algebra](/theorems/fundamental-theorem-of-algebra) guarantees five complex roots. It means no algebraic formula using radicals can express them.

The contrast: the quadratic formula ($\frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$) does this for degree 2. Cardano's formulas do it for degrees 3 and 4. Degree 5 and above: impossible in general.

## History

Paolo Ruffini gave incomplete proofs in 1799 and 1813, but they contained gaps and were largely ignored. [Abel](/mathematicians/abel) published a complete, rigorous proof in 1826 in the first volume of *Crelle's Journal* — he sent it to Gauss, who reportedly never read it. Abel died of tuberculosis in 1829 at age 26, never knowing the full impact of his work. [Galois](/mathematicians/galois) then identified *which* quintics have radical solutions (those with solvable Galois groups), a sharper and more general result.

## Why it matters

Abel's theorem closed a 300-year-old problem: mathematicians had been trying to extend Cardano's formulas to degree 5 since 1545. More importantly, the proof method — showing impossibility by analysing the structure of permutations of the roots — was the seed of Galois theory. It showed that the way to answer "can we solve this algebraically?" is to study the symmetries of the solutions.

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but the framework of Galois fields that grew from this work underpins modern [error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes).
