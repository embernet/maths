## What it says

The **Kolmogorov complexity** $K(x)$ of a string $x$ (relative to a fixed universal Turing machine) is the length of the shortest program that outputs $x$ and halts.

Key properties:
- $K(x) \leq |x| + c$ (you can always describe $x$ by copying it, up to a constant)
- Most strings of length $n$ have $K(x) \approx n$ (they are incompressible — no short description exists)
- $K$ is not computable (computing it would solve the halting problem)
- **Coding theorem:** $K(x) \approx -\log_2 P_{\text{opt}}(x)$ where $P_{\text{opt}}$ is the optimal compressor

## History

[Kolmogorov](/mathematicians/kolmogorov) introduced the concept in 1963; Ray Solomonoff and Gregory Chaitin independently developed related ideas. Chaitin's $\Omega$ (the halting probability) is a specific incompressible number that encodes many arithmetic truths.

## Why it matters

Kolmogorov complexity gives a definition of randomness that does not require a probability distribution: a string is random if it has no short description. This connects information theory, computability, and probability in a deep way. It also gives insight into [data compression](/applications/data-compression) — you cannot compress below $K(x)$ — and provides theoretical grounding for Occam's razor (prefer simpler models).

## Real-world applications

[Data compression](/applications/data-compression) algorithms approach the Kolmogorov complexity limit. [Machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) generalisation bounds can be phrased in terms of description length.
