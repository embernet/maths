## What it says

Hilbert's programme, developed in the 1920s, sought to:
1. Formalise all of mathematics as a formal axiomatic system
2. Prove that this system is **consistent** (cannot derive contradictions) — using only finitary methods
3. Prove that the system is **complete** (every mathematical truth is provable)
4. Prove that the system is **decidable** (an algorithm exists to determine whether any statement is provable)

The programme was motivated by the foundational crises of the late nineteenth century: Russell's paradox, Cantor's transfinite sets, and the logical paradoxes that threatened to undermine mathematics.

## History

[Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert) launched the programme at his 1920 Königsberg lecture. It attracted major mathematicians to proof theory. In 1931, Gödel's [incompleteness theorems](/theorems/incompleteness-theorems-1931) demolished it: no consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic can prove its own consistency, and no such system is complete. Turing's undecidability results (1936) finished off the decidability goal.

## Why it matters

Hilbert's programme, though failed, was enormously productive. The attempt to formalise mathematics created proof theory, model theory, and recursion theory. The failure — Gödel's theorems — is one of the deepest results in all of mathematics. Understanding what cannot be done is as important as what can.

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but the logic and computability theory developed in the programme's wake underlies all of computer science.
