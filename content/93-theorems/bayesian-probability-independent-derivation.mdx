## What it says

[Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace) independently derived the theorem of inverse probability (now called Bayes' theorem) in 1774, several years after Bayes's posthumous paper. Laplace's formulation was clearer and more general:

$$P(\text{cause} \mid \text{effect}) \propto P(\text{effect} \mid \text{cause}) \cdot P(\text{cause})$$

He applied it to practical problems: estimating the true value of a physical quantity from multiple measurements, estimating birth rates from census data, and assessing the probability of events from historical frequencies. His **rule of succession** estimated the probability that the sun will rise tomorrow given that it has risen every day for recorded history.

Laplace also developed the **method of moments** and showed that the posterior distribution, with a flat prior and many observations, concentrates around the maximum likelihood estimate — an early version of what is now called the Bernstein–von Mises theorem.

## History

Laplace was a far more prolific developer of Bayesian methods than Bayes himself. Much of what we call "Bayesian statistics" in practice was first systematically applied by Laplace.

## Why it matters

Laplace showed that probability theory can be used for inference — reasoning from effects back to causes — not just for computing frequencies. This is the Bayesian programme, and it underpins modern probabilistic machine learning.
