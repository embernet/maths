## What it says

**Bernoulli's equation** (for steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a streamline):

$$P + \frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}$$

where $P$ is pressure, $\rho$ is fluid density, $v$ is flow speed, $g$ is gravitational acceleration, and $h$ is height.

The equation says: where fluid speeds up, pressure falls (and vice versa). This is why a wing generates lift — faster airflow over the curved upper surface creates lower pressure than the slower flow below.

Daniel Bernoulli also developed an early kinetic theory of gases in the same 1738 work *Hydrodynamica*, proposing that gas pressure results from the bombardment of container walls by moving molecules — a remarkably modern idea.

## History

Daniel [Bernoulli](/mathematicians/bernoulli-family) published *Hydrodynamica* in 1738. His father Johann (unfairly) backdated his own competing text *Hydraulica* to claim priority, a bitter episode in a family already given to priority disputes. Daniel's kinetic theory of gases was ignored for a century until Maxwell and Boltzmann developed it properly.

## Why it matters

Bernoulli's equation is the simplest and most widely used result in fluid mechanics. It applies whenever viscosity and compressibility can be neglected — aerodynamics at moderate speeds, water flow in pipes, wind over terrain. The Navier–Stokes equations (a Millennium Prize Problem) generalise it to viscous flows.

## Real-world applications

[Aerodynamics and airfoil design](/applications/airfoil-and-aerodynamics) use Bernoulli's equation for lift calculations. [Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) models incorporate incompressible flow approximations for large-scale atmospheric dynamics.
