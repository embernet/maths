## What it says

[Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) gave the definitive formulation of the **$\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition of limit**: a function $f$ has limit $L$ at $a$ if for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < |x - a| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$.

From this he built a complete rigorous framework:

- **Continuity**: $f$ is continuous at $a$ if $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$
- **Uniform continuity**: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, a single $\delta > 0$ works for all $a$ simultaneously
- **Uniform convergence** of a sequence of functions $f_n \to f$: for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that $n > N$ implies $|f_n(x) - f(x)| < \varepsilon$ for all $x$
- **Completeness of $\mathbb{R}$**: every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges

He also introduced the construction of the real numbers via power series over rationals, tying the whole framework together without reference to geometric intuition about "infinitely small quantities."

## History

Cauchy had taken a first step towards rigour in his 1821 *Cours d'analyse*, but his treatment still relied on intuitive notions. Weierstrass, from his lectures at Berlin in the 1860s and 1870s — which were distributed in notes and deeply influenced an entire generation of analysts — gave the fully rigorous treatment. He showed that without precision, seemingly obvious results fail: a function can be continuous everywhere yet differentiable nowhere (his [famous example](/theorems/weierstrass-function-continuous-everywhere-differentiable-nowhere)). His approach, transmitted through students such as Cantor, Klein, and Schwarz, became the standard treatment.

## Why it matters

Rigorous analysis ended a century of productive but logically unsound work in calculus. It established that intuition must be checked against precise definitions — a principle that now governs all of mathematics. The $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition is the first technical hurdle every undergraduate analyst faces, and the ability to read and write $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ proofs is the defining skill of a working analyst.

## Real-world applications

The rigorous framework underpins numerical analysis and error bounds — knowing precisely when approximations are valid and quantifying how close they are.
