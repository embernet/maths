## What it says

Given data $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ from a distribution with parameter $\theta$, the **likelihood** is:

$$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n p(x_i; \theta)$$

The **maximum likelihood estimate (MLE)** is $\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_\theta L(\theta)$, or equivalently (and numerically more tractable) $\hat{\theta} = \arg\max_\theta \ell(\theta)$ where $\ell = \log L$ is the **log-likelihood**.

Key properties of MLEs: under regularity conditions, they are consistent (converge to the true parameter), asymptotically normal, and asymptotically efficient (achieve the Cramér–Rao lower bound, related to the [Fisher information](/theorems/the-fisher-information-matrix)).

## History

[Fisher](/mathematicians/fisher) published MLE in 1922, following Gauss's earlier use of least squares (a special case of MLE for Gaussian errors). Fisher showed that MLE is optimal in a broad asymptotic sense, unifying many ad hoc estimation methods. His 1925 paper on the theory of estimation established the framework used in statistics to this day.

## Why it matters

MLE is the workhorse of statistical estimation. Linear regression (OLS is MLE for Gaussian errors), logistic regression, neural network training (cross-entropy loss is negative log-likelihood for categorical distributions), and virtually every fitted model in statistics and machine learning is an MLE or a Bayesian approximation to one. Understanding MLE means understanding the foundations of learning from data.

## Real-world applications

[Clinical trials](/applications/clinical-trials-design) use MLE for treatment effect estimates. [Machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) training minimises negative log-likelihood. [Medical diagnosis](/applications/medical-diagnosis-screening) fits diagnostic models by MLE. [Census statistics](/applications/census-and-survey-statistics) use MLE for demographic parameter estimation.
