## What it says

If the Lagrangian of a physical system is invariant under a one-parameter family of transformations, then there is a corresponding conserved quantity.

Examples:
- **Time translation symmetry** $\Rightarrow$ **conservation of energy**: if the laws of physics are the same today as yesterday, energy is conserved.
- **Spatial translation symmetry** $\Rightarrow$ **conservation of momentum**: if the laws are the same here and there, momentum is conserved.
- **Rotational symmetry** $\Rightarrow$ **conservation of angular momentum**.

The converse also holds: every conservation law arises from a symmetry.

## History

[Noether](/mathematicians/noether) proved the theorem in 1915 while working at Göttingen, invited by Hilbert and Klein to help resolve questions about energy conservation in Einstein's general relativity. The theorem was published in 1918 and immediately recognised as fundamental. It was, however, published by a woman who was not allowed to hold a professorship at Göttingen — she worked for years without pay. Hilbert reportedly said to those who objected: "I do not see that the sex of the candidate is an argument against her admission as a Privatdozent. After all, we are a university, not a bath house."

## Why it matters

Noether's theorem is arguably the deepest insight in all of theoretical physics. It explains *why* conservation laws hold — not as empirical facts but as mathematical consequences of the symmetries of the laws themselves. Every force, every interaction in modern physics is described in terms of symmetries (gauge symmetries); every conserved quantity is a Noether charge.
