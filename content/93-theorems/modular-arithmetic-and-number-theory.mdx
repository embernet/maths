## What it says

Two integers $a$ and $b$ are **congruent modulo $n$** (written $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$) if $n$ divides $a - b$. Congruence is an equivalence relation, and the equivalence classes form a ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ under addition and multiplication.

Key results:
- $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field for prime $p$ (every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse)
- The Chinese Remainder Theorem: if $\gcd(m, n) = 1$, then $\mathbb{Z}/mn\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$
- Fermat's little theorem: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$ for prime $p$ and $\gcd(a, p) = 1$

## History

[Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) systematised modular arithmetic in *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801), writing it in the congruence notation $a \equiv b \pmod{n}$ that is now standard. The book also contains his proof of the law of quadratic reciprocity (which he called the "golden theorem") and the theory of binary quadratic forms. Gauss was 24 when it was published.

## Why it matters

Modular arithmetic is the language of number theory and modern cryptography. Working modulo a prime makes the integers into a field — a structure where division is possible — and all of linear algebra over $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ follows. The difficulty of inverting certain modular operations (computing discrete logarithms, factoring integers) is the security basis of RSA and elliptic curve cryptography.
