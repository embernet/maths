## What it says

For a parametric model $p(x; \theta)$, the **Fisher information** (scalar case) is:

$$\mathcal{I}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} \log p(X;\theta)\right)^2\right]$$

The **Cramér–Rao bound:** For any unbiased estimator $\hat{\theta}$ of $\theta$:

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\theta}) \geq \frac{1}{\mathcal{I}(\theta)}$$

No unbiased estimator can have variance smaller than $1/\mathcal{I}(\theta)$. MLEs achieve this bound asymptotically — they are *asymptotically efficient*.

The Fisher information matrix generalises to vector parameters: $\mathcal{I}(\theta)_{ij} = \mathbb{E}[\frac{\partial \log p}{\partial \theta_i} \frac{\partial \log p}{\partial \theta_j}]$.

## History

[Fisher](/mathematicians/fisher) introduced the concept in his 1922 and 1925 papers on estimation. The Cramér–Rao bound was proved independently by Cramér and Rao in 1945–46. The geometric interpretation of Fisher information as a metric on the space of probability distributions (information geometry) was developed by Rao and later formalised by Amari in the 1980s.

## Why it matters

The Fisher information matrix tells you how much you can learn from data about a parameter. High Fisher information means the data is highly informative; low Fisher information means the parameter is hard to estimate. This has practical consequences for experimental design — you want to design experiments that maximise Fisher information. In machine learning, the natural gradient (using the inverse Fisher information matrix as a preconditioner) is a second-order optimisation method.

## Real-world applications

[Clinical trial design](/applications/clinical-trials-design) optimises experiment designs to maximise Fisher information.
