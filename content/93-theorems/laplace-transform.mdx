## What it says

For a function $f(t)$ defined for $t \geq 0$, the **Laplace transform** is:

$$\mathcal{L}\{f\}(s) = F(s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)\, e^{-st}\, dt$$

Key property: $\mathcal{L}\{f'\}(s) = sF(s) - f(0)$. Differentiation becomes multiplication by $s$.

This converts a linear ODE with constant coefficients $ay'' + by' + cy = g(t)$ into an algebraic equation in $F(s)$. Solve for $F(s)$, then invert using tables or the Bromwich integral $f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\sigma - i\infty}^{\sigma + i\infty} F(s) e^{st}\, ds$.

## History

[Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace) used operator methods for differential equations in *Mécanique céleste* (1799). The transform bears his name though earlier precursors exist. Oliver Heaviside popularised operational calculus — essentially the Laplace transform, without the rigorous inversion theory — in the 1890s for electrical circuit analysis.

## Why it matters

The Laplace transform is the workhorse of linear systems analysis and control theory. It converts differential equations into algebraic equations, making it much easier to analyse the steady-state and transient behaviour of linear systems. The transfer function $F(s)$ encodes everything about a linear time-invariant system's response.

## Real-world applications

[Signal processing](/applications/signal-processing-jpeg-mp3) and [robotics control](/applications/robotics-and-control) design is done in the Laplace ($s$) domain. [Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) uses Laplace methods for linearised stability analysis.
