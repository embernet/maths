## What it says

A **Riemann surface** is a connected one-dimensional complex manifold: a surface $S$ covered by open sets, each homeomorphic to an open subset of $\mathbb{C}$, with holomorphic transition maps between overlapping charts.

The key idea: multi-valued functions such as $\sqrt{z}$ or $\log z$ become single-valued when their domain is replaced by a Riemann surface on which the function is well-defined. For $w = \sqrt{z}$, the Riemann surface is a double cover of $\mathbb{C}$ branched at $0$ and $\infty$, homeomorphic to $\mathbb{C}$ itself.

Compact Riemann surfaces are classified by their **genus** $g$: the sphere has $g=0$, the torus $g=1$, and higher-genus surfaces $g \geq 2$ correspond to quotients of the upper half-plane by discrete subgroups of $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$. The **Riemann–Roch theorem** relates the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions on a compact surface to its genus.

## History

[Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann) introduced Riemann surfaces in his 1851 doctoral dissertation at Göttingen, where he was studying multi-valued complex functions. He needed a way to make sense of these functions without ambiguity. His idea — construct a surface over $\mathbb{C}$ on which the function is single-valued — was geometrically radical and initially not fully rigorous. Hermann Weyl gave the rigorous modern definition in 1913.

## Why it matters

Riemann surfaces are where complex analysis, topology, and algebraic geometry meet. Every compact Riemann surface is simultaneously a 1-dimensional complex manifold, a 2-dimensional real surface, and a smooth projective algebraic curve. The uniformisation theorem (Poincaré and Koebe, 1907) — that every simply connected Riemann surface is conformally equivalent to the sphere, the plane, or the upper half-plane — is one of the deepest results in 19th-century mathematics.

## Real-world applications

Riemann surfaces appear in [theoretical physics and mathematics](/applications/theoretical-physics-and-mathematics), particularly in conformal field theory and string theory, where the worldsheet swept out by a string is a Riemann surface.
