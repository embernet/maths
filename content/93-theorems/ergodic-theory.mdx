## What it says

A measure-preserving dynamical system $(X, \mathcal{F}, \mu, T)$ is **ergodic** if every $T$-invariant set has measure 0 or 1.

**Birkhoff's ergodic theorem:** For an ergodic system and any integrable function $f$:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(T^k x) = \int_X f\, d\mu \quad \text{for almost every } x$$

Time averages equal space averages. This justifies the statistical mechanical assumption: a gas molecule eventually visits all accessible states, so time-averaging gives the same result as averaging over all molecules.

## History

The ergodic hypothesis in physics was formulated by Boltzmann in the 1870s. Birkhoff and von Neumann proved ergodic theorems in 1931. [Kolmogorov](/mathematicians/kolmogorov) made major contributions to ergodic theory in the 1950s–60s, including the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theorem about quasi-periodic orbits in nearly-integrable Hamiltonian systems.

## Why it matters

Ergodic theory explains when statistical mechanics is valid: when time averages equal ensemble averages. It also describes when chaotic systems can be modelled probabilistically. The KAM theorem is central to understanding the stability of the solar system.

## Real-world applications

[Weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) and [climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) use ergodic arguments to justify long-run statistics. [Actuarial science](/applications/actuarial-science) assumes ergodic behaviour in many risk models.
