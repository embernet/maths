## What it says

In Euclidean geometry, through any point not on a line there is exactly one parallel. In **hyperbolic geometry** (one form of non-Euclidean geometry), there are infinitely many parallels through a given point. In **elliptic geometry**, there are none.

Both alternatives are consistent — they satisfy all of Euclid's first four postulates, contradicting only the fifth. The angle sum of a triangle is less than $180°$ in hyperbolic geometry and more than $180°$ on a sphere (elliptic case).

The surface of constant negative curvature (the hyperbolic plane) is the natural setting for hyperbolic geometry; the sphere is the setting for elliptic geometry.

## History

[Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) appears to have realised this in private by around 1817–1820 but never published, reportedly fearing controversy. Bolyai (1832) and Lobachevsky (1830) published their hyperbolic geometries publicly and independently. When Bolyai sent his work to Gauss, Gauss replied that he had discovered the same thing years earlier — a response that crushed Bolyai. [Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann)'s 1854 lecture then generalised both geometries into a single framework of variable curvature.

## Why it matters

Non-Euclidean geometry proved that Euclid's parallel postulate is independent of the other axioms — consistent geometries exist without it. This broke the identification of mathematics with physical truth: geometry is a formal system, not a description of reality. Einstein's general relativity uses curved (Riemannian) geometry, validating Riemann's framework in physics.

## Real-world applications

[GPS positioning](/applications/gps-positioning) uses spherical (elliptic) geometry for calculations on Earth's surface. [Gravitational-wave detection](/applications/gravitational-wave-detection) operates in the curved spacetime predicted by general relativity.
