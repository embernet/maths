## What it says

A **left ideal** $I$ of a ring $R$ is a non-empty subset satisfying:
1. $a, b \in I \Rightarrow a - b \in I$ (closed under subtraction)
2. $r \in R$, $a \in I \Rightarrow r \cdot a \in I$ (closed under left multiplication by ring elements)

For commutative rings (no left/right distinction): the ideal generated by elements $a_1, \ldots, a_n$ is $(a_1, \ldots, a_n) = \{r_1 a_1 + \cdots + r_n a_n : r_i \in R\}$.

**Principal ideal:** generated by a single element $a$. In a principal ideal domain (PID), every ideal is principal. The ring $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID; the ideal $(n) = n\mathbb{Z}$ is the set of multiples of $n$.

Quotient rings $R/I$ generalise modular arithmetic: the integers mod $n$ are $\mathbb{Z}/(n)$.

## History

[Dedekind](/mathematicians/dedekind) introduced ideals in 1871 to resolve the failure of unique factorisation in algebraic integer rings. [Noether](/mathematicians/noether) later abstracted and unified the theory of ideals across rings, modules, and fields in the 1920s, establishing the framework of modern abstract algebra.

## Why it matters

Ideals are to rings what normal subgroups are to groups: the correct notion of "kernel" that lets you form quotients. The quotient construction $R/I$ is ubiquitous: $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is modular arithmetic; $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2 + 1)$ is the complex numbers; $F[x]/(p(x))$ for irreducible $p$ is a field extension. Understanding ideals is understanding how rings can be "cut down" to simpler structures.

## Real-world applications

[Error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes) over polynomial rings use ideal structure — cyclic codes are ideals in $\mathbb{F}_q[x]/(x^n - 1)$.
