## What it says

In $\mathbb{Z}$, every integer factors uniquely into primes. In rings of algebraic integers like $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, unique factorisation can fail: $6 = 2 \times 3 = (1 + \sqrt{-5})(1 - \sqrt{-5})$, and all four factors are irreducible.

[Dedekind](/mathematicians/dedekind) restored unique factorisation by replacing elements with **ideals** — special subsets of the ring. In a **Dedekind domain** (an integrally closed Noetherian domain of dimension 1), every nonzero ideal factors uniquely into a product of prime ideals, even when elements themselves don't factor uniquely.

The rings of algebraic integers in number fields are Dedekind domains. This makes ideals the correct objects to study in algebraic number theory.

## History

Dedekind introduced ideals in supplements to Dirichlet's *Vorlesungen über Zahlentheorie* (1871). Kummer had earlier introduced "ideal numbers" as a workaround for the failure of unique factorisation; Dedekind's formulation made the concept rigorous and general.

## Why it matters

Dedekind domains are the algebraic framework for modern number theory. They appear in the study of Diophantine equations, algebraic curves, and the arithmetic of number fields. The ideal class group — which measures how far a ring is from having unique factorisation — is a central object in algebraic number theory, with connections to elliptic curves and the arithmetic of modular forms.

## Real-world applications

The algebraic number theory built on Dedekind domains is used in advanced [RSA variants](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) and lattice-based [cryptography](/applications/error-correcting-codes).
