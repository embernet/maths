## What it says

If allele $A$ has frequency $p$ and allele $a$ has frequency $q = 1-p$, then after one generation of random mating, the genotype frequencies are:

$$AA: p^2, \quad Aa: 2pq, \quad aa: q^2$$

These **Hardy–Weinberg proportions** are maintained in subsequent generations indefinitely, provided the population is large, mating is random, and there is no selection, mutation, or migration.

This is a consequence of the binomial theorem applied to allele sampling: each offspring independently inherits one allele from each parent, chosen at random.

## History

[Hardy](/mathematicians/hardy) proved this in a brief letter to *Science* in 1908, prompted by a question from the biologist Reginald Punnett. German physician Wilhelm Weinberg proved the same result independently the same year. Hardy reportedly found the problem trivial — he was a pure mathematician of the highest rank — but the result was not obvious to biologists.

## Why it matters

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is the null model of population genetics: deviations from HWE indicate that selection, non-random mating, or other forces are operating. It is used in forensic genetics (probability of a genotype occurring by chance), disease gene mapping, and population structure analysis.

## Real-world applications

[Epidemiological modelling](/applications/epidemiological-modelling) of heritable disease uses HWE as a baseline. [Population dynamics](/applications/population-dynamics-ecology) models use it to detect selection.
