## What it says

**Weak law (Bernoulli):** For i.i.d. random variables with mean $\mu$ and finite variance, the sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ converges *in probability* to $\mu$:

$$P\!\left(|\bar{X}_n - \mu| > \varepsilon\right) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty, \text{ for any } \varepsilon > 0$$

**Strong law (Kolmogorov):** The sample mean converges *almost surely* to $\mu$:

$$P\!\left(\lim_{n\to\infty} \bar{X}_n = \mu\right) = 1$$

The distinction matters: the weak law says the probability of being far off goes to zero; the strong law says you are almost certain to converge, trajectory by trajectory.

## History

Jacob [Bernoulli](/mathematicians/bernoulli-family) proved the weak law for the binomial distribution in *Ars Conjectandi* (published posthumously 1713) and called it his "golden theorem." He needed it to justify using observed frequencies as estimates of probability. The strong law waited until [Kolmogorov](/mathematicians/kolmogorov)'s rigorous foundations of probability (1933), which gave the tools to make "almost surely" precise.

## Why it matters

The law of large numbers is what makes probability a science rather than a philosophical game. Without it, there would be no connection between the abstract definition of probability and observed frequencies. The LLN justifies: taking averages of measurements to reduce noise, using sample statistics to estimate population parameters, and the entire enterprise of statistical inference. The Central Limit Theorem tells you *how fast* the convergence happens and what the fluctuations look like.

## Real-world applications

[Actuarial science](/applications/actuarial-science) relies on the LLN to predict aggregate claims from individual risk distributions. [Gambling](/applications/gambling-and-game-theory) — the house edge exists only in the long run because of the LLN. [Survey sampling](/applications/census-and-survey-statistics) and [epidemiology](/applications/epidemiological-modelling) estimate population means from samples.
