## What it says

**Ramsey's theorem (1930):** For any integers $r, s \geq 1$, there exists a smallest integer $R(r, s)$ such that any two-colouring of the edges of the complete graph $K_n$ with $n \geq R(r,s)$ contains either a red clique of size $r$ or a blue clique of size $s$.

The simplest non-trivial case: $R(3, 3) = 6$. Among any six people, either three mutually know each other or three are all strangers.

Known values: $R(3,3) = 6$, $R(3,4) = 9$, $R(3,5) = 14$, $R(4,4) = 18$. Even $R(5,5)$ is unknown; Erdős is reported to have said that if aliens threatened to destroy Earth unless humans computed $R(5,5)$, the right response would be to devote all available computers to the problem, but if they demanded $R(6,6)$ the right response would be to attack.

## History

Frank Ramsey proved the theorem in 1930. [Erdős](/mathematicians/erdos) and Szekeres independently reproved it in 1935 and Erdős then drove the field forward, using the [probabilistic method](/theorems/probabilistic-method-in-combinatorics) to show that $R(k,k) \geq 2^{k/2}$ — that Ramsey numbers grow exponentially.

## Why it matters

Ramsey theory says that any sufficiently large structure must contain a specified pattern. This "order out of chaos" principle pervades combinatorics, number theory (van der Waerden's theorem, Hales–Jewett theorem), and theoretical computer science. Computing Ramsey numbers is famously hard; most remain unknown.

## Real-world applications

Pattern-recognition in networks — [cybersecurity anomaly detection](/applications/cybersecurity-anomaly-detection) — uses Ramsey-type reasoning about the inevitability of certain structures in large graphs.
