## What it says

For any integer $n > 2$, the equation:

$$x^n + y^n = z^n$$

has no solution in positive integers $x$, $y$, $z$.

When $n = 2$ there are infinitely many solutions (Pythagorean triples: $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$, etc.). Fermat claimed the analogous statement fails for all higher powers.

## History

[Fermat](/mathematicians/fermat) (c. 1607–1665) wrote in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's *Arithmetica* that he had found a "marvellous proof" but the margin was too narrow to contain it. No proof was found for 358 years. Over that time, the problem stimulated enormous advances: Kummer's work on ideal numbers, developments in algebraic number theory, the theory of modular forms, and the Langlands programme.

Andrew Wiles proved the theorem in 1995, after seven years of secret work. His proof runs to over 100 pages and passes through elliptic curves, modular forms, and Galois representations — far beyond anything Fermat could have known. The consensus is that Fermat's "marvellous proof" did not exist in a correct form.

## Why it matters

Fermat's Last Theorem is famous because it is elementary to state and extraordinarily hard to prove. Its proof by Wiles is not mainly important for the theorem itself (which has few direct applications) but for the mathematics it generated and consolidated: the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture (now theorem) connecting elliptic curves and modular forms, which has sweeping consequences across algebraic geometry and number theory.

## Real-world applications

The surrounding mathematics — especially elliptic curves and modular arithmetic — underpins [RSA cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) and [digital signatures](/applications/digital-signatures), though the theorem itself is not directly used in these applications.
