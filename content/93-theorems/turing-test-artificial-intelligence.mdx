## What it says

In "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (1950), [Turing](/mathematicians/turing) proposed replacing the question "Can machines think?" with an operational test he called the **imitation game**. An interrogator communicates by written messages with two parties — one human, one machine — and must decide which is which. If a machine can perform indistinguishably from a human across a wide range of topics, Turing argued, we should attribute thought to it in the same sense we attribute thought to other humans.

Turing anticipated nine objections to machine intelligence and rebutted each. He predicted that by the year 2000, machines would be able to fool a human interrogator about 70% of the time after five minutes of conversation.

The test is not a mathematical theorem — it is a philosophical proposal about how to operationalise a concept that resists direct definition.

## History

Turing published the paper in the philosophical journal *Mind* in 1950, two years after being appointed reader at the University of Manchester. The paper introduced the phrase "artificial intelligence" into wide circulation and became the founding document of the field. Turing died in 1954; his vision of machine intelligence was largely vindicated in outline, though the Turing test itself has since been critiqued extensively (Searle's Chinese Room argument, Goedel-based objections, and others).

## Why it matters

The Turing test shifted the question of machine intelligence from metaphysics to engineering — a characteristically mathematical move: replace an unanswerable philosophical question with a measurable criterion. It defined the research agenda for artificial intelligence for decades and remains a touchstone in philosophy of mind. The Large Language Models developed from the 2010s onwards are the first systems to produce sustained, fluent conversation at human levels in restricted contexts.

## Real-world applications

[Artificial intelligence and machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) — the entire field — owes its framing to Turing's 1950 paper. Natural language processing, conversational agents, and benchmark evaluation methodology trace directly back to the imitation game.
