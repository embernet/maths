## What it says

The *Monadologie* (1714) presents Leibniz's mature metaphysics. The universe consists of **monads** — simple, immaterial, indivisible substances that have no windows (they do not causally interact with each other). Each monad contains within itself a complete representation of the entire universe from its own perspective. The apparent harmony between mind and body, and between different minds, is not causal but pre-established by God: the world is like two perfectly synchronised clocks that never touch.

Leibniz was motivated partly by the problem of Descartes's dualism — if mind and matter are categorically different, how do they interact? His solution: they don't. Apparent interaction is pre-established harmony.

## History

Leibniz developed monadology throughout his philosophical career and summarised it in this late text. It was never his main mathematical work but reflects the same drive towards systematisation and formal precision that characterised his mathematics.

## Why it matters

Monadology is one of the great systems of early modern philosophy. It has influenced debate in philosophy of mind, mereology (the study of parts and wholes), and theoretical computer science (the monad abstraction in functional programming — Haskell's IO monad — takes its name from Leibniz, though the connection is oblique).

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but the philosophical tradition from Leibniz to formal logic is part of the intellectual heritage of computer science.
