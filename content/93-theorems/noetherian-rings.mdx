## What it says

A ring $R$ is **Noetherian** if it satisfies the **ascending chain condition (ACC) on ideals**: every chain $I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots$ of ideals eventually stabilises ($I_n = I_{n+1} = \cdots$ for some $n$).

Equivalent conditions:
- Every ideal is finitely generated
- Every non-empty family of ideals has a maximal element (Zorn's lemma)

Examples: $\mathbb{Z}$, every field, polynomial rings $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ over a field (Hilbert basis theorem), rings of algebraic integers (Dedekind domains are Noetherian).

**Hilbert basis theorem:** If $R$ is Noetherian, so is $R[x]$.

## History

[Noether](/mathematicians/noether) introduced and studied Noetherian rings in her 1921 paper, naming them after properties she had identified. The term "Noetherian" was coined posthumously to honour her. [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert)'s basis theorem (1888) — that polynomial rings over fields are Noetherian — was one of the key precursors.

## Why it matters

The Noetherian condition is a finiteness condition that makes algebra tractable. In algebraic geometry, algebraic varieties over fields have coordinate rings that are Noetherian; this ensures every variety has finitely many irreducible components, algebraic maps have finite fibres, and algorithms for computing with varieties terminate. Without Noetherianness, many standard results fail.

## Real-world applications

Algebraic geometry over finite fields (used in [error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes)) requires Noetherian rings.
