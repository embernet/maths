## What it says

For vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in an inner-product space:

$$|\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle| \leq \|\mathbf{u}\|\, \|\mathbf{v}\|$$

with equality if and only if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are parallel (one is a scalar multiple of the other).

In $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the dot product this says $|\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}| \leq |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|$, which you can interpret geometrically: $|\cos\theta| \leq 1$. For integrals (the Schwarz version):

$$\left(\int_a^b f(x)g(x)\,dx\right)^2 \leq \int_a^b f(x)^2\,dx \cdot \int_a^b g(x)^2\,dx$$

## History

[Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy) proved the finite sum version in 1821. Viktor Bunyakovsky extended it to integrals in 1859, and Hermann Amandus Schwarz gave the general inner-product version in 1885. The inequality carries different names in different countries; in the UK "Cauchy–Schwarz" is standard.

## Why it matters

This is the bit that catches students out: the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality turns up in virtually every area of mathematics. It proves the triangle inequality for inner-product norms. It underlies the definition of angles in abstract vector spaces. It is the key step in proving that correlation coefficients lie between $-1$ and $1$. In quantum mechanics it implies the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. In Fourier analysis it bounds how well a signal and a filter can simultaneously be localised.

## Real-world applications

[Machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) uses cosine similarity (essentially normalised inner products) for document and embedding comparison — the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality guarantees that similarity scores are bounded. [Signal processing](/applications/signal-processing-jpeg-mp3) uses it in matched filter derivations. [Quantum computing](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms) uses it in the analysis of quantum states and measurement bounds.
