## What it says

Given a functional $J[y] = \int_a^b F(x, y, y')\,dx$, find the function $y(x)$ satisfying given boundary conditions that makes $J$ stationary. The necessary condition is the **Euler–Lagrange equation**:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = 0$$

This is a second-order ODE for $y(x)$. Solutions are the extremals — analogues of critical points for functions of a single variable.

## History

The brachistochrone problem (1696) prompted Jacob and Johann [Bernoulli](/mathematicians/bernoulli-family) to develop the foundations of the calculus of variations. Euler gave the general method (including the Euler–Lagrange equation) in 1744; [Lagrange](/mathematicians/lagrange) gave it the variational notation in 1755. The field has been expanding ever since.

## Why it matters

Lagrangian mechanics — the elegant reformulation of classical mechanics as finding the path of stationary action — is entirely built on the calculus of variations. [Noether's theorem](/theorems/noether-s-theorem-symmetry-and-conservation-laws-in-physics) (symmetries of the action correspond to conserved quantities) is a direct application. Optimal control theory, used in engineering and economics, is a modern extension. Minimal surface problems (soap films) and geodesics (shortest paths on curved surfaces) are classical variational problems.

## Real-world applications

[Robotics and control](/applications/robotics-and-control) uses optimal control theory, the modern form of the calculus of variations. [Aerodynamics](/applications/airfoil-and-aerodynamics) optimises shapes using variational methods. [Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) uses variational data assimilation.
