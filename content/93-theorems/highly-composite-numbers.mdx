## What it says

A **highly composite number** (HCN) is a positive integer $n$ such that $d(n) > d(k)$ for all positive integers $k < n$, where $d(n)$ is the number of divisors.

The first few: 1, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 120, 180, 240, 360, 720, 840, 1260, 1680, 2520, 5040, …

[Ramanujan](/mathematicians/ramanujan) proved that highly composite numbers have a specific form: $2^{a_1} 3^{a_2} 5^{a_3} \cdots p_k^{a_k}$ where $a_1 \geq a_2 \geq \cdots \geq a_k \geq 1$ (a product of consecutive primes with non-increasing exponents). He also introduced **superior highly composite numbers** (a stronger variant) and related them to the Riemann hypothesis.

## History

Ramanujan submitted his paper to the London Mathematical Society in 1915. The published version was shortened to save space during WWI; the full version was published only in 1997.

## Why it matters

Highly composite numbers are maximally divisible — they have the most factors of any number their size. This makes them useful in contexts where you want maximum flexibility in dividing quantities: 12 hours (12 = HCN with 6 divisors), 60 minutes (60 = HCN), 360 degrees. Ramanujan's analysis of their structure connects to deep results about the distribution of primes.

## Real-world applications

FFT algorithms perform best when the input size is a highly composite number — [signal processing](/applications/signal-processing-jpeg-mp3) and [data compression](/applications/data-compression) exploit this.
