## What it says

The **Erdős number** of a mathematician is defined recursively:
- Erdős himself has Erdős number 0
- Anyone who has co-authored a paper directly with Erdős has number 1
- Anyone who has co-authored with a number-1 mathematician (but not with Erdős directly) has number 2
- And so on

This is exactly the **graph distance** from Erdős in the co-authorship graph. The Erdős number is an instance of the "six degrees of separation" phenomenon: the mathematical collaboration network is a small-world graph in which most vertices are within a small number of hops from each other.

## History

The Erdős number was coined informally in the mathematical community, often attributed to Casper Goffman (1969). It became widely tracked after [Erdős](/mathematicians/erdos)'s death in 1996. The American Mathematical Society maintains an online database. Einstein, Erdős, and Turing all have finite Bacon numbers (collaborative distance in film) — an example of the small-world property.

## Why it matters

The Erdős number is a playful but genuinely instructive example of graph theory and network science in action. It demonstrates: the small-world phenomenon (most pairs of vertices are nearby), the power of hubs (high-degree nodes like Erdős reduce average distances dramatically), and the utility of graph-theoretic thinking for social networks. [PageRank](/applications/pagerank-google) and [recommender systems](/applications/recommender-systems) are more sophisticated versions of the same graph-distance reasoning.

## Real-world applications

The ideas in graph distance underpin [PageRank](/applications/pagerank-google) and [recommender system](/applications/recommender-systems) algorithms.
