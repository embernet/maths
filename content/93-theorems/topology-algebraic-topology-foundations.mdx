## What it says

[Poincaré](/mathematicians/poincare) introduced the **fundamental group** $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ in *Analysis Situs* (1895): the group of loops based at $x_0$ up to continuous deformation (homotopy). Two loops are the same if you can continuously deform one into the other.

He also developed **homology** — sequences of abelian groups $H_n(X)$ associated to a topological space, measuring $n$-dimensional holes. The **Euler characteristic** $\chi = V - E + F$ for polyhedra is $\sum_n (-1)^n \text{rank}(H_n)$.

**Homotopy equivalence:** two spaces are homotopy equivalent if there exist continuous maps between them that are "inverses up to homotopy." Algebraic invariants (fundamental group, homology groups) are preserved by homotopy equivalence.

## History

Poincaré launched algebraic topology in his 1895 paper and five supplements. He introduced the fundamental group and computed it for several surfaces. [Noether](/mathematicians/noether) later transformed Betti numbers into homology groups, giving the field its modern algebraic structure.

## Why it matters

Algebraic topology asks: what are the topological properties of spaces that are preserved under continuous deformation? The answer — holes of various dimensions, measured by homology groups; the structure of loops, measured by the fundamental group — gives powerful tools for distinguishing spaces that cannot be deformed into each other.

## Real-world applications

Persistent homology (applied algebraic topology) is used in [topological data analysis](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks). [Data compression](/applications/data-compression) uses topological features.
