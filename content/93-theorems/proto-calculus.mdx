## What it says

[Fermat](/mathematicians/fermat) developed a method he called "adequality" (adaequalitas) for finding maxima and minima. For a polynomial $f(x)$, to find a maximum, he set $f(x) = f(x + e)$ ("adequate" them), simplified, divided by $e$, then set $e = 0$. This gives what we now recognise as the condition $f'(x) = 0$.

For a tangent to a curve $f(x) = 0$ at a point $(x_0, y_0)$, he used a similar "doubling" technique. The conceptual step — letting a small quantity $e$ be nonzero during calculation, then setting it to zero at the end — is exactly what Newton and Leibniz systematised into calculus.

## History

Fermat (c. 1607–1665) developed these methods by around 1629–1636, in letters to Mersenne. He did not publish them (he published almost nothing in his lifetime), but they circulated and were known to Newton. Whether Newton was directly influenced is debated. Fermat also developed [analytic geometry](/theorems/analytic-geometry) independently of Descartes at about the same time.

## Why it matters

Fermat showed that optimisation problems — find the point where this function is largest — have algebraic solutions. This insight, that the derivative equals zero at extrema, is central to every optimisation problem in mathematics and science. He reached it thirty years before the formal invention of calculus.

## Real-world applications

Optimisation via setting derivatives to zero underlies [machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) (gradient descent), [option pricing](/applications/option-pricing-finance) (Black–Scholes derivation), and virtually all of applied mathematics.
