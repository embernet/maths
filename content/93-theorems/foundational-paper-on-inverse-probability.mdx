## What it says

[Bayes](/mathematicians/bayes) posed the inverse problem: given that $m$ successes and $n$ failures have been observed in a series of Bernoulli trials, what can be inferred about the probability $p$ of success?

He derived what we now call Bayes' theorem and worked out the posterior distribution over $p$ when the prior is uniform (flat) on $[0, 1]$. This is the **Beta distribution** as a posterior — the foundation of Bayesian parameter estimation.

The key conceptual move: probability is a measure of belief, and that belief can be updated systematically when data arrives. This is "inverse probability" — working from effect back to cause — as opposed to the "forward" calculation of how likely an outcome is given a known probability.

## History

[Bayes](/mathematicians/bayes) wrote the essay as a thought experiment; he seems to have been uncertain whether to publish it. His friend Richard Price found the manuscript after Bayes's death and submitted it to the Royal Society, where it was published in 1763. [Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace) independently derived the same results around 1774 and applied them far more broadly; much of what we call Bayesian statistics in practice derives from Laplace.

## Why it matters

This paper introduced the idea that probability can describe degrees of belief, not just frequencies of events — the cornerstone of Bayesian statistics. The distinction between Bayesian and frequentist approaches to probability still structures the debates in modern statistics and data science.
