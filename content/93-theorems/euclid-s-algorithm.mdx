## What it says

To find $\gcd(a, b)$ with $a \geq b > 0$, repeatedly replace $(a, b)$ with $(b, a \bmod b)$ until the remainder is zero. The last non-zero remainder is the GCD.

$$\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b,\, a \bmod b)$$

For example: $\gcd(252, 105)$. Divide: $252 = 2 \times 105 + 42$. Then $\gcd(105, 42)$: $105 = 2 \times 42 + 21$. Then $\gcd(42, 21)$: $42 = 2 \times 21 + 0$. Answer: $\gcd = 21$.

The algorithm terminates because the remainders strictly decrease; it is correct because dividing out common factors at each step preserves divisibility.

## History

[Euclid](/mathematicians/euclid) presented the algorithm in the *Elements* (Books VII and X, c. 300 BCE), though it may be older. It is widely considered the first non-trivial algorithm in recorded history. The name "algorithm" itself derives (much later, via Arabic scholarship) from [al-Khwarizmi](/mathematicians/al-khwarizmi), but the concept Euclid described is the direct ancestor of every systematic computational procedure.

## Why it matters

The algorithm is fast — the number of steps is proportional to the number of digits of the smaller input, not its magnitude. This efficiency made it practically invaluable long before computers existed. In modern number theory it is the gateway to Bézout's identity ($\gcd(a,b) = ma + nb$ for integers $m, n$), which in turn underpins the construction of modular inverses — the arithmetical heart of public-key cryptography.

## Real-world applications

Every time you visit a secure website, [RSA public-key cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) is running under the hood, and computing modular inverses via the extended Euclidean algorithm is a key step. The same mechanism appears in [digital signatures](/applications/digital-signatures) and [error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes).
