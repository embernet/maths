## What it says

The *Elements* is not a single theorem but a complete axiomatic system. [Euclid](/mathematicians/euclid) takes five postulates — including the famous parallel postulate — and derives 465 propositions in geometry, number theory, and the theory of proportions. Everything follows from the postulates by logical deduction alone. No appeal to diagrams or intuition is allowed to substitute for proof.

The five postulates in modern paraphrase:
1. Any two points can be joined by a straight line segment.
2. A line segment can be extended indefinitely.
3. A circle can be drawn with any centre and any radius.
4. All right angles are equal.
5. **(The parallel postulate)** If a line crossing two others makes the interior angles on one side sum to less than two right angles, the two lines meet on that side.

## History

[Euclid](/mathematicians/euclid) compiled the *Elements* around 300 BCE in Alexandria. It is the second most-printed book in history after the Bible, and remained the primary geometry textbook in Western schools until the twentieth century. The parallel postulate always felt different from the others — more like a theorem than an axiom. Centuries of attempts to prove it from the first four failed, and in the nineteenth century [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss), Bolyai, and Lobachevsky independently showed why: you can replace it with its negation and get consistent non-Euclidean geometries.

## Why it matters

The *Elements* established the axiomatic method: start from a small set of self-evident assumptions, define your terms precisely, and derive everything else by logic. That template — axioms, definitions, propositions, proofs — is what mathematics still looks like. [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert)'s programme in the early twentieth century was essentially an attempt to extend Euclid's approach to all of mathematics.

The *Elements* also contains early number theory: the infinitude of primes (Book IX, Proposition 20), the algorithm for greatest common divisors ([Euclid's algorithm](/theorems/euclid-s-algorithm)), and results on perfect numbers.

## Real-world applications

Euclidean geometry underpins [structural engineering](/applications/bridge-and-structural-engineering), surveying, and [3D graphics](/applications/computer-graphics-3d). More abstractly, the axiomatic method the *Elements* pioneered is the foundation of every formal verification system used in software and hardware design today.

## Where you'll meet it

The *Elements* is the backdrop to the [Classical Greece era](/history/02-classical-greece). Its methods appear in [logic and proof](/theorems/proof-by-contradiction) from your first term, and its geometry is revisited when you study [Riemannian geometry](/theorems/riemannian-geometry) and [topology](/theorems/topology-algebraic-topology-foundations) in later years.
