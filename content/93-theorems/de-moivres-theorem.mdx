        For any real $\theta$ and integer $n$:

$$
(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta).
$$

Combined with Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, this is the modern restatement: $e^{in\theta} = (e^{i\theta})^n$.

## History

Abraham de Moivre — a French Huguenot refugee in London — discovered the result in 1707, long before [Euler](/mathematicians/euler) put the complex exponential at the centre of analysis. De Moivre made his living tutoring and computing astronomical tables, and famously predicted the date of his own death from his observation that he was sleeping an extra fifteen minutes per day.

## Why it matters

It converts a hard trigonometric calculation (powers of $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$) into an easy arithmetic one (multiply the angle by $n$). The formulas for $\cos(n\theta)$ and $\sin(n\theta)$ as polynomials in $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ — the Chebyshev polynomials — fall out as the real and imaginary parts.

## Where you'll meet it

In your first complex-numbers course.
