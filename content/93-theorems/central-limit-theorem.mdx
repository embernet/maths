## What it says

Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots, X_n$ be independent identically distributed random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$. Define the sample mean $\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i$. Then:

$$\sqrt{n}\,\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty$$

The distribution of the standardised sample mean converges to the standard normal, regardless of the shape of the original distribution. You do not need the $X_i$ to be Gaussian — any distribution with finite variance will do.

## History

Abraham de Moivre proved a special case for coin flips in 1733. Laplace extended it and began to understand its generality. The fully rigorous modern version was proved by Lyapunov (1901) and Lindeberg (1922) — [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss)'s name is attached through his fundamental role in establishing the normal distribution and least-squares theory that the CLT justifies.

## Why it matters

The CLT is the reason Gaussian statistics work in so many settings. Whenever you measure something that is itself the result of many small additive contributions — noise in a measurement, the height of a population, the return on a diversified portfolio — the CLT says the distribution will be approximately Gaussian. This justifies the ubiquity of $z$-tests, $t$-tests, confidence intervals, and almost everything in classical frequentist statistics.

The proof, in its clean form using [Fourier transforms](/theorems/fourier-transform) of characteristic functions, is a beautiful application of analysis to probability.

## Real-world applications

[Survey statistics](/applications/census-and-survey-statistics) and [clinical trials](/applications/clinical-trials-design) rely on CLT-derived standard errors. [Actuarial science](/applications/actuarial-science) uses it to model aggregate risk. [Option pricing](/applications/option-pricing-finance) assumes log-normal returns, justified partly by CLT-type arguments. [Epidemiological modelling](/applications/epidemiological-modelling) uses Gaussian approximations for large populations.
