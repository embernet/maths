## What it says

Before [Noether](/mathematicians/noether), topologists worked with **Betti numbers** — integer invariants counting independent cycles in different dimensions. Noether recognised that these numbers are the **ranks of abelian groups** (the homology groups $H_n(X)$), and that the groups themselves, not just their ranks, carry more information.

The chain complex $(C_n, \partial_n)$ — with boundary maps $\partial_n: C_n \to C_{n-1}$ satisfying $\partial_{n-1} \circ \partial_n = 0$ — gives homology groups $H_n = \ker\partial_n / \text{im}\, \partial_{n+1}$. This is now the standard framework.

## History

Noether presented this perspective in informal discussions in Göttingen in the early 1920s. Her student Heinz Hopf and contemporaries Alexandrov and Vietoris adopted it. The algebraic approach to topology — using chain complexes, exact sequences, and functors — became the standard by the 1930s and defines algebraic topology to this day.

## Why it matters

The shift from Betti numbers to homology groups is a perfect example of the structural approach: by identifying the right algebraic objects, a whole new level of understanding becomes possible. Exact sequences, long exact sequences of pairs, the Mayer–Vietoris sequence — all the tools of modern algebraic topology depend on the group structure that Noether insisted on.

## Real-world applications

Persistent homology — a variant of homology theory — is used in [topological data analysis](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks) for machine learning on complex datasets. [Data compression](/applications/data-compression) can use topological features.
