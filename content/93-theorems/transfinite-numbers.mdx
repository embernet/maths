## What it says

Cantor introduced two types of transfinite numbers:

**Ordinals** measure the order-type of a well-ordered set. After the finite ordinals $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ comes $\omega$ (the order-type of $\mathbb{N}$), then $\omega + 1$, $\omega \cdot 2$, $\omega^2$, $\omega^\omega$, $\varepsilon_0$, and beyond.

**Cardinals** measure the size (cardinality) of a set. The cardinal of $\mathbb{N}$ is $\aleph_0$; the cardinal of $\mathbb{R}$ is $\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}$. The Continuum Hypothesis asks whether $\mathfrak{c} = \aleph_1$ (the next cardinal after $\aleph_0$) — a question shown independent of ZFC.

The relationship between cardinals and ordinals is given by the von Neumann ordinal construction and the axiom of choice.

## History

[Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) introduced ordinal numbers in 1883 and developed the full theory of cardinals and ordinals through the 1890s. The theory was philosophically contentious but mathematically precise. Hilbert called it "the finest product of mathematical genius and one of the supreme achievements of purely intellectual human activity." Its independence results (Gödel 1938, Cohen 1963) showed the theory's deep subtlety.

## Why it matters

Transfinite numbers make precise the informal notion that "some infinities are bigger than others." They are essential for the theory of well-ordered sets, transfinite induction, and the axiom of choice. In set theory, ordinals are used to construct the universe of sets $V_\alpha$ by transfinite recursion.

## Real-world applications

No direct engineering applications. The theory sets the mathematical foundation for all of formal logic and the theory of computation.
