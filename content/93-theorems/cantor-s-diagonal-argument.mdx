## What it says

Suppose you could list all real numbers between 0 and 1 as an infinite sequence $r_1, r_2, r_3, \ldots$. Write each in decimal:

$$r_1 = 0.d_{11}d_{12}d_{13}\ldots$$
$$r_2 = 0.d_{21}d_{22}d_{23}\ldots$$
$$r_3 = 0.d_{31}d_{32}d_{33}\ldots$$

Now construct a number $d = 0.d_1 d_2 d_3 \ldots$ by choosing each digit $d_n$ to differ from $d_{nn}$ (the diagonal digit of $r_n$). Then $d$ differs from every $r_n$ in the list — in the $n$th decimal place. So $d$ is a real number not in the list. Contradiction: no such list can be complete.

Therefore $\mathbb{R}$ is **uncountable**: no bijection from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ exists.

## History

[Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) published the diagonal argument in 1891, though he had proved the uncountability of $\mathbb{R}$ by a different method in 1874. The diagonal version is cleaner, more general, and has proved extraordinarily reusable: [Gödel](/mathematicians/godel) used a similar self-referential construction in his incompleteness theorems, and [Turing](/mathematicians/turing) used it to prove the undecidability of the halting problem.

## Why it matters

The diagonal argument is one of the great proof techniques in mathematics. It shows that infinity comes in different sizes — a shocking idea in 1891 — and establishes that there are strictly more real numbers than integers, even though both sets are infinite. This opened the entire theory of cardinality and transfinite numbers.

The reusability of the diagonal method makes it a template: whenever you need to show that a proposed enumeration must miss something, look for a diagonal construction.

## Real-world applications

The undecidability results that the diagonal argument spawned — via Gödel and Turing — set fundamental limits on what any computer can compute. [Quantum computing](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms) must work within these limits.
