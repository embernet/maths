## What it says

The cubic formula solves $x^3 + px + q = 0$ (the depressed cubic) by:

$$x = \sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{q^2}{4} + \frac{p^3}{27}}} + \sqrt[3]{-\frac{q}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{q^2}{4} + \frac{p^3}{27}}}$$

The quartic formula (due to Cardano's student Ferrari) reduces degree-4 equations to a cubic. Both formulas express the roots using $+, -, \times, \div$, and cube/fourth roots — the same operations as the quadratic formula, but far more complex.

## History

[Cardano](/mathematicians/cardano) published both formulas in *Ars Magna* (1545). The cubic formula had been discovered by Scipione del Ferro around 1510 and kept secret; Niccolò Tartaglia rediscovered it and shared it with Cardano under an oath of secrecy. Cardano broke the oath when he learnt del Ferro had priority, provoking a bitter dispute with Tartaglia. The quartic was solved by Ferrari and first published here. *Ars Magna* is a landmark in the history of algebra — the first great modern mathematical text in Latin, moving decisively towards symbolic reasoning.

## Why it matters

The cubic and quartic formulas showed that polynomial equations of degrees 3 and 4 are solvable by radicals, as the quadratic had been for centuries. This made the degree-5 problem the next obvious challenge — and its eventual impossibility (Abel, 1826) required entirely new mathematics. Along the way, Cardano noticed that the cubic formula sometimes requires taking square roots of negative numbers even when the final answer is real — forcing mathematicians to begin taking complex numbers seriously.

## Real-world applications

No direct modern applications — numerical solvers are used in practice — but the story drove the development of [Galois theory](/theorems/galois-theory) and abstract algebra.
