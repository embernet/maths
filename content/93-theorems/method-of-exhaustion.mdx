## What it says

To find the area enclosed by a curve, inscribe a polygon of $n$ sides, compute its area $A_n$, and show that as $n$ increases the gap between $A_n$ and the true area $A$ can be made as small as desired. The proof structure is by double contradiction: assume $A > A_n + \varepsilon$; derive a contradiction; assume $A < A_n - \varepsilon$; derive another contradiction. Therefore $A = $ limit of $A_n$.

This is not a limit in the modern sense (limits had not been invented), but it achieves the same conclusion by a purely logical route.

## History

Eudoxus of Cnidus (c. 370 BCE) developed the method; [Euclid](/mathematicians/euclid) used it in the *Elements*; but [Archimedes](/mathematicians/archimedes) deployed it with greatest virtuosity — using it to compute the area of parabolic segments, the volume of spheres, and the surface area of cones and cylinders. His *Method* (rediscovered in 1906 on a palimpsest) shows he first found his results by a heuristic "mechanical" method, then proved them rigorously by exhaustion.

## Why it matters

The method of exhaustion is the conceptual forerunner of the Riemann integral. Both approximate a smooth region by simple shapes and take a limit. The key insight — that you can bound an unknown area from above and below, and squeeze the bounds together — is the same insight that underlies all of integration theory. Archimedes was, in this sense, two thousand years ahead of his time.

## Real-world applications

Numerical integration methods (Simpson's rule, Gaussian quadrature) are modern implementations of the same basic idea: approximate a region by simpler pieces.
