## What it says

$\sqrt{2}$ is **irrational**: it cannot be written as $p/q$ with $p, q$ integers.

**Proof by contradiction:** Suppose $\sqrt{2} = p/q$ in lowest terms. Then $2q^2 = p^2$, so $p^2$ is even, so $p$ is even ($p = 2k$). Then $4k^2 = 2q^2$, so $q^2 = 2k^2$, so $q$ is even. But then $p$ and $q$ are both even, contradicting the assumption that $p/q$ is in lowest terms.

More generally: $\sqrt{n}$ is irrational whenever $n$ is not a perfect square. The proof generalises.

## History

The discovery is attributed to the Pythagorean school, traditionally around 450 BCE. Legend says the proof was discovered by Hippasus of Metapontum, who was supposedly drowned at sea by his fellow Pythagoreans for the heresy of discovering incommensurable magnitudes. The story is almost certainly apocryphal, but the mathematical result was certainly troubling to a school that believed "all is number" (meaning rational number).

## Why it matters

The discovery of irrational numbers was the first crisis in the foundations of mathematics. The Greeks had assumed all geometric magnitudes were commensurable — expressible as ratios of integers. $\sqrt{2}$ (the diagonal of a unit square) refuted this. It forced the development of a more careful theory of proportion (Eudoxus, Book V of Euclid's *Elements*) and eventually the construction of the real number line by [Dedekind](/mathematicians/dedekind).

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but the real number system built to accommodate irrationals is the foundation of all of calculus and analysis.
