Pick a positive integer $n$. **Euler's totient function** $\varphi(n)$ counts how many integers between $1$ and $n$ share no common factor with $n$ — equivalently, how many invertible elements there are in modular arithmetic modulo $n$.

If $p$ is prime, every number from $1$ to $p-1$ is coprime to $p$, so $\varphi(p) = p - 1$. For products of distinct primes $\varphi$ is multiplicative:

$$
\varphi(pq) = (p-1)(q-1) \qquad \text{when $p, q$ are distinct primes.}
$$

More generally,

$$
\varphi(n) = n \prod_{p \mid n}\left(1 - \frac{1}{p}\right).
$$

The key result attached to $\varphi$ is **Euler's theorem**: if $a$ is coprime to $n$, then

$$
a^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}.
$$

When $n$ is prime this reduces to [Fermat's little theorem](/theorems/fermat-s-little-theorem). Euler's theorem is the more general statement and the more useful one for cryptography.

## History

[Leonhard Euler](/mathematicians/euler) introduced the totient in a paper of 1763 and proved his generalisation of Fermat's little theorem the same year. The notation $\varphi$ was Gauss's, introduced in *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801). For nearly two centuries afterwards the function lived a quiet life inside pure number theory.

## Why it matters

Knowing $\varphi(n)$ tells you the order of the group of units modulo $n$ — and orders of groups are the starting point of group theory. It also gives you the key identity that makes [RSA](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) work: a message $m$ encrypted with public exponent $e$ and decrypted with private exponent $d$ returns to itself because $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$.

## Real-world applications

Every TLS handshake on the web, every SSH connection, every signed software update relies on RSA — and RSA relies on $\varphi$. The security of the scheme rests on the fact that $\varphi(pq)$ is easy to compute *if* you know $p$ and $q$, and extremely hard if you do not.

## Where you'll meet it

In abstract algebra (the order of the group of units mod $n$), in elementary number theory, and again in any advanced course on cryptography.
