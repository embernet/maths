## What it says

[Archimedes](/mathematicians/archimedes) showed that the area of a parabolic segment (the region bounded by a parabola and a chord) is $\frac{4}{3}$ times the area of the inscribed triangle.

His proof sums the geometric series:
$$1 + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{64} + \cdots = \frac{4}{3}$$

Each term is $\frac{1}{4}$ of the previous (since each new triangle has $\frac{1}{4}$ the area of the previous one). Archimedes summed this using a "proof by exhaustion" argument rather than the modern formula $\sum_{k=0}^\infty r^k = \frac{1}{1-r}$, but the structure is identical.

## History

Archimedes described this in his treatise *Quadrature of the Parabola*, one of seventeen surviving works. The method of exhaustion — proving a result by showing that any deviation leads to a contradiction — was the Greek substitute for limits. Archimedes pushed it further than anyone before or after him in antiquity. His letter to Eratosthenes (the *Method*) reveals he often found results by a heuristic "mechanical method" first, then proved them rigorously by exhaustion.

## Why it matters

This is the earliest known use of an infinite series to compute a definite quantity. The modern theory of integration is, at one level, precisely what Archimedes was doing: approximating area by sums of simpler shapes and taking a limit. Archimedes lacked the language of limits and functions, but the conceptual step — sum an infinite series, get a finite answer — was his.

## Real-world applications

No direct modern applications, but the method of exhaustion is the conceptual ancestor of the [Riemann integral](/theorems/riemann-integral) and numerical integration methods used everywhere in science and engineering.
