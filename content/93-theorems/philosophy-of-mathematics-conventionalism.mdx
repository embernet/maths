## What it says

[Poincaré](/mathematicians/poincare) argued in *La Science et l'Hypothèse* (1902) that the axioms of geometry are not truths about physical space but **conventions** — choices we make for their convenience and utility. If physical experiments showed that Euclidean geometry failed (e.g., the angles of a large triangle do not sum to 180°), we would not abandon Euclidean geometry but instead modify the physics (e.g., attribute the discrepancy to curved light paths).

He also argued that the continuum of real numbers, the rules of arithmetic, and other mathematical structures are free creations that are not derived from experience, though experience guides our choices.

## History

Poincaré wrote *La Science et l'Hypothèse* as popular philosophy and it sold widely. His conventionalism influenced the logical empiricists of the Vienna Circle and Einstein. The discovery that general relativity actually does use non-Euclidean geometry was, by Poincaré's logic, a change of physical theory, not a refutation of Euclidean geometry as pure mathematics.

## Why it matters

The philosophy of mathematics asks: what are mathematical objects, and what do mathematical truths mean? Poincaré's answer — that mathematical structures are free inventions, convenient frameworks rather than discovered truths — remains a significant position in the debate between Platonism (mathematics is discovered) and formalism/conventionalism (mathematics is invented).

## Real-world applications

No direct applications, but the question of what mathematics is matters for how it is taught and used.
