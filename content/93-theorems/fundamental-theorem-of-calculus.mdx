There are two halves, and once you see them together you understand why calculus is a single subject rather than two.

**The first fundamental theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then the area function

$$
F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt
$$

is differentiable, and $F'(x) = f(x)$.

**The second fundamental theorem.** If $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ on $[a,b]$ (any function with $F' = f$), then

$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).
$$

Together they say: differentiation undoes integration, and integration undoes differentiation. The two operations — slope of a curve, area under a curve — are opposite sides of the same coin.

## History

Glimpses of the theorem appear in seventeenth-century work by Isaac Barrow (Newton's teacher) and James Gregory, but the unified statement and its power were the great achievement of [Isaac Newton](/mathematicians/newton) and [Gottfried Leibniz](/mathematicians/leibniz) in the 1660s–1680s — discovered independently and in different notations. The bitter priority dispute that followed split European mathematics for a century. The rigorous proof, with continuous functions and a precise notion of integral, came much later — Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave the modern argument in 1823, and Bernhard Riemann's definition of the integral (1854) made the whole theorem fully precise.

## Why it matters

The FTC turns area calculation — historically a brutally hard problem requiring inscribed polygons and limit arguments — into a routine of antidifferentiation. It lets you exchange a hard integral for a search for an antiderivative, which is often easy. It underlies every change-of-variable, every integration by parts, and every numerical scheme that breaks an integral into local slopes.

## Real-world applications

Every domain that adds up a continuous quantity uses the FTC implicitly. [Climate models](/applications/climate-modelling) integrate fluxes over time and space; the [discrete cosine transform](/applications/signal-processing-jpeg-mp3) inside JPEG and MP3 is integration's continuous cousin made discrete; [Black–Scholes option pricing](/applications/option-pricing-finance) hinges on an integral representation of expected payoff.

## Where you'll meet it

It is the climax of your first calculus course and the foundation of your second-year analysis course, where Riemann's rigorous proof is at last available to you.
