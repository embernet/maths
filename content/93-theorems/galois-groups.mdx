## What it says

Given a polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ with roots $\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$ in $\mathbb{C}$, the **splitting field** $K = \mathbb{Q}(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)$ is the smallest field containing all roots. The **Galois group** $\text{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ consists of all field automorphisms $\sigma: K \to K$ that fix $\mathbb{Q}$ pointwise.

These automorphisms permute the roots among themselves, so $\text{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ is a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_n$.

The **Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory:** There is a bijective correspondence between subgroups of $\text{Gal}(K/\mathbb{Q})$ and intermediate fields $\mathbb{Q} \subseteq F \subseteq K$, reversing inclusion. Normal subgroups correspond to Galois (normal) extensions.

## History

[Galois](/mathematicians/galois) developed the theory in 1830–32. The Galois group precisely encodes the symmetries of the roots — the obstruction to writing them by radicals is captured entirely in the group's structure.

## Why it matters

The Galois correspondence is a deep duality between algebra (groups) and algebra (fields). It is the prototype for the correspondences in modern algebraic geometry (the étale fundamental group), number theory (absolute Galois groups and the Langlands programme), and topology (fundamental groups and covering spaces).

## Real-world applications

Galois fields $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}$ — which arise from this theory — are the algebraic substrate of [error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes) and [quantum computing algorithms](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms).
