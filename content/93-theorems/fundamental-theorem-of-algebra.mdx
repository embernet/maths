## What it says

Every polynomial $p(z) = a_n z^n + a_{n-1} z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$ of degree $n \geq 1$ with coefficients in $\mathbb{C}$ has exactly $n$ roots in $\mathbb{C}$ (counted with multiplicity).

Equivalently: $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed. You cannot escape $\mathbb{C}$ by taking roots of polynomials.

A degree-$n$ polynomial factors completely as $a_n(z - z_1)(z - z_2) \cdots (z - z_n)$ over $\mathbb{C}$.

## History

The theorem was widely believed before it was proved. [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) gave the first rigorous proof in his 1799 doctoral dissertation, later improving it. His proof is essentially topological: he showed that the image of a large circle under a polynomial wraps around the origin, and used the intermediate value theorem to conclude a root must exist inside. Argand, Laplace, and d'Alembert had earlier incomplete attempts. The modern proof using [Cauchy's theorem](/theorems/cauchys-integral-theorem) — if $p(z)$ had no root, $1/p(z)$ would be entire, and a contour integral argument gives a contradiction — is slick and elegant.

## Why it matters

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra is why complex numbers are "complete" in a sense the reals are not. Over $\mathbb{R}$, the polynomial $x^2 + 1$ has no roots. Over $\mathbb{C}$, every polynomial has as many roots as its degree. This completeness is what makes complex analysis so powerful — there are no missing roots to worry about, and partial fraction decompositions always work. It also explains why [Cardano's](/mathematicians/cardano) formula for the cubic must sometimes pass through complex numbers even when all three roots are real (the *casus irreducibilis*).

## Real-world applications

[Signal processing](/applications/signal-processing-jpeg-mp3) works with rational transfer functions, factored over $\mathbb{C}$ into poles and zeros — the FTA guarantees this factorisation is always possible. [Option pricing](/applications/option-pricing-finance) involves characteristic functions that are polynomials in the complex plane.
