## What it says

The **Laplace equation** is:

$$\nabla^2 u = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2} = 0$$

Solutions are **harmonic functions**. They satisfy the mean value property: the value at any point equals the average over any sphere centred there. Harmonic functions are infinitely differentiable and cannot have interior maxima or minima (maximum principle) — the extrema occur on the boundary.

The **Poisson equation** $\nabla^2 u = f$ has a source term $f$ and generalises to include non-zero charge/mass density.

## History

[Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace) derived the equation in the 1780s while studying gravitational potentials. It is also a consequence of [Gauss's law](/theorems/gauss-s-law-electrostatics) in regions with no charge. The equation appears in potential theory, complex analysis (harmonic = real part of holomorphic), and conformal mapping.

## Why it matters

Laplace's equation is the paradigm for elliptic PDEs. Unlike the heat equation (parabolic) or wave equation (hyperbolic), its solutions are determined entirely by boundary data — no initial conditions needed. This makes it the correct model for equilibrium states: steady-state temperature, electrostatic potential, and the velocity potential of irrotational flow.

## Real-world applications

[Seismic imaging](/applications/seismic-imaging-oil-exploration) uses potential theory (Laplace-type equations) for subsurface reconstruction. [Structural engineering](/applications/bridge-and-structural-engineering) uses the Airy stress function (satisfying the biharmonic equation $\nabla^4 u = 0$). [Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) uses the Laplace equation for geopotential height.
