        The fundamental equation of the calculus of variations. Among all curves $y(x)$ joining two fixed points, the one that minimises (or extremises) the functional $\int_a^b L(x, y, y')\,dx$ satisfies

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = 0.
$$

## History

[Euler](/mathematicians/euler) derived a geometric form in the 1740s; the young [Lagrange](/mathematicians/lagrange) sent Euler a more elegant variational derivation in 1755, and Euler, generously, withdrew his own paper until Lagrange's was published. The result became the basis of analytical mechanics.

## Why it matters

Lagrangian mechanics rewrites Newton's laws as the Euler-Lagrange equation for the action integral $\int L\,dt$. The form is invariant under any change of coordinates — which is why physicists love it. The same machinery underlies general relativity, gauge theory and modern field theory.

## Real-world applications

Trajectory optimisation in [robotics](/applications/robotics-and-control), shape optimisation in engineering, and every variational PDE in [climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) reduces to Euler-Lagrange equations.

## Where you'll meet it

A first encounter in second-year multivariable calculus; full treatment in third-year mechanics or PDE courses.
