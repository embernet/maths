## What it says

For any prime $p$ and integer $a$ with $\gcd(a, p) = 1$:

$$a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$$

Equivalently, $a^p \equiv a \pmod{p}$ for all integers $a$.

In plain language: raise any integer not divisible by $p$ to the $(p-1)$th power, and you get a multiple of $p$ plus 1. For example, $2^6 = 64 = 63 + 1 = 9 \times 7 + 1$, and indeed $64 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$.

The modern proof uses group theory: the integers $\{1, 2, \ldots, p-1\}$ form a group under multiplication mod $p$ of order $p-1$, and by Lagrange's theorem every element's order divides $p-1$, so $a^{p-1} = e = 1$ in the group.

## History

[Fermat](/mathematicians/fermat) stated the theorem in a letter to Frénicle de Bessy in 1640 without proof. Euler gave the first published proof in 1736 and generalised it to composite moduli via the Euler totient function (see [Euler's totient function](/theorems/euler-s-totient-function)). Leibniz had an unpublished proof around the same time as Fermat, showing that independent discovery was possible.

## Why it matters

Fermat's little theorem is the direct ancestor of the RSA cryptosystem. RSA encryption relies on the fact that computing $a^e \pmod{n}$ is easy, while recovering $a$ from $a^e \pmod{n}$ without knowing the prime factorisation of $n$ is computationally infeasible. The correctness proof for RSA decryption uses Euler's generalisation of Fermat's theorem. Every secure website using RSA certificates owes a debt to a 1640 observation.
