## What it says

Classical invariant theory studied polynomial expressions in the coefficients of binary forms (homogeneous polynomials in two variables) that remain unchanged under linear transformations. The central question: is the ring of invariants finitely generated?

[Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert) proved in 1888 that it is — every invariant ring is finitely generated. His proof was an existence argument (using what is now called the Hilbert basis theorem: every ideal in a polynomial ring is finitely generated) rather than a constructive one. The classicist Gordan said "this is not mathematics, this is theology" — meaning it was non-constructive. Hilbert responded by providing a constructive proof in 1893.

**Hilbert's basis theorem:** If $R$ is a Noetherian ring, then $R[x]$ is Noetherian. In particular, $\mathbb{Q}[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is Noetherian — all ideals are finitely generated.

## History

Gordan had proved finite generation for binary forms by explicit computation. Hilbert's 1888 proof settled the general case abstractly and in the process invented a new style of algebra. It forced the mathematical community to accept non-constructive existence proofs as legitimate mathematics.

## Why it matters

Invariant theory is one of the roots of modern algebraic geometry and representation theory. The Hilbert basis theorem is a foundational result in commutative algebra. The concept of invariants — quantities preserved by symmetry — is central throughout mathematics and physics (e.g., [Noether's theorem](/theorems/noether-s-theorem-symmetry-and-conservation-laws-in-physics)).

## Real-world applications

Invariants of geometric objects are used in [3D computer graphics](/applications/computer-graphics-3d) for shape recognition and geometric queries.
