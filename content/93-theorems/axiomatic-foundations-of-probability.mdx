## What it says

A **probability space** consists of:
- A sample space $\Omega$ (the set of all possible outcomes)
- A $\sigma$-algebra $\mathcal{F}$ of events (subsets of $\Omega$ closed under complement and countable union)
- A probability measure $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$

satisfying the **Kolmogorov axioms**:
1. $P(A) \geq 0$ for all $A \in \mathcal{F}$
2. $P(\Omega) = 1$
3. For disjoint events $A_1, A_2, \ldots$: $P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(A_i)$

From these three axioms, all of probability theory — conditional probability, independence, expectations, distributions, limit theorems — is derived.

## History

[Kolmogorov](/mathematicians/kolmogorov) published *Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung* in 1933, at age 30. Before this, probability was a collection of useful techniques without a rigorous foundation — different mathematicians used different definitions and the subject was not clearly a branch of mathematics. Kolmogorov grounded it in measure theory (which Lebesgue had developed for integration), giving probability a clean axiomatic base. The [strong law of large numbers](/theorems/law-of-large-numbers) and the central limit theorem were immediately expressible and provable with full rigour.

## Why it matters

Kolmogorov's axioms are the reason you can prove things *about* probability rather than merely compute with it. Measure-theoretic probability handles continuous distributions, infinite sequences of events, and limit theorems in a unified framework. It is the prerequisite for stochastic processes, Brownian motion, and the mathematical theory of finance — all of which require careful handling of uncountably many events.
