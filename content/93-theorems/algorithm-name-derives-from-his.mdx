## What it says

An **algorithm** is a finite, deterministic, step-by-step procedure that solves a class of problems. The word derives from *Algoritmi* — the twelfth-century Latin transliteration of al-Khwarizmi's name — via his treatise on arithmetic with Hindu-Arabic numerals.

Al-Khwarizmi's arithmetic treatise described systematic procedures for addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division using the positional decimal system — procedures that were far more efficient than Roman numeral arithmetic and could be taught systematically, like a recipe, to anyone.

## History

[Al-Khwarizmi](/mathematicians/al-khwarizmi) wrote his arithmetic treatise around 820 CE. When translated into Latin in the twelfth century, the opening phrase "Dixit Algoritmi..." ("Thus spoke al-Khwarizmi...") led medieval European scholars to call any systematic arithmetic procedure an *algoritmus*. By the time the word entered modern English as "algorithm," it had broadened to mean any systematic computational procedure. [Euclid's algorithm](/theorems/euclid-s-algorithm) and [Turing machines](/theorems/turing-machines-theory-of-computation) are two landmarks in the history of the concept.

## Why it matters

The idea of an algorithm — a procedure that mechanically solves a problem class — is the foundation of computer science. Every sorting routine, every search engine ranking, every machine learning training loop is an algorithm. The conceptual lineage from al-Khwarizmi's arithmetic recipes to modern algorithms is direct and intentional: the word itself records the intellectual debt.
