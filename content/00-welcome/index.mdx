Mathematics is one of the oldest and most far-reaching things people do, and it hangs together as a single connected subject in a way that is easy to lose sight of when you meet its pieces one at a time. This guide is a map of that whole landscape — written in plain language, for anyone who wants to see how the parts connect: whether you use mathematics in your work, explore it as a hobby, are studying it formally, or are simply curious.

It assumes only school-level mathematics as a starting point. From there it builds up, in the order that lets each idea rest on the ones before it.

## From calculating to understanding

There is a shift that happens as mathematics deepens: from *doing* it to *understanding* it. Earlier mathematics mostly asks you to find the answer. Mathematics at this level asks something more — to show *why* an answer is correct, in a way that holds for every case without exception, as a chain of reasoning that leaves no gaps. That activity is **proof**, and learning to read and write proofs is the real threshold of the subject. Much of this guide is about what lies beyond it.

## How the material is organised

The topics are arranged in four broad levels, each building on the one before:

**Foundations** — the four pillars: algebra and linear algebra; real analysis (the rigorous treatment of limits and continuity); calculus and its methods (extending to several variables, vectors, and differential equations); and probability and statistics. This is also where proof itself is introduced.

**Core mathematics** goes deeper into each strand and introduces new ones: abstract algebra, metric spaces and rigorous integration, complex analysis (one of the most beautiful parts of the subject), partial differential equations, statistical inference, and a first taste of numerical computing.

**Specialist areas** are where the subject fans out — Galois theory, functional analysis, measure theory, topology, fluid dynamics, mathematical biology, mathematical finance, statistics and data science. They represent the full breadth of modern mathematics.

**Advanced topics** reach toward the research frontier: algebraic geometry, analytic number theory, advanced PDEs, and the deep connections between mathematics and physics.

This progression mirrors the order a typical university curriculum follows — a well-tested way to see how the subject fits together — but nothing here assumes you are following one. See the [About page](/98-about) for more on how, and why, the material is arranged this way.

## How to use this guide

Each topic page covers one area: what it is, where it came from, and why it matters, with worked examples and key definitions. It is a companion, not a textbook — a way to orientate yourself before you meet a topic, and to see the connections between topics that are easy to miss when you encounter them separately.

The [history section](/90-history) traces nine eras of mathematical development, from Babylonian arithmetic to twenty-first-century breakthroughs. The [mathematician profiles](/91-mathematicians) give short portraits of the people behind the results. The [resources page](/92-resources) lists books and online courses worth your time.

A note on notation: mathematical expressions here are typeset with LaTeX. If the notation is unfamiliar, you will pick it up quickly.

Welcome. However you have arrived at mathematics, there is a great deal here to explore.
