You have spent two years — perhaps more — learning a very effective version of mathematics. Differentiation, integration, complex numbers, matrices, mechanics, statistics: the A-level curriculum is genuinely impressive. You are good at this.

University mathematics is going to feel different. Not harder in the same way a harder A-level question feels harder. Different in kind.

The shift is from *doing* mathematics to *understanding* it. A-level asked you to find the answer. A degree asks you to prove the answer is correct — and to prove it in a way that holds for all cases, without exception, with a logical chain of reasoning that leaves no gaps. This is not a superficial change of emphasis. It is a different activity.

This guide is a map of what you are about to meet. It covers, in plain language, the topics that appear in each year of a standard UK mathematics undergraduate degree, the mathematicians who created them, and a brief history of how the subject developed from ancient clay tablets to twenty-first-century research frontiers. You can read it before term begins, dip in whenever you encounter an unfamiliar term in lectures, or use it alongside your notes throughout the year.

## How the degree is structured

A three-year mathematics degree is typically structured like this:

**Year One** builds the foundations. You will meet four broad areas: algebra and linear algebra; real analysis (the rigorous treatment of limits and continuity); calculus and its methods (extending to multiple variables, vectors, and differential equations); and probability and statistics. These are not independent subjects — they constantly refer to each other. Year One is also where you learn proof: how to construct a logical argument, how to read a definition, and why the phrase "it seems obvious" is not acceptable in mathematics.

**Year Two** goes deeper into each strand and introduces new ones. Abstract algebra takes the concrete groups you met in Year One and reveals the general theory. Real analysis becomes metric spaces and rigorous integration. Complex analysis — functions of a complex variable — is one of the most beautiful parts of the entire degree. You will also encounter partial differential equations, statistical inference, and your first taste of numerical computing.

**Year Three** is where you choose your path. Typically twenty-seven or more optional units are available. You might go deep into algebra (Galois theory, representation theory), into analysis (functional analysis, measure theory, topology), into applied mathematics (fluid dynamics, mathematical biology, mathematical finance), or into statistics and data science. The shape of your degree in Year Three reflects who you are as a mathematician.

**Year Four** exists on four-year integrated master's programmes. It operates at a different level — closer to research. Units are assessed at masters standard; many students take a year-long research project supervised by a department member.

## What to expect in your first weeks

The first lecture of your degree will almost certainly introduce you to mathematical proof. You will be shown the statement of a theorem — perhaps something familiar like the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ — and then walked through a proof by contradiction. If you have done Further Mathematics at A-level, you may have seen this before. What will be new is the *demand* for this standard in everything you do.

By Week Three, most students have encountered their first genuinely difficult proof — something where the path from hypotheses to conclusion is not immediately visible, and where several previous definitions need to be deployed simultaneously. This is normal. It is not a sign that you have chosen the wrong degree.

The other thing that strikes many students early on is the sheer number of new definitions. A definition in mathematics is not a vague description; it is a precise statement that specifies exactly which objects fall into a category and which do not. Learning to read definitions carefully — to ask what each clause is doing and why it cannot be relaxed — is one of the most important skills you will develop.

## How to use this guide

Each topic page covers one area of the curriculum, typically at the level of "what this is, where it came from, and why it matters." There are worked examples and key definitions, but this guide is not a textbook. It does not replace attending lectures, attempting problem sheets, or reading the recommended texts. It is a companion: a way to orientate yourself before you arrive at a new topic, and a way to see the connections between topics that a lecture sequence necessarily spreads across weeks.

The [history section](/90-history) traces nine eras of mathematical development, from Babylonian arithmetic through to twenty-first-century breakthroughs. The [mathematician profiles](/91-mathematicians) give short portraits of the people behind the results you will study. The [resources page](/92-resources) lists books and online courses that students have found genuinely useful.

A note on notation: mathematical expressions in this guide are typeset using LaTeX. You will encounter this notation constantly in lecture notes and textbooks; if you are not already familiar with it, you will pick it up quickly.

Welcome to the degree. It will be one of the most demanding and most rewarding things you will ever do.
