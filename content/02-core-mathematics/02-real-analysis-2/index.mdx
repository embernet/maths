The analysis you met in foundational topics was analysis on the real line. Every definition involved $|x - y|$ as the measure of distance between points. core mathematics analysis asks: what if you replace $|x - y|$ with a more general notion of distance? What remains true, and what breaks?

This is the theory of **metric spaces** — and the answer turns out to be: almost everything you proved in foundational topics remains true, with $|x - y|$ replaced by $d(x, y)$. That is the power of abstraction: prove it once in the right generality, and it applies everywhere.

## Metric spaces

A **metric space** is a set $X$ together with a function $d : X \times X \to [0, \infty)$ satisfying:
1. $d(x, y) = 0$ if and only if $x = y$.
2. $d(x, y) = d(y, x)$ (symmetry).
3. $d(x, z) \le d(x, y) + d(y, z)$ (triangle inequality).

The most important examples:

- **Euclidean space** $(\mathbb{R}^n, d_2)$: $d_2(x,y) = \|x - y\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum (x_i - y_i)^2}$.
- **Function spaces**: the set $C([0,1])$ of continuous functions on $[0,1]$ with $d_\infty(f,g) = \sup_{x \in [0,1]} |f(x) - g(x)|$ is a metric space.
- **Discrete metric**: $d(x,y) = 1$ if $x \ne y$, $0$ if $x = y$.
- **$p$-adic metric** on $\mathbb{Q}$: a different notion of "closeness" used in number theory.

An **open ball** $B(x, r) = \{y \in X : d(x,y) < r\}$ generalises the interval $(x-r, x+r)$ in $\mathbb{R}$. A set $U \subseteq X$ is **open** if every point of $U$ is the centre of some open ball contained in $U$.

## Convergence and completeness in metric spaces

A sequence $(x_n)$ in a metric space $(X, d)$ **converges** to $x$ if $d(x_n, x) \to 0$, i.e., for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ such that $d(x_n, x) < \varepsilon$ for all $n > N$.

A **Cauchy sequence** satisfies $d(x_m, x_n) \to 0$ as $m, n \to \infty$. A metric space is **complete** if every Cauchy sequence converges. Completeness is the key property: $\mathbb{R}$ is complete; $\mathbb{Q}$ is not. The function space $C([0,1])$ with the sup metric is complete — this is the basis for the Banach fixed-point theorem.

## Compactness and the Heine-Borel theorem

A metric space $K$ is **compact** if every open cover has a finite sub-cover (the Heine-Borel-Lebesgue definition). In $\mathbb{R}^n$, this is equivalent (by the **Heine-Borel theorem**) to being closed and bounded. In general metric spaces, this equivalence fails: you need the notion of **sequentially compact** (every sequence has a convergent subsequence).

Compact sets behave extremely nicely: a continuous function on a compact metric space is uniformly continuous and bounded, and attains its maximum and minimum. These are the general forms of the theorems you met in foundational topics.

## Uniform convergence

This is the point where core mathematics analysis departs most importantly from foundational topics.

You already know what it means for a sequence of functions $(f_n)$ to **converge pointwise** to $f$: for each fixed $x$, $f_n(x) \to f(x)$. The trouble is that pointwise convergence is too weak: a sequence of continuous functions can converge pointwise to a discontinuous limit; a sequence of differentiable functions can converge to a non-differentiable limit; the integral $\int_a^b f_n(x)\, dx$ need not converge to $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$.

**Uniform convergence** is the fix: $(f_n)$ **converges uniformly** to $f$ on $[a,b]$ if

$$\sup_{x \in [a,b]} |f_n(x) - f(x)| \to 0.$$

The $N$ in the convergence condition works for all $x$ simultaneously — it does not depend on $x$. This is the difference: pointwise says $\forall x, \forall \varepsilon, \exists N(x, \varepsilon)$; uniform says $\forall \varepsilon, \exists N(\varepsilon), \forall x$.

The **Weierstrass M-test** provides a convenient sufficient condition for uniform convergence of series: if $|f_n(x)| \le M_n$ for all $x$ and $\sum M_n < \infty$, then $\sum f_n$ converges uniformly.

**Theorem**: the uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. This is the reason uniform convergence matters: it preserves analytically important properties that pointwise convergence does not.

[Weierstrass's](/mathematicians/weierstrass) famous example of a continuous nowhere-differentiable function was constructed using a uniformly convergent series of "tooth-like" functions — each term smooth, but the limit pathological.

## The Riemann integral, made rigorous

You have probably been computing integrals for years. What does $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$ actually mean? The answer, due to [Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann), uses **upper and lower sums**.

For a bounded function $f : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ and a partition $P = \{a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b\}$, define:

$$U(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n M_i (x_i - x_{i-1}), \quad L(f, P) = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i (x_i - x_{i-1})$$

where $M_i = \sup_{[x_{i-1}, x_i]} f$ and $m_i = \inf_{[x_{i-1}, x_i]} f$. The function $f$ is **Riemann integrable** if $\inf_P U(f,P) = \sup_P L(f,P)$, and the common value is $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$.

Every continuous function on $[a,b]$ is Riemann integrable. But there are bounded functions that are not: the function that is 1 on rationals and 0 on irrationals (Dirichlet's function) has upper sum 1 and lower sum 0 for every partition. This is the starting point for Lebesgue integration (specialist areas).

The **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus** — both parts — can now be stated and proved rigorously. Part I: if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\, dt$, then $F' = f$. Part II: $\int_a^b F'(x)\, dx = F(b) - F(a)$.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: the Banach fixed-point theorem — proved in this module — guarantees that iterative algorithms converge, underpinning everything from [robotics control loops](/applications/robotics-and-control) to the Picard iteration for ODEs. The completeness of function spaces is what makes [numerical PDE solvers](/03-specialist-areas/05-numerical-analysis) well-posed; without it, you could not prove that finite-element approximations converge to the true solution. Uniform convergence is the condition that separates convergent neural network approximations from divergent ones in [deep learning theory](/applications/machine-learning-deep-neural-networks).

## Where this leads

Metric spaces and completeness are the entry point to functional analysis (specialist areas): the study of infinite-dimensional spaces of functions. The Banach fixed-point theorem uses completeness to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to differential equations. The theory of compact operators on Hilbert spaces — the mathematics of quantum mechanics — extends compactness from finite to infinite dimensions. Every step of that staircase starts here.
