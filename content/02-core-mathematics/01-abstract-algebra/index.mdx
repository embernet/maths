You have encountered groups before, even if you did not call them that. The integers under addition, the non-zero rationals under multiplication, the invertible $n \times n$ matrices under matrix multiplication, the rotations and reflections of an equilateral triangle — all of these are groups. Abstract algebra asks: what do they have in common? What can be proved from the common structure alone, without mentioning any specific example?

## Groups

A **group** $(G, *)$ is a set $G$ with a binary operation $*$ satisfying:
1. **Closure**: $a * b \in G$ for all $a, b \in G$.
2. **Associativity**: $(a * b) * c = a * (b * c)$ for all $a, b, c \in G$.
3. **Identity**: there exists $e \in G$ such that $e * a = a * e = a$ for all $a$.
4. **Inverses**: for every $a \in G$ there exists $a^{-1} \in G$ with $a * a^{-1} = e$.

If additionally $a * b = b * a$ for all $a, b$, the group is **abelian** (commutative). Named after [Niels Henrik Abel](/mathematicians/abel).

Groups come in two flavours: **finite** (with $|G|$ elements, called the *order*) and **infinite** (like $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition). Finite group theory centres on understanding all finite groups up to isomorphism — a project that was completed only in 2004 with the Classification of Finite Simple Groups, one of the great achievements in the history of mathematics.

**Subgroups** are subsets of $G$ that are themselves groups under the same operation. A crucial shortcut: a non-empty subset $H \le G$ is a subgroup if and only if $a * b^{-1} \in H$ whenever $a, b \in H$.

## Lagrange's theorem and cosets

If $H$ is a subgroup of a finite group $G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$. This is **Lagrange's theorem**, and its proof is elegant: partition $G$ into **left cosets** $aH = \{ah : h \in H\}$, observe that each coset has $|H|$ elements and any two cosets are either identical or disjoint, so $G$ splits into $[G:H] = |G|/|H|$ cosets of equal size.

An immediate consequence: the order of any element $g \in G$ (the smallest $n$ such that $g^n = e$) divides $|G|$. So if $|G| = p$ is prime, $G$ must be cyclic — every non-identity element generates the whole group.

## Normal subgroups and quotient groups

A subgroup $N \le G$ is **normal** if $gNg^{-1} = N$ for all $g \in G$ (equivalently, left cosets equal right cosets). When $N$ is normal, the set of cosets $G/N = \{gN : g \in G\}$ forms a group under $(aN)(bN) = (ab)N$ — this is the **quotient group** of $G$ by $N$.

A **group homomorphism** is a map $\phi : G \to H$ respecting the group structure: $\phi(ab) = \phi(a)\phi(b)$. The **kernel** $\ker\phi = \{g \in G : \phi(g) = e_H\}$ is always a normal subgroup of $G$; the **image** $\text{im}\,\phi$ is a subgroup of $H$.

The **First Isomorphism Theorem** — attributed to [Emmy Noether](/mathematicians/noether) who gave it its abstract formulation — states:

$$G / \ker\phi \cong \text{im}\,\phi.$$

This is the group-theory analogue of the rank-nullity theorem. It says the quotient of $G$ by "the bit that gets destroyed by $\phi$" is isomorphic to "the bit of $H$ that $\phi$ reaches."

## Cayley's theorem and permutation groups

Arthur Cayley's theorem: every group $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the symmetric group $S_{|G|}$ (the group of all permutations of a set of $|G|$ elements). In other words, every abstract group can be realised concretely as a group of permutations.

The **symmetric group** $S_n$ (permutations of $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$) and its normal subgroup $A_n$ (the **alternating group**, even permutations) are the building blocks of finite group theory. The simplicity of $A_n$ for $n \ge 5$ — the fact that $A_n$ has no proper non-trivial normal subgroups — is what makes the quintic equation unsolvable by radicals, as [Galois](/mathematicians/galois) showed.

## Rings and fields

A **ring** $(R, +, \cdot)$ is a set with two operations: addition (making $R$ an abelian group) and multiplication (associative, distributive over addition). If multiplication is also commutative and every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse, the ring is a **field**.

Examples: $\mathbb{Z}$ is a ring (not a field, since $2$ has no integer inverse); $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$ are fields; $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ is a field when $p$ is prime.

**Ideals** are the ring-theory analogue of normal subgroups: a subset $I \subseteq R$ is an ideal if it is a subgroup under addition and $rI \subseteq I$ for all $r \in R$. Quotient rings $R/I$ are defined analogously to quotient groups.

The key structure theorems for rings mirror those for groups: the isomorphism theorems, the correspondence between ideals and quotient rings, the classification of principal ideal domains.

**Polynomial rings** $\mathbb{F}[x]$ over a field $\mathbb{F}$ behave remarkably like the integers: there is a division algorithm, a Euclidean algorithm, and a notion of prime (irreducible) polynomials. The **Eisenstein irreducibility criterion** is a powerful test: $p(x) = a_n x^n + \cdots + a_0 \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ if there is a prime $q$ dividing $a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_{n-1}$ but not $a_n$, with $q^2$ not dividing $a_0$.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [Error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes) — used in QR codes, CDs, and deep-space communication — are built from Reed-Solomon codes whose construction requires the theory of finite fields and polynomial rings introduced here. [RSA cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) works in the multiplicative group modulo $n$; its security depends on the group-theoretic structure of $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. [Quantum computing algorithms](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms) exploit Fourier analysis over cyclic groups — the hidden subgroup framework that unifies Shor's and other quantum algorithms is abstract algebra over groups.

## Why abstract algebra matters

This material is not abstraction for its own sake. Groups classify all symmetries: the symmetry groups of crystals predict the 230 crystallographic space groups and constrain the physical properties of materials. In quantum mechanics, the standard model of particle physics is classified by representations of certain Lie groups ($SU(3) \times SU(2) \times U(1)$). In coding theory, the algebraic structure of certain field extensions underpins Reed-Solomon codes, used in CDs, DVDs, and QR codes.

In specialist areas, Galois theory uses the interplay between field extensions and group theory to answer the deepest question in classical algebra: which polynomial equations can be solved by radicals?
