Symbols stick better when you know their stories — and the stories are good: full of rivalry, accident, wit, and the occasional act of vandalism. (The definitive source for most of what follows is Florian Cajori's wonderful 1928–29 *A History of Mathematical Notations*, still the field's standard reference.)

## Plus, minus, and the warehouse (1489)

The earliest printed $+$ and $-$ appear in Johannes Widmann's 1489 book on *commercial* arithmetic, where they marked **surplus and deficit** — chests of goods over or under their nominal weight. Symbols born in a warehouse ledger ended up at the foundation of all mathematics; the subject has never been embarrassed about its mercantile childhood.

## The parallel lines of Robert Recorde (1557)

Recorde — a Welsh physician and mathematics writer — grew tired of ending every line of algebra with the words "is equal to". In *The Whetstone of Witte* he proposed a remedy: *"a paire of paralleles… bicause noe 2 thynges can be moare equalle."* The equals sign is thus one of the few symbols with a stated design rationale — it is a tiny **picture of its own meaning**. Recorde died in a debtors' prison the following year, never knowing he had written one of the most-used characters in human history.

## Descartes casts the alphabet (1637)

[Descartes](/mathematicians/descartes)' *La Géométrie* set two conventions we still obey: **unknowns from the end of the alphabet** ($x$, $y$, $z$), knowns from the beginning ($a$, $b$, $c$), and superscripts for powers ($x^3$). Why is $x$ *the* unknown? The romantic explanations are legion; the honest answer is that nobody is certain — Descartes used all three end-letters and $x$ simply won. Sometimes a convention has no deeper story than habit hardening into law.

## The infinite lazy eight (1655)

John Wallis introduced $\infty$ without a word of explanation. Scholars' best guesses: a worn Roman numeral for 1,000 (written CIƆ), or a closed-up final omega. The honest summary — **nobody knows** — is somehow fitting for the symbol of the unending.

## One day in October (1675)

On **29 October 1675**, in a Paris manuscript, [Leibniz](/mathematicians/leibniz) first stretched the letter S — for *summa*, "sum" — into $\int$, and within weeks had paired it with $dx$. He was a notation designer of genius who *tested* his symbols in correspondence before committing to them. His great rival [Newton](/mathematicians/newton) had the calculus earlier but wrote it with terse dots ($\dot{y}$), and after their priority war, British loyalty to the dots became a patriotic duty — and a scientific handicap. A century later, Cambridge's young Analytical Society (including Charles Babbage, later of computing fame) campaigned to adopt Leibniz's $d$-notation, mocking their own university's stubbornness as *"the principles of pure D-ism in opposition to the Dot-age of the University"* — possibly the finest pun ever to change a curriculum.

## Euler, the one-man standards committee

More of our notation traces to [Euler](/mathematicians/euler) than to anyone else: he introduced $e$ (using it from the late 1720s, first in print 1736 — and almost certainly *not* named after himself), $f(x)$ (1734), $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$ (1777), and $\Sigma$ for sums (1755), and his adoption of $\pi$ — first used for the circle constant by the Welsh schoolmaster William Jones in 1706 — made it universal. Euler wrote so much, so clearly, that his choices simply *became* mathematics' house style. Laplace's advice to students was blunt: *"Read Euler, read Euler, he is the master of us all."*

## Gauss's clockwork congruence (1801)

[Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) opened his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* — written at twenty-one — by defining $a \equiv b \pmod n$. The triple bar deliberately echoes $=$: congruence *behaves* like equality (you can add and multiply congruences), and the notation makes modular arithmetic feel as natural as ordinary arithmetic. Two centuries later that "clock arithmetic" runs the [RSA cryptography](/applications/rsa-public-key-cryptography) protecting your bank login — with Gauss's notation unchanged.

## The bridge Hamilton vandalised (1843)

On 16 October 1843, walking along Dublin's Royal Canal, [Hamilton](/mathematicians/hamilton) saw in a flash how to multiply in four dimensions — and, unable to resist, **carved the rules into the stone of Broom Bridge**: $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$. The carving is long gone (a plaque remains, and mathematicians re-walk the route every 16 October), but the notation shrapnel is everywhere: our $\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}$ basis vectors, the dot and cross products, even the word *vector* — all salvage from quaternions.

## The harp called nabla

Hamilton also toyed with a rotated-delta symbol, and Maxwell and Tait put $\nabla$ to work in physics. It needed a name. The scholar William Robertson Smith suggested **"nabla"** — the Greek word for a Phoenician **harp** of roughly that shape. Maxwell found this hilarious and addressed letters to Tait as the *"Chief Musician upon Nabla"*. The joke stuck; the name became standard; and physicists have been playing the harp ($\nabla$, $\nabla\cdot$, $\nabla\times$) ever since.

## New alphabets for new infinities

When [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) discovered that infinities come in different sizes, he decided so radical an idea deserved a fresh alphabet — and reached for Hebrew: $\aleph_0, \aleph_1, \ldots$ Meanwhile the logicians were coining their own kit: Peano bent an epsilon into $\in$ (abbreviating *est*, Latin for "is", 1889) and reversed an E into $\exists$ (1897); Gentzen inverted an A into $\forall$ (1935). And the symbol for the empty set, $\varnothing$, was chosen by André Weil of the Bourbaki collective — not a Greek phi but the **Norwegian letter Ø**, imported by a mathematician who happened to know Scandinavian alphabets. Mathematics takes its symbols wherever it finds them.

## Chalk, type, and code

Three quieter revolutions shaped how symbols look today:

- **Blackboard bold.** Printers set $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{Z}$ as plain bold — but you can't write bold in chalk, so lecturers doubled a stroke. The improvised chalk form became so universal that print now imitates the blackboard.
- **The tombstone.** The little $\blacksquare$ ending proofs replaced the Latin *Q.E.D.*; Paul Halmos borrowed it from magazine typesetting, where it marked the end of an article, and it is now called the **Halmos**.
- **Notation becomes software.** In 1977 Donald Knuth, appalled by the ugly typesetting of his own books, paused his life's work to build **TeX** — the program (via LaTeX) that now typesets essentially all mathematics, including every formula on this site. Around the same lineage, Kenneth Iverson's programming language APL gave mathematics back the floor and ceiling brackets $\lfloor x \rfloor, \lceil x \rceil$. The five-century project of notation design hasn't ended; it has just moved into Unicode tables, programming languages, and proof assistants like [Lean](/96-lean4/01-introduction), where a symbol is no longer ink but executable meaning.

Every one of these marks was an invention — argued for, mocked, refined, adopted. Whatever mathematics you go on to read, somewhere under the symbols there is a person, a quarrel, and usually a good story.
