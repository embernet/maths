Classical PDE theory requires that solutions are differentiable — you can only insert a function into $-\Delta u = f$ if $u$ has second derivatives in the classical sense. But many natural problems have data (boundary conditions, forcing terms) that are not smooth, and insisting on classical solutions excludes important physical situations.

The modern resolution is to expand the class of "solutions" to include **weak solutions** — functions that satisfy the PDE in an integral (distributional) sense. This requires the theory of **Sobolev spaces** and functional analytic tools.

## Weak derivatives and Sobolev spaces

A function $u \in L^2(\Omega)$ has a **weak partial derivative** $\partial u / \partial x_i$ if there exists $v \in L^2(\Omega)$ such that for all smooth test functions $\phi$ with compact support:

$$\int_\Omega u\, \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x_i}\, dx = -\int_\Omega v\, \phi\, dx.$$

This is the integration-by-parts formula turned into a definition: $v$ behaves as a derivative of $u$ "in the sense of distributions."

The **Sobolev space** $H^k(\Omega) = W^{k,2}(\Omega)$ consists of functions in $L^2(\Omega)$ whose weak derivatives up to order $k$ are also in $L^2(\Omega)$, with inner product:

$$\langle u, v \rangle_{H^k} = \sum_{|\alpha| \le k} \int_\Omega D^\alpha u\, D^\alpha v\, dx.$$

These are Hilbert spaces. The space $H^1_0(\Omega)$ (functions in $H^1$ that vanish on $\partial \Omega$) is the natural space for solutions of elliptic PDEs with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions.

## Weak formulations and the Lax-Milgram theorem

Consider the elliptic PDE $-\Delta u = f$ on $\Omega$ with $u = 0$ on $\partial\Omega$. Multiply by a test function $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$ and integrate:

$$\int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\, dx = \int_\Omega fv\, dx.$$

This is the **weak formulation**: find $u \in H^1_0(\Omega)$ such that $a(u,v) = F(v)$ for all $v \in H^1_0(\Omega)$, where $a(u,v) = \int \nabla u \cdot \nabla v\, dx$ and $F(v) = \int fv\, dx$.

The **Lax-Milgram theorem**: if $a(\cdot,\cdot)$ is a bounded, coercive bilinear form on a Hilbert space $H$, and $F$ is a bounded linear functional, then there exists a unique $u \in H$ with $a(u,v) = F(v)$ for all $v$.

Coercivity for the Laplacian follows from **Poincaré's inequality**: $\|u\|_{L^2}^2 \le C\|\nabla u\|_{L^2}^2$ for $u \in H^1_0(\Omega)$. This bounds the full $H^1$ norm by the gradient alone.

## Elliptic regularity

A weak solution is merely in $H^1_0$. Is it actually smooth? **Elliptic regularity theory** answers: if $f \in H^k(\Omega)$ and $\partial\Omega$ is sufficiently smooth, then $u \in H^{k+2}(\Omega)$. The solution is "two derivatives more regular than the data" — a hallmark of elliptic PDEs.

In the limit: if $f$ is smooth ($f \in C^\infty$), then $u \in C^\infty(\Omega)$ (interior regularity). This is a striking result: a function defined merely as a minimiser of an integral functional is automatically infinitely differentiable.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) uses finite element and finite volume discretisations of atmospheric PDEs whose mathematical foundations are precisely the weak formulations and Lax-Milgram theory in this course; without the elliptic regularity results, there would be no rigorous justification that the discretised model is solving the right equation. [Seismic imaging for oil and gas exploration](/applications/seismic-imaging-oil-exploration) inverts acoustic wave equations (a hyperbolic PDE) to reconstruct subsurface structure from surface measurements — an inverse problem whose well-posedness rests on the PDE theory here. [Bridge and structural engineering](/applications/bridge-and-structural-engineering) simulates stress and deformation using finite element analysis; the Céa's lemma error bound you prove in this course is what justifies trusting those simulations when lives depend on them.

## Connection to finite element methods

The weak formulation is also the starting point for numerical computation. Restrict to a finite-dimensional subspace $V_h \subset H^1_0$ (piecewise polynomial functions on a mesh) and solve $a(u_h, v_h) = F(v_h)$ for all $v_h \in V_h$. This is the **Galerkin approximation**, the mathematical core of the finite element method.

**Céa's lemma** gives the best possible error bound: $\|u - u_h\|_{H^1} \le C \inf_{v_h \in V_h} \|u - v_h\|_{H^1}$. The FEM solution is quasi-optimal: up to the constant $C$, it is as good as the best approximation from $V_h$. Mesh refinement and higher-order elements improve the approximation, with convergence rates controlled by Sobolev embedding theorems.
