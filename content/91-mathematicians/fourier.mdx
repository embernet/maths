Fourier had one of the more eventful lives among mathematicians. Orphaned at nine, educated by Benedictines, he narrowly avoided the guillotine during the French Revolutionary Terror (reportedly reprieved at the last moment when the administrator who signed his arrest warrant was himself arrested). He accompanied Napoleon to Egypt as a scientific secretary, helped compile the *Description de l'Égypte*, and was appointed prefect of the Isère department in Grenoble, where he began his mathematical work on heat conduction.

His 1807 memoir on the theory of heat proposed that any function — including piecewise functions with jumps, sawtooth waves, anything — could be expressed as a sum of sines and cosines:

$$f(x) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty (a_n \cos nx + b_n \sin nx).$$

The claim was too strong for the referees. [Lagrange](/mathematicians/lagrange) and [Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace) rejected the paper; Lagrange in particular objected that a convergent trigonometric series cannot represent a discontinuous function. He was wrong, as it turned out — but this was not fully understood until the work of Dirichlet and Riemann decades later.

Fourier's mathematics turned out to be correct and profoundly important. Fourier series are used to solve the heat equation, the wave equation, and Laplace's equation. The Fourier transform — the continuous generalisation — underlies signal processing, quantum mechanics, image compression (JPEG), and spectroscopy. Every digital audio file, every X-ray scan, every radio transmission, every MP3 and JPEG uses ideas originating in Fourier's theory of heat conduction.

He died in 1830, reportedly having permanently damaged his health in Egypt and Grenoble by insisting on extremely high room temperatures, having developed a conviction (perhaps from Egypt) that dry heat was beneficial to health.
