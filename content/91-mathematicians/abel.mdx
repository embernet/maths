Abel's life is one of the most poignant in the history of mathematics: extraordinary achievement compressed into six years of adult work, ended by tuberculosis at 26, largely unrecognised during his lifetime, celebrated immediately after.

His great early result — published in 1824, when he was 21, in a pamphlet he printed himself at his own expense and distributed to prominent mathematicians (including [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss), who reportedly did not read it) — was the proof that **the general polynomial equation of degree five or higher cannot be solved by radicals**. Quadratics, cubics, and quartics all have solution formulae involving only arithmetic operations and root extraction. The quintic $x^5 + px + q = 0$ does not, and Abel proved it.

This result settled a question that had been open since the sixteenth century, when Cardano had found the cubic formula. [Galois](/mathematicians/galois) would later explain *why* at a deeper level — through what is now called Galois theory — but Abel proved the fact first, in 1824, several years before Galois developed his framework between 1830 and 1832.

Abel then turned to elliptic integrals — integrals of the form $\int R(x, \sqrt{P(x)})\, dx$ where $P$ is a polynomial of degree 3 or 4. These resist evaluation by elementary functions, but Abel discovered their inversion (what are now called elliptic functions) had remarkable properties, including double periodicity in the complex plane. His *Recherches sur les fonctions elliptiques* (1827) inaugurated the modern theory of elliptic functions, which [Jacobi](/mathematicians/jacobi) would develop independently and in parallel.

In 1826, he submitted a major memoir on elliptic integrals to the Paris Academy. [Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy), as one of the referees, mislaid it. The paper was found intact in Paris in 1830, after Abel had died. The Academy eventually awarded him the Grand Prize for Mathematics — posthumously, shared with Jacobi.

**Abelian groups** — commutative groups, in which $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all elements — are named in his honour, though the term was attached to this concept after his death.
