Euler is, by any measure of volume, the most prolific mathematician in history: his collected works fill over 70 printed volumes and are still not completely published. He contributed to virtually every area of mathematics known in the eighteenth century: number theory, analysis, algebra, geometry, mechanics, optics, and music theory.

Among his most famous results: the formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, proved by comparing power series. Setting $\theta = \pi$ gives $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ — a relation connecting the five most important constants in mathematics. Euler did not consider this a single "identity" to marvel at; for him it was a routine consequence of the power series definitions.

He popularised the notation $\pi$ for the ratio of circumference to diameter (the symbol had been introduced by William Jones in 1706; Euler's consistent use from 1737 standardised it), established $e$ as the base of natural logarithms, introduced $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$, $\Sigma$ for summation, and $f(x)$ for a function applied to an argument — a notational revolution that standardised mathematical communication.

His 1736 paper on the Königsberg bridge problem — seven bridges connecting four landmasses; can you cross each exactly once and return to the start? — is the founding document of graph theory. The answer (no, because vertices with odd degree cannot be intermediate in such a walk) introduced the concept of graph connectivity.

In 1771, Euler lost the sight in his remaining eye. He continued his mathematical work by dictating to assistants and performing increasingly complex calculations in his head. He produced roughly half of his total output during the years of complete blindness. His capacity for mental arithmetic was legendary. He died in 1783 while calculating the orbit of Uranus — reportedly stopping mid-sentence.
