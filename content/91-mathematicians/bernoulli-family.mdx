The Bernoullis were a Basel merchant family who produced, over three generations, at least eight significant mathematicians. The most important were Jacob (1655–1705), his brother Johann (1667–1748), and Johann's son Daniel (1700–1782).

**Jacob Bernoulli** studied the calculus immediately after Leibniz published it in 1684 and became one of its most skilled early practitioners. He discovered the Bernoulli numbers (appearing in the Taylor series of trigonometric functions and in Euler's work on the zeta function), proved the Law of Large Numbers (the first rigorous result in probability), and formulated the brachistochrone problem: find the curve along which a bead slides from one point to another in minimum time under gravity. He died before seeing the answer.

**Johann Bernoulli** solved the brachistochrone problem (the cycloid) and issued it as a challenge to mathematicians across Europe in 1696. Newton solved it overnight, according to legend. Johann extended the calculus substantially and taught it to the young Euler, among others. He also published L'Hôpital's rule — a rule he had sold to the Marquis de L'Hôpital for a monthly stipend, in one of the less glorious transactions in mathematical history.

**Daniel Bernoulli** derived the equation $\frac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + p + \rho gz = \text{const}$ along a streamline in 1738 — Bernoulli's equation, the foundation of fluid dynamics. He also developed the kinetic theory of gases (relating pressure and temperature to molecular motion) decades before this was generally accepted. His father Johann, embittered by his son's superior reputation, back-dated his own *Hydraulica* to before Daniel's *Hydrodynamica* and had Daniel expelled from the family home. Family dynamics notwithstanding, the name Bernoulli appears throughout the degree.

Jacob's tombstone in Basel, per his request, bears a logarithmic spiral with the motto *Eadem mutata resurgo* ("Though changed, I shall arise the same"). The stonemason carved an Archimedean spiral instead — the two look similar but are mathematically quite different.
