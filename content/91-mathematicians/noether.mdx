Noether transformed abstract algebra. Before her, the subject consisted of a collection of specific techniques — for polynomials, for groups, for matrices. After her, it was a unified theory organised around axiomatic structures: rings, ideals, modules. Modern algebra as it is taught today — the algebra of Years One, Two, and Three — is essentially her subject.

Her *Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen* (1921) and the series of papers that followed developed the abstract theory of **rings** and **ideals**. She generalised [Dedekind](/mathematicians/dedekind)'s theory of ideals from number rings to arbitrary commutative rings, and identified the structural properties — now called **Noetherian** — that made the theory work. A ring is Noetherian if every ascending chain of ideals eventually stabilises. This condition is precisely what ensures that factorisation and ideal theory behave well.

**Noether's theorem** (1915, published 1918) is one of the deepest results in mathematical physics. It states that every continuous symmetry of the action of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity. Time-translation symmetry gives conservation of energy. Spatial-translation symmetry gives conservation of linear momentum. Rotational symmetry gives conservation of angular momentum. The theorem unifies and explains these conservation laws at a fundamental level; it underlies all of modern theoretical physics.

When [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert) and Felix Klein wanted to appoint her to the Göttingen faculty in 1915, the philosophy faculty objected that it was inappropriate for a woman to hold a professorship. Hilbert replied: "I do not see that the sex of the candidate is an argument against her admission as a Privatdozent. After all, we are a university, not a bathing establishment." He lost the argument; she was permitted to lecture only under his name for several years.

In 1933, under the Nazi racial laws, she was dismissed from her position. She moved to Bryn Mawr College in Pennsylvania, where she died suddenly two years later from complications following surgery, aged 53. Hermann Weyl's obituary and Einstein's letter to the New York Times are among the most eloquent tributes in the mathematical literature.
