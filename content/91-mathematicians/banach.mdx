Banach is the founder of functional analysis, the branch of mathematics that studies infinite-dimensional spaces. His 1920 doctoral thesis — *Sur les opérations dans les ensembles abstraits et leur application aux équations intégrales* — introduced what are now called **Banach spaces** and proved the fundamental theorems of the theory.

A **Banach space** is a complete normed vector space: a vector space with a norm $\|\cdot\|$ such that every Cauchy sequence converges. The spaces $\ell^p$ (sequences with $\sum |x_n|^p < \infty$), $L^p(\mu)$ (measurable functions with $\int |f|^p\, d\mu < \infty$), and $C([a,b])$ (continuous functions on a closed interval, with the supremum norm) are all Banach spaces. The completeness condition is what makes analysis work: limits exist.

**Banach's fixed-point theorem** (contraction mapping theorem): if $T: X \to X$ is a contraction on a complete metric space $X$ — that is, $d(Tx, Ty) \leq k\, d(x,y)$ for some $k < 1$ — then $T$ has a unique fixed point. This theorem is used throughout analysis and differential equations to prove existence and uniqueness of solutions.

The **Hahn–Banach theorem** guarantees that continuous linear functionals on a subspace can be extended to the whole space without increasing their norm. It implies that Banach spaces have enough continuous linear functionals to separate points — a fundamental structural result.

The **Banach–Tarski paradox** (proved with Alfred Tarski in 1924) states that a solid ball in $\mathbb{R}^3$ can be decomposed into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls of the same size as the original. The pieces are not measurable sets; the "paradox" exploits the axiom of choice and has no physical interpretation. It is one of the most counterintuitive results in mathematics.

Banach was a central figure in the Lwów mathematical community that gathered at the Scottish Café from around 1935 onwards. The mathematicians there kept a large notebook — the **Scottish Book** — in which they wrote open problems and offered prizes for their solution (a live goose was offered for one). The Scottish Book survived the war and problems from it continue to drive research in functional analysis.

Banach died in August 1945, shortly after the German occupation ended, from lung cancer contracted during the war.
