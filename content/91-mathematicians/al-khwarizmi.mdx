Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi worked at the House of Wisdom in Baghdad under the caliph al-Mamun in the early ninth century. He was a geographer, astronomer, and mathematician, but his two most influential contributions were both mathematical.

His *Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala* ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing") gave algebra its name and its method. *Al-jabr* — completion — means transposing a subtracted quantity to the other side of an equation; *al-muqabala* — balancing — means cancelling equal quantities from both sides. These two operations reduce any linear or quadratic equation to a standard form, which can then be solved. The book was translated into Latin in the twelfth century and became the standard European algebra text.

His second influential work described the Hindu-Arabic numeral system — digits 0–9 with decimal place value — and was translated into Latin as *Algoritmi de numero Indorum* ("Al-Khwarizmi on the Indian Numerals"). The Latinised form of his name, *Algoritmi*, gave the word "algorithm" to all subsequent human culture.

Al-Khwarizmi was transmitting a system he had learned from Indian mathematics, not inventing it. The Indian origins of the decimal system — particularly the work of Brahmagupta (628 CE) — should be acknowledged whenever his name is cited. But his role in transmitting and explaining the system to the Islamic world, and from there to medieval Europe, was decisive.

When you follow an algorithm — any algorithm, in mathematics or computing — you are using a concept that bears the shape of his name.
