Pierre de Fermat was a lawyer and councillor at the Parlement of Toulouse who did mathematics as a hobby. He was, by almost any measure, the greatest amateur mathematician in history.

Fermat's birth year is recorded variously as 1601 or c.1607; the earlier date derives from a different Pierre Fermat (likely a half-brother) and is almost certainly wrong. The MacTutor History of Mathematics archive gives c.1607 as the more reliable estimate.

Fermat corresponded with Mersenne, Descartes, Pascal, and other leading mathematicians of his day, rarely publishing but sending results as challenges in letters. He independently developed analytic geometry (coordinate geometry) around the same time as Descartes. He developed the "method of adequality" for finding maxima, minima, and tangent lines — a precursor to the calculus.

His deepest work was in number theory. **Fermat's little theorem** states: if $p$ is prime and $\gcd(a, p) = 1$, then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{p}$. This theorem is the basis of the Fermat primality test and underpins RSA cryptography. He conjectured (correctly, though not proved until 1995) that the integers of the form $4k+1$ are sums of two squares in essentially one way.

The **Last Theorem** — that $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no positive integer solutions for $n \ge 3$ — was written in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's *Arithmetica* around 1637. Whether Fermat had a proof is unknown; the margin note says he had one. Modern consensus is that he almost certainly had a proof for $n = 4$ (which he did publish), but probably not a general proof, since the complete proof (by Wiles in 1995) required mathematics invented centuries after Fermat's death.

The marginal note haunted mathematics for 358 years.
