Riemann worked in the tradition of [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) — he was Gauss's student at Göttingen — and, like Gauss, his contributions span multiple areas of mathematics so deeply that it is difficult to survey them briefly.

The **Riemann integral** is the first rigorous definition of integration most students meet. The integral $\int_a^b f(x)\, dx$ is defined as the limit of Riemann sums — partitions of the interval $[a,b]$ into subintervals, with $f$ evaluated at sample points and multiplied by the subinterval widths. Riemann showed exactly which functions are integrable in this sense (those with a set of discontinuities of measure zero) and gave the framework within which [Lebesgue](/mathematicians/lebesgue) would later build a more powerful theory.

**Riemann surfaces** provide the natural setting for complex analysis: to make $\sqrt{z}$ a single-valued function, one constructs a two-sheeted surface over the complex plane where the function is well-defined everywhere. His 1851 doctoral dissertation, presenting this framework, was called "a masterwork" by Gauss.

**Riemannian geometry** generalises the differential geometry of [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) to spaces of any dimension. Riemann's 1854 Habilitationsvortrag ("On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry") introduced the concept of a Riemannian manifold: a space equipped with a metric that can vary from point to point. He proposed that the geometry of physical space might be curved, and that curvature could be intrinsic rather than imposed from outside. Einstein's general relativity is built on exactly this framework, using Riemannian geometry to describe spacetime curvature.

In number theory, Riemann's 1859 paper "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude" introduced the **Riemann zeta function** $\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}$, extended to the whole complex plane, and proposed — without proof — the **Riemann hypothesis**: all non-trivial zeros of $\zeta(s)$ lie on the line $\text{Re}(s) = \frac{1}{2}$. This remains the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics and is one of the Clay Millennium Prize Problems.
