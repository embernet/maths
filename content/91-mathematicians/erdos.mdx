Erdős is the most collaborative mathematician in history and one of the most prolific. He published roughly 1,500 papers and had direct co-authors — people who published a paper with him — numbering 509. The concept of the **Erdős number** emerged from this: mathematicians with a joint paper with Erdős have Erdős number 1; those with a paper with an Erdős-1 mathematician have number 2; and so on. The median Erdős number among active mathematicians is around 5.

He owned almost nothing and lived from hospitality, mathematical awards, and stipends. He would arrive at a mathematician's house, stay for a few days of intensive collaboration, and move on. His habitual greeting was "my brain is open." He referred to himself as "SF" (Supreme Fascist, a name for God, with whom he had a running theological dispute) and to children as "epsilons."

His most lasting mathematical contribution is the **probabilistic method** in combinatorics: to prove that an object with certain properties exists, show that a randomly chosen object has those properties with positive probability. This idea — that existence can be demonstrated by probability — was revolutionary, and Erdős was its pioneer. The probabilistic method is now a central technique in combinatorics, graph theory, and theoretical computer science.

In **Ramsey theory** (the study of unavoidable structure in large combinatorial objects), he proved fundamental results. Ramsey's theorem states that in any sufficiently large graph, there is either a clique of size $k$ or an independent set of size $l$; the exact bounds on "sufficiently large" are mostly unknown, and Erdős worked intensively on these questions. He once offered prize money for a proof that the Ramsey number $R(5,5)$ lies in a specific range; the exact value is still unknown.

He also contributed to analytic number theory (the elementary proof of the prime number theorem, co-proved with Selberg in 1949), graph theory, and set theory.

He died at a mathematics conference in Warsaw in 1996, at 83, while working on a problem. He had said he wanted to die doing mathematics; he did.
