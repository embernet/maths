Turing is the father of theoretical computer science, a central figure in the breaking of German codes during the Second World War, and a pioneer of artificial intelligence — and he achieved all this in a career cut short at 41.

His 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem" introduced the **Turing machine**: an abstract device with a tape of cells (each blank or carrying a symbol), a read/write head, and a state table that determines the machine's action based on its current state and the symbol it reads. The machine is simple enough to be fully defined, yet powerful enough to simulate any mechanical computation.

Turing used this model to resolve the **Entscheidungsproblem** (decision problem), posed by [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert): is there an algorithm that can decide the truth or falsehood of any mathematical statement? Turing proved the answer is no, via the **halting problem**: there is no Turing machine that can determine, for an arbitrary programme $P$ and input $I$, whether $P$ eventually halts or runs forever. The proof is by diagonalisation, in the spirit of [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) and [Gödel](/mathematicians/godel).

This result established the first absolute limits on computability: some problems are not just hard but provably unsolvable by any algorithm. The class of functions computable by Turing machines is now understood to coincide with those computable by any reasonable model of computation — the **Church–Turing thesis**.

At Bletchley Park during the Second World War, Turing was central to the cryptanalysis of the German Enigma cipher. He designed the **Bombe**, an electromechanical device that exploited cribs (known or guessed plaintext) to rapidly eliminate candidate Enigma settings. The intelligence produced at Bletchley — "Ultra" — is widely considered to have shortened the war by several years.

His 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" introduced the **Turing test**: a proposed criterion for machine intelligence based on whether a human interrogator can distinguish, in text exchange, between a machine and a human. The test has been debated ever since; it is now less central to AI research than when Turing wrote, but it crystallised the question of what machine intelligence might mean.

His 1952 paper on morphogenesis proposed reaction-diffusion equations as the mechanism for biological pattern formation — how a developing embryo generates spatial patterns from initially uniform conditions. This initiated the mathematical biology of pattern formation, and the "Turing instability" in reaction-diffusion systems remains an active research area.

In 1952, he was prosecuted for gross indecency, convicted, and subjected to chemical castration as a condition of avoiding prison. His security clearance was revoked. He died on 7 June 1954; the coroner's inquest returned a verdict of suicide by cyanide poisoning. He was 41. He was granted a royal pardon in 2013.
