Dedekind is the mathematician most responsible for turning [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor)'s intuitions into rigorous foundations, and for placing the construction of the real number system on a firm basis.

The problem with the real numbers, in the mid-nineteenth century, was that nobody had defined them. Mathematicians worked with them freely, but the real numbers were simply assumed to exist as a continuum. Dedekind's solution — published in 1872, in *Stetigkeit und irrationale Zahlen* ("Continuity and Irrational Numbers") — was the **Dedekind cut**.

A Dedekind cut is a partition of the rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ into two non-empty sets $(A, B)$ where every element of $A$ is less than every element of $B$ and $A$ has no greatest element. Each cut corresponds to a real number: the cut $(\{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q < \sqrt{2}\}, \{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q \geq \sqrt{2}\})$ defines the irrational number $\sqrt{2}$ in terms of purely rational quantities. Arithmetic operations on cuts can be defined, and the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$ — that every Cauchy sequence converges — follows immediately.

This construction, alongside [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor)'s construction via equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences, established that the real numbers can be rigorously defined from the rationals.

In algebra, Dedekind introduced the concept of an **ideal** in a ring. The motivation was Kummer's work on Fermat's Last Theorem: in the rings of algebraic integers that arise in number theory, unique factorisation into primes can fail. Kummer had introduced "ideal numbers" to restore it; Dedekind reformulated this as the study of certain subsets of rings (ideals) and proved that in Dedekind domains — rings sharing key properties of the integers — every ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals. This is the foundation of algebraic number theory.

He was a close friend and collaborator of [Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann) and [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor), and edited the collected works of [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) and Dirichlet. He spent most of his career in Brunswick in what, by the standards of the time, looked like obscurity — he turned down offers of prestigious chairs in favour of his home town.
