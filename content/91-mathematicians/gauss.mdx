Gauss is often called the *Princeps Mathematicorum* — the Prince of Mathematicians — and unlike many such honorifics, it holds up. His contributions span number theory, algebra, statistics, differential geometry, electromagnetism, and astronomy, and in several of those fields he is the founding figure.

His doctoral thesis (1799) proved the fundamental theorem of algebra — that every polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root. Earlier alleged proofs, including one by [Euler](/mathematicians/euler), had gaps; Gauss gave the first rigorous demonstration. He gave four distinct proofs over his lifetime.

His *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801), written when he was 24, created modern number theory essentially from scratch. It introduced modular arithmetic, proved the law of quadratic reciprocity (which Gauss called the "gem of arithmetic" and proved eight times), and classified all binary quadratic forms. Number theorists still work within the framework Gauss built.

The **Gaussian distribution** — the bell curve — $\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$ bears his name because he used it to model astronomical measurement errors. He also developed the method of **least squares** (independently of [Legendre](/mathematicians/legendre), and before), which is the foundation of linear regression: find the line that minimises the sum of squared deviations from the data. The method was crucial for the rediscovery of the asteroid Ceres after it was lost behind the sun — Gauss predicted where it would reappear, and he was right.

In differential geometry, Gauss proved the *Theorema Egregium* ("remarkable theorem"): the Gaussian curvature of a surface — its intrinsic measure of how curved it is — is preserved under bending without stretching. A flat map of the Earth necessarily distorts distances or angles because the sphere has non-zero curvature. This theorem initiated the study of intrinsic geometry that led, through Riemann, to general relativity.

Gauss almost certainly developed non-Euclidean geometry before [Bolyai](/mathematicians/bolyai) and [Lobachevsky](/mathematicians/lobachevsky) published it — his private correspondence strongly implies this. He chose not to publish, reportedly unwilling to face the "clamour of the Boeotians" (the uncomprehending public).

He was famously reluctant to publish anything he considered incomplete. "Few but ripe" was his motto. This habit of suppression meant that many of his most important discoveries were found in his diary only after his death, creating awkward priority disputes with contemporaries who had, fairly, published first.
