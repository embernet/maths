Pascal was a prodigy. He reportedly rediscovered several of Euclid's theorems on his own as a child. At sixteen he proved Pascal's theorem: if a hexagon is inscribed in a conic section (circle, ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola), then the three pairs of opposite sides, extended, meet at three collinear points. At eighteen he designed and built the *Pascaline* — one of the first mechanical calculators — to help his father manage tax accounts.

His correspondence with Fermat (1654) on a problem posed by the Chevalier de Méré established the foundations of probability theory: how to divide stakes fairly when a game of chance is interrupted before its conclusion. The resulting framework — expected value, the binomial distribution, and the notion of equally likely outcomes — underlies all of probability theory.

Pascal's triangle (the triangular arrangement of binomial coefficients) appears in his 1653 *Traité du triangle arithmétique*. Though the triangle had been known in earlier Arabic and Chinese mathematics, Pascal's systematic development of its properties and applications (to combinatorics and probability) gave it canonical form in the Western tradition.

After a dramatic carriage accident in 1654, in which his horses fell over a bridge and he narrowly escaped death, Pascal experienced a religious conversion. He abandoned mathematics almost entirely, devoted himself to Jansenist theology, and spent his remaining years writing the *Pensées* (Thoughts) — an unfinished apologetics for Christianity that is one of the masterpieces of French literature. He died at thirty-nine.

Pascal's mathematical legacy — the binomial theorem, Pascal's triangle, the beginnings of probability — was compressed into about fifteen years of work.
