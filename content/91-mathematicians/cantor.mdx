Cantor created set theory and, in doing so, discovered that infinity is not one thing but a hierarchy of sizes — a result so unexpected, so counterintuitive, and so fundamental that it transformed mathematics.

His first breakthrough, in 1874, was the proof that the real numbers cannot be put into one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers. The natural numbers $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$ are countably infinite. The real numbers in any interval are **uncountably infinite** — a strictly larger infinity.

He proved this with the **diagonal argument** (1891, though a different proof appeared in 1874). Suppose, for contradiction, that the real numbers in $[0,1]$ can be listed: $r_1, r_2, r_3, \ldots$ Construct a new real number $d$ whose $n$th decimal digit differs from the $n$th decimal digit of $r_n$. Then $d$ is not on the list — it differs from $r_n$ at position $n$ for every $n$. Contradiction. The list cannot be complete.

This argument — now one of the most celebrated proofs in mathematics — shows that **there are different sizes of infinity**. Cantor denoted the cardinality of the natural numbers $\aleph_0$ (aleph-null) and the cardinality of the real numbers $\mathfrak{c}$ (the cardinality of the continuum). He proved $\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}$, and conjectured the **continuum hypothesis**: there is no set whose cardinality lies strictly between $\aleph_0$ and $\mathfrak{c}$.

The continuum hypothesis was the first of [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert)'s 23 problems (1900). Gödel showed in 1940 that it cannot be disproved from the standard axioms of set theory; [Cohen](/mathematicians/cohen) showed in 1963 that it cannot be proved. The continuum hypothesis is independent of the axioms — neither provable nor disprovable.

The **Cantor set** is constructed by taking $[0,1]$, removing the middle third, removing the middle thirds of each remaining interval, and repeating. The resulting set is uncountable (the same cardinality as $[0,1]$), has measure zero (its total length is zero), and is a perfect set with no isolated points. It is the canonical example of a fractal and appears throughout analysis as a source of counterexamples.

Cantor's work was fiercely opposed by [Kronecker](/mathematicians/kronecker), who believed that mathematics should concern only finite, constructive operations and that actual infinity was metaphysics, not mathematics. The opposition contributed to Cantor's periods of mental illness. [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert) defended him; Poincaré was sceptical; [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) was broadly supportive. By the time Cantor died, set theory had become the foundation of mathematics.
