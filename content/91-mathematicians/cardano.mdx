Cardano was one of the most extraordinary figures of the Renaissance: physician, mathematician, philosopher, astrologer, and gambler. He wrote one of the first books on probability (*Liber de Ludo Aleae*, "Book on Games of Chance"), cast his own horoscope and predicted the date of his own death — and, when the day arrived, allegedly committed suicide to make the prediction true.

His *Ars Magna* (1545) was the most important algebra book of the sixteenth century. It contained the general solution to cubic equations (obtained from Niccolò Tartaglia under a confidentiality agreement that Cardano then broke) and the extension to quartic equations (due to his student Ludovico Ferrari). The publication caused one of the great priority disputes in mathematics, with Tartaglia outraged by what he saw as theft.

The *Ars Magna* is also the first published work to use complex numbers. In solving cubic equations with three real roots, the method requires intermediate calculations involving square roots of negative numbers — even when the final answers are real. Cardano wrote down expressions like $\sqrt{-15}$ and performed calculations with them, while calling them "sophistic" and dismissing them as useless.

He was wrong about their uselessness. The complex numbers that Cardano could not accept as legitimate mathematical objects are now, as [Euler's](/mathematicians/euler) formula makes clear, among the most fundamental structures in mathematics. Quantum mechanics is written in complex numbers; electrical engineers use the imaginary unit daily; Fourier analysis is formulated in terms of complex exponentials. The "useless subtlety" Cardano discovered became indispensable.
