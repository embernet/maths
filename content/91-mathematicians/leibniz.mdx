Leibniz was a polymath in the Renaissance mould: lawyer, diplomat, historian, philosopher, linguist, and mathematician. He invented the calculus independently of Newton in the early 1670s, motivated by problems of tangents and quadratures (areas under curves). His notation — $dy/dx$ for the derivative, the integral sign $\int$ (an elongated S, for *summa*) — was chosen with care to make calculations as transparent as possible.

The notation won. Leibniz's $dy/dx$ expresses the chain rule self-evidently: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$. Newton's $\dot{y}$ notation conceals this. The multivariable calculus — partial derivatives $\partial f / \partial x$, the Jacobian, the gradient — is completely natural in Leibniz notation. British mathematicians who refused to use it out of loyalty to Newton fell behind continental mathematics by roughly a century.

Leibniz also invented binary arithmetic: the representation of all numbers using only 0 and 1, with the rules $1 + 1 = 10$, $10 + 10 = 100$, and so on. He was fascinated by its connection to the I Ching and to philosophical ideas about creation from nothing (God as 1, nothingness as 0). He could not have imagined that binary arithmetic would become, three centuries later, the language of all digital computation.

The priority dispute with Newton poisoned the last years of his life. The Royal Society — with Newton himself managing events from behind the scenes — accused Leibniz of plagiarism in a 1713 report. Modern historians agree that both men invented calculus independently; Leibniz published first (1684), Newton derived it first. The verdict against Leibniz was unjust.

He died in 1716, in dispute with the Hanoverian court and isolated from the intellectual community. His funeral, according to contemporary accounts, was attended by his secretary alone.
