Pythagoras founded a community in Croton (southern Italy) that was simultaneously a mathematical school, a religious brotherhood, and something approaching a sect. Members followed strict rules: no eating beans; sleep facing east; do not sit on a quart-measure. What united them was the conviction that *number* was the fundamental reality underlying the universe, and that all natural phenomena — from musical harmonies to the motions of the planets — could be expressed as ratios of whole numbers.

The Pythagorean theorem ($a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ for a right-angled triangle) almost certainly predates Pythagoras — Babylonian clay tablets record the result over a millennium earlier — but the Pythagorean school gave the first *proof*.

The discovery that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational — that it cannot be expressed as a ratio $p/q$ of integers — destroyed the worldview the school was built on. The proof is simple: assume $\sqrt{2} = p/q$ in lowest terms; then $p$ must be even; then $q$ must be even; contradiction. No complicated machinery, just logic.

According to legend, the discovery was made by one Hippasus during a sea voyage. He was thrown overboard for the sacrilege of revealing that irrational numbers exist. Whether this is true or a later embellishment is unknown — but it captures something true about the experience of encountering a mathematical proof that overturns a comfortable worldview.

You will encounter irrational numbers throughout your degree. The construction of the real numbers from the rationals (via Dedekind cuts or Cauchy sequences) is, in a sense, the rigorous resolution of the crisis that Pythagoras's followers could only suppress.
