Cauchy is the father of modern mathematical rigour. Before him, the calculus worked, but nobody could say exactly why; the key notions of limit, continuity, and derivative rested on intuition and geometric argument. Cauchy replaced intuition with precise definition.

His *Cours d'Analyse* (1821) gave the first rigorous treatment of limits: rather than appealing to "infinitely small quantities," he defined the limit of a function in terms of inequalities. The **ε–δ definition** — that $f(x) \to L$ as $x \to a$ means that for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $\delta > 0$ such that $|x - a| < \delta$ implies $|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon$ — is Cauchy's legacy, and it is the definition every analysis student encounters in foundational topics or core mathematics.

A **Cauchy sequence** is a sequence in which the terms eventually become arbitrarily close to one another: for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists $N$ such that $|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon$ for all $m, n > N$. The question of whether every Cauchy sequence converges is the question of completeness — and completeness turned out to be what distinguishes the real numbers from the rationals.

Cauchy made fundamental contributions to complex analysis. **Cauchy's integral theorem** — that for a holomorphic function on a simply connected domain, $\oint_\gamma f(z)\, dz = 0$ for any closed path $\gamma$ — and the **Cauchy integral formula** expressing the value of a holomorphic function at an interior point as a contour integral over the boundary are the two pillars of the subject.

He also made important contributions to group theory (studying permutation groups in the context of polynomial equations, and proving what is now called Cauchy's theorem in group theory: if a prime $p$ divides the order of a group, the group has an element of order $p$) and to mechanics, elasticity, and optics.

His prolificacy was matched by a certain ruthlessness in matters of priority. [Abel](/mathematicians/abel) submitted a major paper on elliptic integrals to the Paris Academy in 1826; Cauchy, as referee, mislaid it. The paper was not found until after Abel's death. His treatment of [Galois](/mathematicians/galois) was similarly unhelpful. He remained unapologetically convinced of his own importance throughout.
