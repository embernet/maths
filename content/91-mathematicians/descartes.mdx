René Descartes is best known as a philosopher — "I think, therefore I am" — but his mathematical contributions were as significant as his philosophical ones. His 1637 *La Géométrie* (appended to his philosophical *Discourse on the Method*) invented what we now call Cartesian coordinates and analytic geometry.

The idea is deceptively simple: assign to each point in the plane a pair of numbers $(x, y)$ measuring its horizontal and vertical distance from a fixed origin. Any geometric curve then corresponds to an algebraic equation relating $x$ and $y$. A circle of radius $r$ is $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$; a parabola is $y = ax^2$; a straight line is $ax + by = c$.

This fusion of algebra and geometry was transformative. Any geometric question could now be translated into algebra and solved with algebraic methods; any algebraic equation could be visualised geometrically. The two had been separate disciplines for two thousand years; Descartes unified them.

The notation he introduced — $x$, $y$, $z$ for unknowns, $a$, $b$, $c$ for known quantities — became standard. Modern algebraic notation is largely Cartesian.

[Pierre de Fermat](/mathematicians/fermat) arrived at essentially the same ideas independently. Historians debate who deserves priority; the insight was in the air in the early seventeenth century, and both men made important contributions.

When you write down an equation and plot it — in Year One, in calculus, in statistics, anywhere — you are using Descartes's unification. The coordinate plane itself bears his name.
