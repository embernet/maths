Almost nothing is known about Euclid's life. We know he worked in Alexandria under the early Ptolemaic dynasty; we know he wrote the *Elements*; and we have the reported exchange with Ptolemy I — if there is no shortcut to geometry for a king, there is none for anyone. Beyond that, he is a name attached to a book.

The *Elements* is thirteen books presenting all of Greek mathematics — plane and solid geometry, number theory, and the theory of proportion — derived from five postulates by purely logical deduction. It was the dominant mathematics textbook for over two thousand years and defined what a mathematical proof is: a finite sequence of statements, each following from previous statements or from the postulates by acknowledged rules of inference.

Euclid's algorithm for computing the greatest common divisor of two integers (repeated subtraction of the smaller from the larger) is one of the oldest algorithms still in everyday use. It underlies the extended Euclidean algorithm (which finds integers $x, y$ with $ax + by = \gcd(a,b)$), the theory of prime factorisation, and the RSA cryptosystem.

The *Elements* contains a famous proof that there are infinitely many primes. Assume finitely many: $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_n$. Form $N = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_n + 1$. Then $N$ is not divisible by any $p_i$ (it leaves remainder 1). Either $N$ is prime (contradicting our complete list), or it has a prime factor not in our list. Either way, the list was incomplete.

This proof is twenty-three centuries old and as clean today as when it was written.
