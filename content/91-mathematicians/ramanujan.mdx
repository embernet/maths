Ramanujan is the most extraordinary example of mathematical talent operating in isolation that the history of mathematics contains. Growing up in Madras with no university education, no access to journals, and no mathematical community, he independently rediscovered and extended large portions of nineteenth-century mathematics — and generated, in parallel, thousands of identities and results that were entirely new.

His early notebooks, compiled before he contacted Hardy in 1913, contain results in number theory, elliptic functions, continued fractions, and infinite series that range from well-known (rediscovered independently) to profoundly original. Many of the proofs were missing or incomplete; verifying and proving his identities occupied mathematicians for decades.

The letter he sent to G. H. Hardy at Cambridge contained about 120 theorems, stated without proof. Hardy and Littlewood spent an evening working through them. Hardy later wrote that the formulae "defeated me completely" — they were unlike anything in the literature, yet obviously correct. Hardy arranged for Ramanujan to come to Cambridge, where they worked together from 1914 until 1919.

Their collaboration on **partitions** — the number of ways of writing an integer as a sum of positive integers — produced the **Hardy–Ramanujan asymptotic formula**: $p(n) \sim \frac{1}{4n\sqrt{3}} e^{\pi\sqrt{2n/3}}$ as $n \to \infty$. The proof used the **circle method**, a technique of complex analysis that became one of the central tools of analytic number theory. The formula is remarkable for its precision: $p(200) = 3{,}972{,}999{,}029{,}388$, and the formula gives this to within one integer.

Ramanujan became the second Indian Fellow of the Royal Society, and a Fellow of Trinity College Cambridge. He returned to India in 1919, ill with tuberculosis (and possibly hepatic amoebiasis — the diagnosis is disputed). He died in 1920, aged 32. His final letter to Hardy, written in January 1920, introduced "mock theta functions" — a concept whose full significance was not understood until decades later.

His notebooks continue to be mined. The last of them, the "Lost Notebook," was discovered in the Wren Library at Trinity College in 1976 by George Andrews; it filled a research programme for the following decades.
