Laplace was the greatest applied mathematician of his era: his *Mécanique Céleste* ("Celestial Mechanics," five volumes, 1799–1825) was the definitive mathematical treatment of the solar system. His predecessor Newton had worried that the solar system might be unstable and would require periodic divine intervention; Laplace demonstrated — though the argument was not fully rigorous — that it was stable on astronomical timescales.

The **Laplace equation** $\nabla^2\phi = 0$ describes gravitational, electrostatic, and steady-state temperature potentials. Its solutions are harmonic functions, characterised by the mean value property and the maximum principle. The **Laplace transform** $\mathcal{L}\{f(t)\} = \int_0^\infty f(t) e^{-st} dt$ converts differential equations to algebraic ones, and is the essential tool of control theory and signal analysis.

Laplace independently derived what we now call Bayes' theorem, and his philosophical writings on probability introduced the principle of insufficient reason (if you have no reason to prefer one outcome over another, assign them equal probability). His *Théorie Analytique des Probabilités* (1812) was the first systematic treatment of probability theory.

He was a consummate political survivor: he served under the Ancien Régime, the Revolution, Napoleon, and the restored monarchy, adapting his allegiances with remarkable flexibility. Napoleon, who made him Minister of the Interior (briefly, until Napoleon found him unsuitable for administration), noted that Laplace "carried the spirit of the infinitely small into administration."

His exchange with Napoleon about God is one of the most quoted in the history of science. Whatever his personal beliefs, mathematically he had no need of that hypothesis.
