Kolmogorov placed probability theory on its modern foundations. Before 1933, probability was an active but logically uncertain subject: there were competing interpretations (frequentist, subjective, geometric), no agreed axioms, and ongoing controversy about what probability actually was. Kolmogorov's *Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung* ("Foundations of the Theory of Probability," 1933) resolved this by defining probability as measure theory.

In Kolmogorov's framework, a **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ where $\Omega$ is the sample space, $\mathcal{F}$ is a $\sigma$-algebra of events, and $P: \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$ is a measure satisfying $P(\Omega) = 1$ and countable additivity. All of probability theory — conditional probability, independence, expectation, random variables, the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem — follows from these axioms as pure mathematical consequences.

In turbulence, Kolmogorov's 1941 scaling theory (K41) predicts how kinetic energy is distributed across scales in a turbulent fluid: the energy spectrum scales as $k^{-5/3}$ in the inertial range (where $k$ is wavenumber). This Kolmogorov scaling law is one of the most widely confirmed predictions in fluid mechanics, and the Kolmogorov microscale — the smallest length scale at which turbulent dissipation occurs — is a fundamental quantity in fluid dynamics.

**Kolmogorov complexity** (developed independently by Kolmogorov, Solomonoff, and Chaitin in the 1960s) measures the algorithmic complexity of a string: the length of the shortest computer programme that produces it. A random string has high Kolmogorov complexity (no short programme generates it); a structured string has low complexity. This gives a formal definition of randomness and provides foundations for algorithmic information theory.

He made major contributions to ergodic theory, stochastic processes (the Kolmogorov forward and backward equations for Markov processes are named for him), and in the 1950s proved the KAM theorem (Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser) on the persistence of quasiperiodic motion in near-integrable Hamiltonian systems — a breakthrough in the theory of dynamical systems.
