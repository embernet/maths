Hardy was the leading British pure mathematician of the first half of the twentieth century and, together with J. E. Littlewood, ran a research programme in analytic number theory and analysis that dominated the subject for three decades.

His most famous book is *A Mathematician's Apology* (1940), written when his mathematical powers were fading — "A mathematician, like a painter or a poet, is a maker of patterns" — a meditation on mathematical beauty, the nature of mathematical truth, and the defence of pure mathematics for its own sake. It remains the most read book about what it is like to think mathematically.

The **Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium** in genetics — that allele and genotype frequencies in a population remain constant in the absence of selection, mutation, or drift — was derived by Hardy (and, independently, by Weinberg) in 1908. Hardy reportedly considered it trivial and was somewhat embarrassed to be cited for it. It became a foundational result in population genetics.

**Hardy spaces** $H^p$ are spaces of holomorphic functions on the unit disc with a controlled $L^p$ norm on the boundary circle. They appear throughout complex analysis and harmonic analysis.

The **Hardy–Ramanujan theorem** — that the number of distinct prime factors of a "typical" integer near $n$ is approximately $\log \log n$ — is one of the founding results of probabilistic number theory, the idea that primes behave in certain respects like random variables.

Hardy's collaboration with Ramanujan, which he described as the most extraordinary episode of his mathematical life, is recounted from his perspective in *A Mathematician's Apology* and in his obituary notice of Ramanujan. His account of receiving Ramanujan's 1913 letter — "a single glance told me that these could not have been written by anyone who did not possess the highest mathematical powers" — is one of the best-known passages in mathematical biography.

He was famously eccentric: deeply allergic to mirrors, convinced that God was a personal enemy, and in the habit of sending test-match cricket scorecards to colleagues in lieu of greeting.
