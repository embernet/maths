Bolzano is one of the great overlooked figures in the history of mathematics. His rigorous treatment of analysis, written in Prague in relative isolation, anticipated much of what Cauchy and Weierstrass later made famous — but because he published in obscure Czech journals and was politically suppressed, his work was largely unknown to the Parisian mathematicians who built the rigour revolution.

His 1817 paper *Rein analytischer Beweis* ("Purely Analytical Proof") gave the first rigorous proof of the **intermediate value theorem**: if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a)$ and $f(b)$ have opposite signs, then there exists $c \in (a, b)$ with $f(c) = 0$. Earlier proofs had relied on geometric intuition; Bolzano's proof worked from the definition of continuity.

The **Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem** — that every bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ has a convergent subsequence — is one of the fundamental results of analysis. It guarantees that you cannot have an infinite sequence of points in a bounded region without some of them clustering. [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) later gave the theorem the form in which it is now taught; Bolzano had the essential idea first.

His later work, *Paradoxien des Unendlichen* ("Paradoxes of the Infinite"), written around 1847 though not published until after his death, discussed infinite sets with remarkable clarity. He noted that an infinite set can be put in one-to-one correspondence with a proper subset of itself — for example, the natural numbers correspond to the even numbers via $n \mapsto 2n$ — and treated this as a property of infinite sets rather than a contradiction. This is the observation that [Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) would later make the foundation of set theory.

As a Catholic priest and philosopher, Bolzano also produced major works in logic and the philosophy of mathematics, developing a notion of logical consequence that anticipates modern formal logic. He was suspended from his professorship in 1820 under pressure from the Austrian authorities, who found his lectures on religion and society dangerously liberal.
