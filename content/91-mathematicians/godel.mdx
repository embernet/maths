Gödel's incompleteness theorems are among the most profound mathematical results of the twentieth century. Published in 1931 when he was 25, they demolished [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert)'s programme and showed that the ambition to formalise all of mathematics was unachievable in principle.

**The First Incompleteness Theorem**: Any consistent formal system $F$ capable of expressing basic arithmetic contains statements that are neither provable nor disprovable within $F$. In other words, consistent formal systems are incomplete: there are truths they cannot prove.

**The Second Incompleteness Theorem**: Such a system $F$ cannot prove its own consistency. If it could, and was consistent, we would have derived what we cannot have.

The proof works via **Gödel numbering**: a method of encoding statements and proofs in arithmetic as natural numbers. Using this encoding, Gödel constructed a sentence $G$ that essentially says "I am not provable in this system." If $G$ were provable, the system would prove a falsehood; if $G$ were disprovable, the system would be inconsistent. So $G$ is neither — yet in the standard model of arithmetic, $G$ is true.

The theorems apply to any system strong enough to express the arithmetic of natural numbers — this includes Peano arithmetic, set theory (ZFC), and anything extending them. They do not apply to weaker systems (Presburger arithmetic, for instance, is complete and consistent).

Gödel also proved the **completeness theorem** for first-order logic (his doctoral thesis, 1929): every logically valid first-order sentence is provable. This is distinct from, and not contradicted by, the incompleteness theorems, which concern sufficiently expressive formal theories, not logic itself.

In 1940, he proved the consistency of the **continuum hypothesis** with the ZFC axioms — Hilbert's first problem — by constructing the "constructible universe" $L$ in which both ZFC and the continuum hypothesis hold. Cohen later showed the negation of the continuum hypothesis is also consistent, making the hypothesis independent of ZFC.

Gödel spent most of his career at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, where he was a close friend of Einstein. He was a deeply reclusive, paranoid figure: he feared poisoning and, when his wife Adele became ill and could no longer prepare his food, he essentially stopped eating. He died in 1978, weighing 65 pounds.
