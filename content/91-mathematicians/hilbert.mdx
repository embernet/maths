Hilbert dominated mathematics in the first third of the twentieth century with an authority matched only by [Euler](/mathematicians/euler) and [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss). The Göttingen school he led was the world's preeminent mathematical centre until the Nazi dismissals of 1933 destroyed it.

His 1900 address to the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris presented **23 problems** for the coming century: a manifesto and agenda for mathematics. They included the Riemann hypothesis ([Problem 8](/mathematicians/riemann)), the continuum hypothesis ([Problem 1](/mathematicians/cantor)), the consistency of arithmetic (Problem 2), and questions in algebraic geometry, number theory, physics, and the calculus of variations. Solving a Hilbert problem remains among the highest distinctions in mathematics.

**Hilbert spaces** — complete inner product spaces — are the natural setting for quantum mechanics and for the spectral theory of operators. The Hilbert space $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ of square-integrable functions, with the inner product $\langle f, g \rangle = \int f(x)\overline{g(x)}\, dx$, is the state space of a quantum particle. Hilbert introduced this framework in his work on integral equations, before quantum mechanics existed; Heisenberg and Schrödinger's formulations were later shown to be equivalent formulations in a Hilbert space.

**Hilbert's programme** was the project of formalising all of mathematics — reducing it to a finite set of axioms and rules of inference from which every true statement could be derived, and proving that the system was consistent (free of contradictions). The programme began with *Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1899), which gave the first fully rigorous axiomatisation of Euclidean geometry, eliminating the implicit assumptions that had lurked in Euclid since the *Elements*.

[Gödel](/mathematicians/godel)'s incompleteness theorems (1931) ended the programme. Gödel showed that any sufficiently powerful formal system either contains unprovable truths or is inconsistent; in either case, the system cannot prove its own consistency. "We must know, we will know," Hilbert had said. Gödel showed that some things cannot be known from within a fixed formal system.

When the Nazis came to power in 1933, most of Hilbert's Jewish colleagues and students were dismissed. A Nazi minister at a dinner asked Hilbert how mathematics was faring at Göttingen, now that it had been freed of Jewish influence. "Mathematics at Göttingen?" Hilbert replied. "There is really none any more."
