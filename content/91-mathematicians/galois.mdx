Galois answered a deeper version of the question [Abel](/mathematicians/abel) had settled. Abel proved that the general quintic cannot be solved by radicals. Galois explained *why*, and characterised precisely which polynomial equations can and cannot be solved in terms of radical expressions.

His key insight was to associate to each polynomial equation a **group** — now called its Galois group — consisting of the permutations of its roots that preserve all algebraic relationships between them. The polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if this group is *solvable* in a technical sense: it has a chain of normal subgroups ending at the trivial group, with each successive quotient being abelian. The general quintic is not solvable by radicals because its Galois group is the symmetric group $S_5$, which is not solvable.

Galois developed this framework between 1830 and 1832, in the period when [Abel](/mathematicians/abel) had already published his unsolvability result (1824–1826). They were working on related problems independently, with Abel's published proof preceding Galois's deeper structural account by several years.

The story of Galois's mathematical output is tangled with political turmoil. He was a passionate republican in the years after the 1830 revolution, was twice imprisoned, and was rejected twice by the École Polytechnique. His mathematical manuscripts were submitted to the Paris Academy; [Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy) initially agreed to present one but then did not follow through, and [Fourier](/mathematicians/fourier) received another but died before acting on it. A third, submitted to Poisson, was returned as "incomprehensible."

He died in a duel on 31 May 1832, aged 20. The night before, he wrote a long letter to his friend Auguste Chevalier, summarising his mathematical results and annotating manuscripts he had been developing over the preceding years. The letter is not the origin of his mathematics — it is a summary of work already done — but it is the document through which much of his theory was eventually transmitted to mathematicians who could develop it.

[Liouville](/mathematicians/liouville) published Galois's manuscripts in 1846, fourteen years after his death. The theory was then recognised as one of the most beautiful and powerful in mathematics.

**Normal subgroups** — those for which left and right cosets coincide — are fundamental to his construction. **Galois extensions**, **Galois correspondence** (between intermediate fields and subgroups), and the concept of a **solvable group** all bear his name.
