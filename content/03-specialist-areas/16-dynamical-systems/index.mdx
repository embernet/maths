A dynamical system is a rule for how a state evolves over time. The rule can be a differential equation, a map, or something more abstract — but in every case, the question is not just *what* happens, but *why*: which states are stable, what happens near equilibrium, and whether small changes in initial conditions lead to small or catastrophically large differences later.

## Phase space and phase portraits

The state of an autonomous ODE system $\dot{x} = f(x)$, $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, is a point in **phase space** $\mathbb{R}^n$. The function $f$ defines a **vector field** — at each point it assigns a velocity vector. Solutions are curves (trajectories) in phase space that are everywhere tangent to $f$.

A **phase portrait** is the picture of these trajectories. For a two-dimensional system $(\dot{x}, \dot{y}) = (f(x,y), g(x,y))$, the portrait can often be sketched qualitatively, revealing the global behaviour without solving the equations explicitly. Fixed points, limit cycles, stable and unstable manifolds, and separatrices are the organising features.

## Equilibria and linearisation

An **equilibrium** (or fixed point) $x^*$ satisfies $f(x^*) = 0$. Near $x^*$, linearise: let $u = x - x^*$, so $\dot{u} \approx Df(x^*)\,u$ where $Df$ is the Jacobian matrix. The linearisation governs local behaviour. The eigenvalues of $Df(x^*)$ determine the type of equilibrium:

- All eigenvalues have negative real part: **stable node or spiral** — trajectories approach $x^*$.
- All have positive real part: **unstable node or spiral** — trajectories depart.
- Mixed signs: **saddle point** — stable manifold approaches, unstable manifold departs.
- Pure imaginary eigenvalues: the linearisation gives a **centre**; the nonlinear system may spiral in, out, or cycle (needs a Lyapunov analysis).

The **Hartman–Grobman theorem** justifies linearisation: near a hyperbolic equilibrium (no eigenvalues on the imaginary axis), the nonlinear flow is topologically conjugate to the linearised flow. This is the rigorous version of "linearise and understand".

## Lyapunov stability

**Lyapunov's direct method** establishes stability without solving the ODE. A function $V : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is a **Lyapunov function** for the system at $x^*$ if:
1. $V(x^*) = 0$ and $V(x) > 0$ for $x \neq x^*$ (near $x^*$).
2. $\dot{V}(x) = \nabla V(x) \cdot f(x) \le 0$ along trajectories.

If such $V$ exists, $x^*$ is **Lyapunov stable**. If $\dot{V} < 0$ (strictly), then $x^*$ is **asymptotically stable** — trajectories approach $x^*$ as $t \to \infty$.

Intuitively, $V$ is an energy-like function that decreases (or at least doesn't increase) along trajectories. The method is constructive: you must find $V$, but once found, stability follows without integration. For many mechanical systems, $V = \text{kinetic} + \text{potential energy}$ works directly.

## Bifurcation theory

A **bifurcation** occurs when the qualitative behaviour of a system changes as a parameter $\mu$ varies. The three most common local bifurcations:

- **Saddle-node bifurcation**: two equilibria collide and annihilate as $\mu$ changes. Normal form: $\dot{x} = \mu - x^2$. For $\mu > 0$, two fixed points at $\pm\sqrt{\mu}$; at $\mu = 0$, one degenerate; for $\mu < 0$, none.

- **Transcritical bifurcation**: two equilibria exchange stability. Normal form: $\dot{x} = \mu x - x^2$.

- **Hopf bifurcation**: a stable equilibrium loses stability and a **limit cycle** (closed periodic orbit) appears. Normal form (in $\mathbb{R}^2$): a pair of complex eigenvalues of the Jacobian crosses the imaginary axis as $\mu$ increases through zero. The Hopf bifurcation explains the onset of oscillation in many physical and biological systems — a linear instability triggers a nonlinear periodic solution.

Bifurcation diagrams plot the position and stability of equilibria as functions of $\mu$, revealing how qualitative structure changes.

## Deterministic chaos

In 1963, Edward Lorenz simplified a fluid convection model to the three-dimensional system:

$$\dot{x} = \sigma(y - x), \quad \dot{y} = x(\rho - z) - y, \quad \dot{z} = xy - \beta z.$$

For parameter values $\sigma = 10$, $\rho = 28$, $\beta = 8/3$, numerical simulation revealed a **strange attractor**: trajectories remain bounded but never repeat, and two nearby initial conditions diverge exponentially. This is **sensitive dependence on initial conditions** — the defining property of a chaotic system.

The Lorenz attractor is not a fixed point or a limit cycle but a fractal object in phase space: a set of non-integer Hausdorff dimension to which almost all trajectories are attracted. The practical consequence is that long-term prediction is impossible even for a completely deterministic system: small errors in specifying the initial state grow exponentially, eventually dominating any prediction.

[Henri Poincaré](/mathematicians/poincare) had anticipated this possibility in his study of the three-body problem in celestial mechanics, but the computing power to visualise and study chaotic attractors directly only arrived in the 1960s.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [weather forecasting](/applications/weather-forecasting) is inherently limited by chaos — the atmosphere is governed by deterministic fluid equations, but the Lyapunov exponent of the system means that forecasts beyond roughly ten days are unreliable regardless of computational power. [Climate modelling](/applications/climate-modelling) operates on much longer time scales where statistical properties of the attractor (rather than individual trajectories) become predictable. [Epidemiological modelling](/applications/epidemiological-modelling) — SIR models and their descendants — uses phase portrait analysis to determine whether an epidemic equilibrates, oscillates, or grows. [Population dynamics](/applications/population-dynamics-ecology) and predator-prey systems (Lotka–Volterra) exhibit bifurcations and limit cycles that are directly visible in field data.

## Why this matters

Dynamical systems theory is the meeting point of geometry, analysis, and physics. Its central insight — that a system described by simple deterministic rules can behave in ways that are computationally irreducible over long times — is one of the most important discoveries of twentieth-century science. Chaos is not noise; it is structure so sensitive that it mimics noise.

The qualitative methods introduced here — phase portraits, Lyapunov functions, bifurcation analysis — replace the brute-force integration of ODEs with geometric reasoning about the shape of solution spaces. [Poincaré](/mathematicians/poincare) recognised that asking the right qualitative question is often more powerful than trying to write down an explicit solution: his invention of the phase portrait and his work on the three-body problem laid the foundations for what is now an entire discipline.
