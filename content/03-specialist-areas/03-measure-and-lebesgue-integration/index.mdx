The Riemann integral, rigorous as it is, has a fundamental weakness: it handles limits badly. The limit of a sequence of Riemann-integrable functions need not be Riemann integrable. Passing limits through integrals requires uncomfortable additional conditions. And Dirichlet's function (1 on rationals, 0 on irrationals) is not Riemann integrable at all, despite being "mostly" zero.

Henri Lebesgue's 1902 doctoral thesis fixed all of this. The key idea is subtle but powerful: instead of partitioning the domain (as Riemann does), partition the range.

## Sigma-algebras and measures

A **$\sigma$-algebra** on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{F}$ of subsets of $X$ satisfying:
1. $X \in \mathcal{F}$.
2. If $A \in \mathcal{F}$, then $A^c \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under complements).
3. If $A_1, A_2, \ldots \in \mathcal{F}$, then $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty A_n \in \mathcal{F}$ (closed under countable unions).

A **measure** $\mu : \mathcal{F} \to [0, \infty]$ satisfies $\mu(\emptyset) = 0$ and countable additivity: $\mu(\bigsqcup A_n) = \sum \mu(A_n)$ for pairwise disjoint sets $A_n$.

The **Borel $\sigma$-algebra** on $\mathbb{R}$ is the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all open intervals. The **Lebesgue measure** $\lambda$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is the unique measure with $\lambda([a,b]) = b - a$ — it assigns to every interval its length. The Lebesgue measure of the rationals $\lambda(\mathbb{Q}) = 0$ (countable union of measure-zero singletons).

## Measurable functions and the Lebesgue integral

A function $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is **measurable** if $\{x : f(x) > t\} \in \mathcal{F}$ for every $t$. This is a mild condition: all continuous functions, all monotone functions, and all pointwise limits of measurable functions are measurable.

The Lebesgue integral is built in stages:
1. **Indicator functions**: $\int \mathbf{1}_A\, d\mu = \mu(A)$.
2. **Simple functions** (finite linear combinations of indicators): $\int \sum a_i \mathbf{1}_{A_i}\, d\mu = \sum a_i \mu(A_i)$.
3. **Non-negative measurable functions**: $\int f\, d\mu = \sup\{\int s\, d\mu : 0 \le s \le f,\ s \text{ simple}\}$.
4. **General functions**: split into $f = f^+ - f^-$ and integrate positive and negative parts separately.

For Lebesgue-integrable functions ($\int |f|\, d\mu < \infty$), the integral agrees with the Riemann integral whenever the latter exists. But the Lebesgue integral handles far more functions.

## The convergence theorems

These are why Lebesgue integration matters for analysis and probability:

**Monotone Convergence Theorem**: if $0 \le f_1 \le f_2 \le \cdots$ and $f_n \to f$ pointwise, then $\int f_n \to \int f$.

**Dominated Convergence Theorem**: if $f_n \to f$ pointwise and $|f_n| \le g$ for some integrable dominating function $g$, then $\int f_n \to \int f$.

These theorems justify passing limits through integral signs under very mild conditions — something the Riemann theory cannot do without painful extra hypotheses.

**Fubini's theorem**: for non-negative or integrable $f(x,y)$,

$$\int \int f(x,y)\, dx\, dy = \int\!\int f(x,y)\, dy\, dx.$$

The order of integration can be swapped — automatically, without conditions on the region of integration (beyond measurability).

## Lp spaces

For $1 \le p < \infty$, the **$L^p$ space** consists of measurable functions with $\int |f|^p\, d\mu < \infty$, equipped with the norm $\|f\|_p = (\int |f|^p)^{1/p}$. These are Banach spaces. $L^2$ is a Hilbert space and the natural setting for Fourier analysis.

The **Hölder inequality**: $\int |fg|\, d\mu \le \|f\|_p \|g\|_q$ for $1/p + 1/q = 1$.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [Option pricing](/applications/option-pricing-finance) and all of stochastic finance require Lebesgue integration — the Itô integral (the stochastic integral at the heart of Black-Scholes) is defined using measure-theoretic tools unavailable in the Riemann framework. [Bayesian machine learning](/applications/machine-learning-bayesian-inference) integrates over high-dimensional parameter spaces using Lebesgue measure; without the dominated convergence theorem, passing limits through integrals in MCMC convergence proofs would be unjustifiable. The actuarial expected present values for [pension fund management](/applications/actuarial-science) are Lebesgue integrals over lifetime distributions.

## Measure-theoretic probability

With measure theory, probability becomes a special case: a probability space is $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ where $P(\Omega) = 1$. Random variables are measurable functions; expectations are Lebesgue integrals. [Kolmogorov's](/mathematicians/kolmogorov) 1933 axiomatisation of probability is precisely this framework. The strong law of large numbers, martingale convergence theorems, and the construction of Brownian motion all depend on Lebesgue-measure tools that have no Riemann-integral counterpart.
