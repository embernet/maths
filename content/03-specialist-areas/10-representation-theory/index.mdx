A representation of a group turns its abstract symmetry into something you can compute with — matrices acting on a vector space — and the astonishing fact is that every finite group can be completely understood by studying a small, finite list of these matrix representations.

## Representations and homomorphisms

Let $G$ be a group and $V$ a vector space over a field $k$. A **representation** of $G$ on $V$ is a group homomorphism

$$\rho : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V),$$

where $\mathrm{GL}(V)$ denotes the group of invertible linear maps from $V$ to itself. Each group element $g$ acts on $V$ via the invertible linear map $\rho(g)$, and the homomorphism condition ensures that $\rho(gh) = \rho(g)\rho(h)$.

If $\dim V = n < \infty$, choosing a basis expresses $\rho$ as a group homomorphism into the matrix group $\mathrm{GL}_n(k)$. The dimension $n$ is the **degree** of the representation.

## Irreducible representations and Maschke's theorem

A subspace $W \subseteq V$ is **$G$-invariant** if $\rho(g)W \subseteq W$ for every $g \in G$. This gives a representation on $W$ (a **subrepresentation**). A representation is **irreducible** if it has no proper non-zero $G$-invariant subspaces.

**Maschke's theorem**: if $G$ is a finite group and $\mathrm{char}(k)$ does not divide $|G|$ (in particular, if $k = \mathbb{C}$), then every representation of $G$ decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles. The representation theory over $\mathbb{C}$ is therefore **semisimple**: every finite-dimensional representation is completely determined by knowing how many times each irreducible appears.

This makes the problem of understanding all representations equivalent to classifying all irreducibles — a finite problem, since there are exactly as many irreducible representations (up to isomorphism) as there are conjugacy classes in $G$.

## Schur's lemma

**Schur's lemma** is the key structural result. Let $\rho_1 : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V_1)$ and $\rho_2 : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V_2)$ be irreducible representations, and let $T : V_1 \to V_2$ be a linear map intertwining the actions ($T\rho_1(g) = \rho_2(g)T$ for all $g \in G$). Then:

1. Either $T = 0$ or $T$ is an isomorphism.
2. If $V_1 = V_2 = V$ and $k = \mathbb{C}$, then $T = \lambda I$ for some scalar $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$.

The second part is the powerful one: over $\mathbb{C}$, the only endomorphisms of an irreducible representation are scalar multiples of the identity. This has immediate consequences — it restricts what maps can exist between representations and forces the character table to be square.

## Characters and the character table

The **character** of a representation $\rho$ is the function $\chi_\rho : G \to \mathbb{C}$ defined by

$$\chi_\rho(g) = \mathrm{tr}\,\rho(g).$$

Characters are constant on conjugacy classes (class functions), so they are determined by their values on conjugacy class representatives. The character table of $G$ is the square matrix with rows indexed by irreducible representations and columns by conjugacy classes.

**Orthogonality relations**: the irreducible characters $\chi_1, \ldots, \chi_r$ form an orthonormal basis for the space of class functions with respect to the inner product

$$\langle \chi, \psi \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g \in G} \chi(g)\overline{\psi(g)}.$$

In particular, $\langle \chi_i, \chi_j \rangle = \delta_{ij}$. This means: two representations are isomorphic if and only if they have the same character; and the multiplicity with which the irreducible $\rho_i$ appears in any representation $\rho$ is exactly $\langle \chi_\rho, \chi_i \rangle$.

## A worked example: $S_3$

The symmetric group $S_3$ has three conjugacy classes: $\{e\}$, $\{(12),(13),(23)\}$, $\{(123),(132)\}$. It therefore has exactly three irreducible representations:

| Rep | Degree | $e$ | $(12)$ | $(123)$ |
|-----|--------|-----|--------|---------|
| trivial $\rho_1$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| sign $\rho_2$ | 1 | 1 | $-1$ | 1 |
| standard $\rho_3$ | 2 | 2 | 0 | $-1$ |

The sum of squares of degrees is $1^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 6 = |S_3|$ — a constraint that holds for any finite group. The standard representation on $\mathbb{C}^2$ is the action by permutation matrices restricted to the orthogonal complement of the all-ones vector.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [quantum computing](/applications/quantum-computing-algorithms) relies on representation theory — the irreducible representations of the unitary group classify how quantum systems transform under symmetry, and Clebsch–Gordan decompositions describe how quantum states combine. [Error-correcting codes](/applications/error-correcting-codes) with algebraic structure exploit group representations to make decoding tractable. In physics, the representation theory of the Poincaré group classifies all elementary particles by mass and spin.

## Why this matters

Representation theory is one of the great unifying tools in modern mathematics. It connects algebra, analysis, and geometry through a single organising idea: a group action on a vector space. The same theory that classifies the irreducible representations of finite groups underlies the representation theory of compact Lie groups (where Peter–Weyl gives a complete decomposition of $L^2(G)$), the classification of elementary particles in physics (where the representations of $SU(2)$ and $SU(3)$ determine particle multiplets), and Langlands' programme (which conjectures deep connections between Galois representations and automorphic forms).

[Emmy Noether](/mathematicians/noether) played a decisive role in abstracting the theory into its modern module-theoretic form. Her theorem that symmetries correspond to conserved quantities can be understood as a statement about representations: a symmetry group acting on a physical system determines, via its representations, which quantities are preserved under the dynamics.
