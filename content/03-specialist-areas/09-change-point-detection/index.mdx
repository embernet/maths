A signal looks stationary for a while, then something happens. The mean shifts, the variance changes, a relationship breaks down. The question of **change point detection** is to decide — given the observations alone — whether such an event occurred, and if so, when. It is one of the oldest problems in applied statistics (Page's CUSUM dates to 1954, motivated by quality control on production lines) and one of the most actively researched today, with applications in genomics, climate science, finance, neuroscience, and any field where a "regime" can change.

## The single change-in-mean problem

Suppose we observe $y_1, y_2, \ldots, y_n$ generated by
$$y_t = \mu_t + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0, \sigma^2).$$

The null hypothesis is that the mean is constant: $\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \cdots = \mu_n$. The alternative is that there is a single **change point** $\tau \in \{1, \ldots, n-1\}$ such that
$$\mu_1 = \cdots = \mu_\tau = \mu^{(1)} \quad \text{and} \quad \mu_{\tau+1} = \cdots = \mu_n = \mu^{(2)},$$
with $\mu^{(1)} \neq \mu^{(2)}$. We want to test the null against the alternative and, if we reject, estimate $\tau$.

## The CUSUM statistic

Fix a candidate change point $\tau$. The natural test statistic compares the mean of the first $\tau$ observations to the mean of the last $n - \tau$:
$$C_\tau = \sqrt{\frac{\tau(n-\tau)}{n}} \, \bigl| \bar y_{1:\tau} - \bar y_{\tau+1:n} \bigr|,$$
where $\bar y_{l:u}$ denotes the empirical mean of $y_l, \ldots, y_u$. The weighting $\sqrt{\tau(n-\tau)/n}$ is exactly what is needed so that, under the null,
$$\frac{1}{\sigma} \sqrt{\frac{\tau(n-\tau)}{n}} \bigl(\bar y_{1:\tau} - \bar y_{\tau+1:n}\bigr) \sim N(0, 1).$$

Since $\tau$ is unknown, we search over all candidates and use the maximum:
$$C_{\max}^2 = \max_{\tau \in \{1, \ldots, n-1\}} \frac{C_\tau^2}{\sigma^2}, \qquad \hat\tau = \arg\max_\tau \frac{C_\tau^2}{\sigma^2}.$$

We reject the null when $C_{\max}^2$ exceeds a threshold $c$. The threshold is chosen so that the test has a prescribed significance level; under the null the distribution of $C_{\max}^2$ converges to that of the supremum of a Brownian bridge, a result that goes back to Darling and Erdős.

A naive evaluation of $C_\tau$ at every $\tau$ is $O(n^2)$ because each $\bar y_{1:\tau}$ would seem to need a fresh sum. But by precomputing the cumulative sums $S_\tau = \sum_{i=1}^\tau y_i$, both means become $O(1)$ updates:
$$\bar y_{1:\tau} = \frac{S_\tau}{\tau}, \qquad \bar y_{\tau+1:n} = \frac{S_n - S_\tau}{n - \tau}.$$
The whole scan is then $O(n)$.

## Page's procedure: detection in the online setting

Page (1954) was working at ICI on industrial quality control: items roll off a production line and you want to *raise an alarm as quickly as possible* the moment something drifts off-target. This is the **online** (sequential) version of the problem — data arrive one at a time, and a decision must be made at each step.

Page's CUSUM statistic for detecting an upward shift from $\mu_0$ to $\mu_0 + \delta$ (with $\delta > 0$ known) is
$$g_t = \max\bigl(0,\, g_{t-1} + (y_t - \mu_0 - \tfrac{\delta}{2})\bigr), \qquad g_0 = 0.$$
Under the in-control distribution, $g_t$ drifts down and is repeatedly pulled back to zero. After a true shift, the increment becomes positively biased and $g_t$ accumulates rapidly. The alarm time is
$$T = \inf\{ t : g_t > h \}$$
for some threshold $h$. Two-sided detection runs two such statistics in parallel — one for upward, one for downward shifts.

The performance of an online detector is measured by two quantities in tension:
- **Average run length to false alarm**, $\mathrm{ARL}_0$ — expected time to an alarm under the null.
- **Detection delay**, $\mathrm{ARL}_1$ — expected delay after a true change.

Larger $h$ raises $\mathrm{ARL}_0$ (fewer false alarms) but also raises detection delay. The choice is a classical Neyman–Pearson-style trade-off, and Page's CUSUM is **optimal** in the Lorden minimax sense: it minimises the worst-case detection delay for a given $\mathrm{ARL}_0$, a result due to Moustakides (1986).

## Multiple change points

When several changes may occur, the natural model is
$$\mu_t = \mu^{(k)} \text{ for } t \in [\tau_{k-1}+1, \tau_k], \qquad 0 = \tau_0 < \tau_1 < \cdots < \tau_K = n,$$
with $K$ also unknown. Estimation reduces to minimising a penalised cost
$$\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal{C}(y_{\tau_{k-1}+1:\tau_k}) + \beta f(K),$$
where $\mathcal{C}$ is a within-segment cost (negative log-likelihood for the Gaussian model) and $\beta f(K)$ penalises the number of segments — typically BIC ($\beta = \log n$) or a modified information criterion.

Three algorithms dominate practice:

- **Binary segmentation.** Find the best single change point via CUSUM; if significant, split the series at it and recurse on each half. Fast ($O(n \log n)$) but greedy and prone to under-estimating $K$.
- **Wild binary segmentation** (Fryzlewicz, 2014). Run binary segmentation on randomly drawn sub-intervals, gaining sensitivity to small changes against long backgrounds.
- **PELT** — *Pruned Exact Linear Time* (Killick, Fearnhead, Eckley, 2012). A dynamic-programming approach that returns the exact optimiser of the penalised cost in expected $O(n)$ time under mild conditions on the data. PELT is the default in the `changepoint` R package and is widely used in genomics and finance.

## Beyond changes in mean

The same scaffolding extends:

- **Change in variance.** Replace the mean test with a likelihood-ratio test for $\sigma_1^2 \neq \sigma_2^2$.
- **Change in regression coefficients.** For $y_t = x_t^T \beta_t + \varepsilon_t$, detect $\beta^{(1)} \neq \beta^{(2)}$. This is the **structural break** problem in econometrics; the Chow and Quandt-Andrews tests are the classical tools.
- **Distribution-free detection.** Tests based on empirical distribution functions or kernel statistics (e.g. $E$-divergence) detect any distributional shift, not just a mean shift.
- **Multivariate and high-dimensional.** Modern work (Wang and Samworth, 2018, *inspect*; Cho, 2016) detects sparse changes in a small subset of coordinates of a high-dimensional time series — relevant for genomics and sensor networks.

## A worked example

Suppose $n = 100$ observations with $\sigma = 1$, true change at $\tau = 60$, from $\mu^{(1)} = 0$ to $\mu^{(2)} = 1.2$. Under the alternative, $C_\tau$ for $\tau = 60$ has expected value $\sqrt{60 \cdot 40 / 100}\,|1.2 - 0| = \sqrt{24}\cdot 1.2 \approx 5.88$, against a null distribution whose 95% quantile (under the supremum-of-Brownian-bridge limit) is around 2.5. The detection is therefore very confident even without a long run.

For a small shift — say $\mu^{(2)} = 0.3$ — the expected statistic falls to roughly $\sqrt{24}\cdot 0.3 \approx 1.47$, well below the threshold. The take-away: **detectability scales with $|\delta|\sqrt{n}$**, the same square-root law that governs hypothesis testing throughout statistics.

## Connections elsewhere in this guide

Change point detection draws on several threads:

- The CUSUM statistic is the discrete analogue of a Brownian bridge under the null, linking it directly to [stochastic processes](/03-specialist-areas/07-stochastic-processes).
- Page's procedure is the prototype of **sequential analysis**, the field that Wald developed in wartime quality control and that underpins modern A/B testing and online experimentation.
- Penalised likelihood for multiple change points is a special case of [model selection](/02-core-mathematics/05-probability-and-statistical-inference), with the segmentation playing the role of the model.
- In [machine learning](/97-python/06-machine-learning), change point detection is a tool for non-stationary stream learning and for splitting data before training; Bayesian online change point detection (Adams and MacKay, 2007) is the workhorse method.

## Applications

The methods generalise far beyond their quality-control origins:

- **Genomics.** Detecting copy-number variation along a chromosome — segments where DNA is amplified or deleted, often a marker of cancer.
- **Climate science.** Identifying regime shifts in long temperature, pressure, or ice-core records.
- **Finance.** Detecting structural breaks in returns or volatility; volatility-regime detection underpins risk models.
- **Neuroscience and biomedical signals.** Segmenting EEG, ECG, or fMRI traces into stationary regimes.
- **Network monitoring and cybersecurity.** Detecting traffic-volume anomalies or new attack patterns in real time.
- **Operational monitoring.** From CPU temperature on a server to engine vibration on a turbine — any sensor stream whose distribution should not drift.
