A **stochastic process** is a collection of random variables $\{X_t\}_{t \ge 0}$ (or $\{X_n\}_{n \ge 0}$) indexed by time. The process describes a system whose state evolves randomly: the position of a molecule in a fluid, the number of customers in a queue, the price of a financial asset. The theory of stochastic processes is one of the great achievements of twentieth-century mathematics.

## Markov chains in discrete time

A discrete-time Markov chain has the **Markov property**: future behaviour depends on the present state only, not on the history of how it arrived there. Formally:

$$P(X_{n+1} = j \mid X_n = i, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}, \ldots) = p_{ij}.$$

The transition probabilities $p_{ij}$ form the **transition matrix** $P$. After $n$ steps, $P^n$ gives the $n$-step transition probabilities.

**Classification of states**: a state is **recurrent** if the chain returns to it with probability 1; **transient** otherwise. A recurrent state is **positive recurrent** if the expected return time is finite. An **irreducible** chain (every state reachable from every other) on a finite state space is positive recurrent.

The **stationary distribution** $\pi = (\pi_i)$ satisfies $\pi P = \pi$ (and $\sum \pi_i = 1$). For an irreducible aperiodic finite chain, $P^n \to \mathbf{1}\pi^T$ as $n \to \infty$: the chain forgets its initial state and converges to $\pi$.

## Continuous-time Markov chains

Continuous-time processes $\{X_t\}_{t \ge 0}$ on a discrete state space hold in each state for a random time (exponentially distributed, memoryless) and then jump. The **generator matrix** $Q = (q_{ij})$ encodes the rates: $q_{ij}$ is the rate at which the chain jumps from $i$ to $j$.

The **Kolmogorov equations** describe how the distribution $\pi_t$ evolves:

$$\frac{d\pi_t}{dt} = \pi_t Q \quad \text{(forward)} \qquad \frac{d}{dt} P_t = Q P_t \quad \text{(backward).}$$

Key examples: the Poisson process (arrivals at constant rate $\lambda$); birth-death processes (queueing, population models); branching processes.

## Martingales

A stochastic process $\{M_n\}$ is a **martingale** with respect to a filtration $\{\mathcal{F}_n\}$ if:
1. $M_n$ is $\mathcal{F}_n$-measurable (adapted).
2. $E[|M_n|] < \infty$.
3. $E[M_{n+1} \mid \mathcal{F}_n] = M_n$ (no bias — a fair game).

Martingales generalise the concept of a "fair game": on average, you cannot gain or lose by playing. The simplest example: a symmetric random walk $S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n$ with $P(X_i = \pm 1) = 1/2$ is a martingale.

The **optional stopping theorem**: if $\tau$ is a stopping time satisfying mild regularity conditions, then $E[M_\tau] = E[M_0]$. This says you cannot improve your expected winnings in a fair game by choosing a clever stopping rule — a formalisation of the futility of gambling strategies.

**Martingale convergence theorem**: a martingale bounded in $L^1$ converges almost surely to a limit. This is a deep result used in the proofs of the strong law of large numbers and many ergodic theorems.

## Brownian motion

**Brownian motion** (also called the Wiener process) $\{W_t\}_{t \ge 0}$ is the continuous-time limit of the random walk:
- $W_0 = 0$.
- Independent increments: $W_t - W_s \perp W_s - W_r$ for $r < s < t$.
- Stationary increments: $W_t - W_s \sim N(0, t-s)$.
- Almost surely continuous paths.

It is a martingale; also $W_t^2 - t$ is a martingale.

Brownian motion has unbounded variation on every interval (its paths are nowhere differentiable) and quadratic variation $[W]_t = t$. These properties require a new calculus — **Itô calculus** — in which the chain rule becomes **Itô's lemma**:

$$dF(W_t) = F'(W_t)\, dW_t + \tfrac{1}{2} F''(W_t)\, dt.$$

The extra $\frac{1}{2}F'' dt$ term has no ordinary-calculus counterpart; it arises from the non-zero quadratic variation.

Itô's lemma is the foundation of the Black-Scholes model for option pricing, where the asset price follows geometric Brownian motion and the pricing PDE is derived using this lemma. Stochastic processes and their rigorous treatment via measure theory is where analysis, probability, and finance converge.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [Option pricing and derivatives](/applications/option-pricing-finance) depend directly on the Itô calculus and geometric Brownian motion in this course — Black-Scholes is a direct consequence of the material in the Brownian motion section, and every quantitative finance desk in the world uses extensions of this theory. [PageRank](/applications/pagerank-google) is the stationary distribution of the Markov chain formed by the web's link structure, computed using exactly the convergence results for irreducible aperiodic chains that you prove here. [Actuarial science](/applications/actuarial-science) models policyholder lifetimes and claims arrivals using continuous-time Markov chains and compound Poisson processes — the tools that determine the premium you pay for life insurance and pensions.
