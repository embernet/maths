Probability is the one area of Year One mathematics where your intuition is most likely to be confidently, spectacularly wrong. The Monty Hall problem, the Birthday Paradox, Simpson's Paradox — these are not tricks or puzzles; they are demonstrations that human intuition about probability is systematically unreliable. The cure is a formal framework that does not rely on intuition at all.

## The probability space

A **probability space** has three components: a sample space $\Omega$ (the set of all possible outcomes), an event space $\mathcal{F}$ (a collection of subsets of $\Omega$ to which we assign probabilities), and a probability measure $P : \mathcal{F} \to [0,1]$.

The measure $P$ must satisfy the **Kolmogorov axioms**:
1. $P(A) \ge 0$ for all events $A$.
2. $P(\Omega) = 1$.
3. For pairwise disjoint events $A_1, A_2, \ldots$: $P\!\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty P(A_i)$.

That third axiom — countable additivity — is the key one. Everything else in probability theory follows from these three axioms. [Kolmogorov](/mathematicians/kolmogorov) established this framework in 1933, finally placing probability theory on the same rigorous footing as analysis.

From the axioms, you immediately derive: $P(\emptyset) = 0$; $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$; $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$ (inclusion-exclusion).

## Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

The **conditional probability** of $A$ given $B$ (with $P(B) > 0$) is:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}.$$

This is the probability that $A$ occurs, knowing that $B$ has occurred. The formula redistributes probability over the reduced sample space $B$.

**Bayes' theorem**, published posthumously in 1763 by the English clergyman Thomas [Bayes](/mathematicians/bayes) and independently derived by [Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace), is:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\, P(A)}{P(B)}.$$

In practice: you observe $B$ (the data); you want to know $P(A \mid B)$ (the probability that hypothesis $A$ is true given the data); you need the likelihood $P(B \mid A)$ and the prior $P(A)$. This is the foundation of Bayesian statistics.

The **law of total probability** provides the denominator: if $\{A_i\}$ is a partition of $\Omega$, then $P(B) = \sum_i P(B \mid A_i)\, P(A_i)$.

## Combinatorics

Counting is the first tool of discrete probability. The key results:

- **Permutations**: ordered arrangements of $r$ from $n$ objects: $P(n,r) = n!/(n-r)!$.
- **Combinations**: unordered selections of $r$ from $n$: $\binom{n}{r} = n!/(r!(n-r)!)$.
- **Multinomial coefficient**: $\binom{n}{k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_m} = n!/(k_1! k_2! \cdots k_m!)$ counts ways to partition $n$ objects into groups of sizes $k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_m$.
- **Inclusion-exclusion**: $|A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n|$ computed by alternating sums of intersections.

## Random variables and distributions

A **random variable** $X$ is a function $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ that maps outcomes to numbers. It is "random" only in the sense that its value depends on which outcome in $\Omega$ is realised.

**Discrete** random variables take values in a countable set; their distribution is described by the **probability mass function** $P(X = k)$.

**Continuous** random variables take values in an interval; their distribution is described by the **probability density function** $f(x)$, where $P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b f(x)\, dx$.

Standard distributions you will use throughout the degree:

| Distribution | Parameter(s) | Mean | Variance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Binomial $B(n,p)$ | $n, p$ | $np$ | $np(1-p)$ | Number of successes in $n$ independent trials |
| Poisson $\text{Po}(\lambda)$ | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ | $\lambda$ | Limit of $B(n,\lambda/n)$ as $n \to \infty$ |
| Uniform $U(a,b)$ | $a, b$ | $(a+b)/2$ | $(b-a)^2/12$ | Constant density on $[a,b]$ |
| Normal $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ | $\mu, \sigma^2$ | $\mu$ | $\sigma^2$ | The bell curve; ubiquitous by the CLT |
| Exponential $\text{Exp}(\lambda)$ | $\lambda$ | $1/\lambda$ | $1/\lambda^2$ | Memoryless; waiting time for Poisson events |

The **expectation** of a random variable $X$ is $E[X] = \sum_k k\, P(X=k)$ (discrete) or $\int_{-\infty}^\infty x\, f(x)\, dx$ (continuous). Expectation is linear: $E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y]$.

The **variance** is $\text{Var}(X) = E[(X - \mu)^2] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$.

## The Central Limit Theorem

The most important theorem in statistics:

> Let $X_1, X_2, \ldots$ be independent, identically distributed random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2 < \infty$. Then:
> $$\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

No matter what distribution the $X_i$ come from — uniform, exponential, Poisson, anything — the standardised sample mean converges in distribution to the standard normal. This explains why the normal distribution appears everywhere: it is the universal limiting shape of sums of independent random variables.

The CLT was first proved for the Binomial distribution by de Moivre in 1733. [Laplace](/mathematicians/laplace) gave a more general version. The full proof covering all distributions with finite variance was completed by Lindeberg in 1922.

## Introduction to statistical inference

Statistics asks the inverse question to probability: given observed data, what can we say about the underlying distribution? Year One introduces the two main frameworks.

**Confidence intervals**: estimate an unknown parameter with an interval that covers the true value with specified probability. A 95% confidence interval for the mean of a Normal population with known variance is $\bar{X} \pm 1.96\, \sigma/\sqrt{n}$.

**Hypothesis testing**: state a null hypothesis $H_0$ (e.g., "the coin is fair"), compute a test statistic, and reject $H_0$ if the statistic falls in the critical region. The $t$-test compares a sample mean to a hypothesised value when population variance is unknown; the test statistic $t = (\bar{X} - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})$ follows a $t$-distribution under $H_0$.

**Maximum likelihood estimation** ([Fisher's](/mathematicians/fisher) contribution): find the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable. For a sample $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ from a distribution with density $f(x; \theta)$, maximise the likelihood $L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i; \theta)$ — or equivalently, the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log f(x_i; \theta)$.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [Medical diagnosis and screening](/applications/medical-diagnosis-screening) applies Bayes' theorem to calculate how likely you actually are to have a disease given a positive test — the counter-intuitive false-positive paradox follows directly from the conditional probability formula in this course. [Spam filters](/applications/spam-filtering) classify emails using naïve Bayes, multiplying the conditional probabilities you learn here. [Clinical trials](/applications/clinical-trials-design) use the hypothesis-testing framework and power calculations built on the normal distribution to determine whether a drug works.

## Where this leads

In Year Two, probability becomes more rigorous: measure-theoretic probability provides the foundation for Markov chains and stochastic processes (Year Two and Three). Statistical inference deepens into estimation theory, the Cramér-Rao bound, and the Neyman-Pearson framework. Stochastic processes in Year Three underpin financial mathematics, and Bayesian statistics (also Year Three) extends Bayes' theorem to full statistical modelling. The probability and statistics thread runs through the entire degree.
