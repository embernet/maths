The rigour revolution put analysis on firm foundations. The structural turn did something conceptually different: it discovered that the most powerful mathematics is about *structure*, not content. A group is not a specific set of symmetries; it is the abstract pattern of symmetry relations. A ring is not the integers or the polynomials; it is any system satisfying certain axioms. The specific objects don't matter — what matters is how the objects relate to each other.

## Galois and the algebra of symmetry

[Évariste Galois](/mathematicians/galois) (1811–1832) came from a politically turbulent background and spent time in prison for his republican activities. He had developed, over several years of intense work, a theory connecting the solubility of polynomial equations to the structure of their symmetry groups — groups that permute the roots of the polynomial.

The night before his duel (29 May 1832), Galois wrote a letter to his friend Auguste Chevalier, revising and annotating the theory he had built up over preceding years, adding notes and new results in the margins. This letter is remarkable, but it was the culmination of years of work, not the invention of a theory in a single night. He died the following day, aged twenty.

His manuscripts were preserved and published by Liouville in 1846, and Galois theory became one of the most beautiful areas of mathematics: a perfect correspondence between field extensions and group structure, explaining exactly which polynomial equations are solvable and which are not.

[Niels Henrik Abel](/mathematicians/abel) had proved six years earlier (Abel published in 1826; Galois's work was developed 1830–32) that the general quintic has no radical solution. Abel and Galois solved different but complementary problems: Abel proved impossibility; Galois characterised the boundary between possible and impossible.

## Cayley and the abstract group

[Arthur Cayley](/mathematicians/cayley) (1821–1895) spent fourteen years as a lawyer before being appointed to a mathematics professorship — remarkably producing over 250 mathematical papers while practising at the bar. He gave the first modern abstract definition of a group (1854): a set of symbols with a composition rule satisfying three axioms. He also proved Cayley's theorem: every group is isomorphic to a permutation group.

## Noether and the axiomatisation of algebra

[Emmy Noether](/mathematicians/noether) (1882–1935) completed the structural turn. She restructured algebra completely around the concepts of rings, ideals, and homomorphisms, providing the abstract framework that unified disparate results across algebra. The First Isomorphism Theorem is attributed to her.

She lectured at Göttingen under Hilbert's name because women were not permitted to teach at German universities. Despite this, she became one of the most influential mathematicians of her generation. In 1933, the Nazi government expelled Jewish academics from German universities; Noether emigrated to the United States and took a position at Bryn Mawr College. Einstein, in her obituary, called her "the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced, so far as women are concerned." The qualifier was unworthy; she was among the greatest of any gender.

Noether's theorem in physics (a separate result from her algebra) states that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conservation law: translational symmetry gives conservation of momentum; rotational symmetry gives conservation of angular momentum. It is one of the most profound results in theoretical physics.

## The legacy of the structural turn

The abstract, structural style that Galois, Cayley, and Noether built became the dominant mode of pure mathematics in the twentieth century. The Bourbaki group in France — a collective of mathematicians writing anonymously as "Nicolas Bourbaki" — wrote a multi-volume textbook of mathematics in purely structural terms, and their approach shaped the teaching of mathematics worldwide for decades.
