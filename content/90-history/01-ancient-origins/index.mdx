The oldest mathematical documents we have are clay tablets from Mesopotamia, written around 1800 BCE. They contain multiplication tables, calculations of square roots, and — most strikingly — what appear to be Pythagorean triples (sets of integers satisfying $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$), listed systematically over a thousand years before Pythagoras was born. Egyptian mathematics, preserved on the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (c.1650 BCE), records methods for calculating areas of fields and volumes of granaries. Both traditions were intensely practical: the goal was to get the right answer, not to understand why the method worked.

The Greeks changed this. Sometime around 600 BCE, Thales of Miletus reportedly provided the first deductive proofs of geometric theorems — results derived by logical argument from previously established facts rather than by measurement or calculation. The idea that you could *prove* a mathematical claim — that you could establish it with certainty for all cases, not just check it in examples — was a Greek invention, and it is one of the most important intellectual innovations in history.

## Pythagoras and irrationality

[Pythagoras](/mathematicians/pythagoras) (c.570–495 BCE) led a mathematical community that treated number as the foundation of reality. The Pythagorean school believed that everything could be expressed as ratios of whole numbers. Geometry, music, and cosmology all resolved into rational proportions.

Then a member of the school (tradition does not agree on who) proved that $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational — that it cannot be expressed as a ratio $p/q$ of integers. The proof by contradiction is one of the most elegant in mathematics: assume $\sqrt{2} = p/q$ in lowest terms; then $2q^2 = p^2$, so $p$ is even, say $p = 2k$; then $2q^2 = 4k^2$, so $q^2 = 2k^2$, so $q$ is even too — contradicting "lowest terms." The Pythagorean worldview collapsed in the face of a single proof.

This is a pattern that repeats throughout the history of mathematics: a discovery that seems to destroy the existing framework turns out to force a deeper and more powerful one. The irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ forced mathematicians to confront the nature of real numbers — a question not properly resolved until Dedekind's constructions in the 1870s.

## Euclid's Elements

[Euclid](/mathematicians/euclid) (c.300 BCE) wrote the *Elements* — thirteen books presenting all of Greek geometry and number theory from five postulates, through hundreds of propositions derived by pure logical deduction. The *Elements* is one of the most influential books ever written: it was the standard textbook of mathematics for over two thousand years, and the model for rigorous axiomatic argument that mathematics still follows.

The famous fifth postulate (the parallel postulate) states — roughly — that through any point not on a line there is exactly one parallel line. Mathematicians tried for two millennia to derive this from the other four postulates, before Bolyai and Lobachevsky showed in the 1820s that denying it gives consistent non-Euclidean geometries. The question of whether the parallel postulate is independent of the others turned out to be one of the deepest in the foundations of mathematics.

## Archimedes and the method of exhaustion

[Archimedes](/mathematicians/archimedes) (c.287–212 BCE) was, by some measures, the greatest mathematician of antiquity. He computed $\pi$ to two decimal places using inscribed and circumscribed polygons; he determined the area of a parabolic segment; and he calculated volumes of spheres and cylinders. His method used what Eudoxus had formalised as the **method of exhaustion**: approximating a curved region by inscribed rectilinear ones and showing the approximation can be made arbitrarily close to the true area.

The formula for the area of a parabolic segment used a remarkable series sum: $A + A/4 + A/16 + \cdots = 4A/3$, where $A$ is the area of the inscribed triangle. This is the sum of a geometric series — the first example of a convergent infinite sum in mathematics, computed over a millennium before the calculus that would give such computations their proper theoretical framework.

Archimedes was killed by a Roman soldier during the siege of Syracuse in 212 BCE, reportedly while working on a geometry problem in the sand.
