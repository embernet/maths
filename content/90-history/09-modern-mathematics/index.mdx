The twentieth century was the most productive in the history of mathematics. The number of mathematicians, the volume of published work, and the range of interconnections between different parts of mathematics all grew dramatically. It is impossible to summarise it fairly in a few hundred words. Instead, here are some of the most important threads.

## Measure theory and the foundations of probability

[Kolmogorov's](/mathematicians/kolmogorov) 1933 monograph *Foundations of the Theory of Probability* placed probability on a rigorous measure-theoretic foundation, resolving centuries of confusion about what probability "really" is. A probability space is simply a measure space with total measure 1; random variables are measurable functions; expectation is the Lebesgue integral. Every result of classical probability — the law of large numbers, the central limit theorem — became a theorem in measure theory.

## Functional analysis and quantum mechanics

[Stefan Banach](/mathematicians/banach) and the Lwów school developed the theory of Banach spaces and operators in the 1920s and 1930s. [Hilbert](/mathematicians/hilbert) had already introduced Hilbert spaces to study integral equations. John von Neumann (1903–1957) showed that quantum mechanics is the theory of self-adjoint operators on Hilbert spaces — unifying the wave-mechanics of Schrödinger and the matrix mechanics of Heisenberg.

## Hardy, Ramanujan, and analytic number theory

[G.H. Hardy](/mathematicians/hardy) and [Srinivasa Ramanujan](/mathematicians/ramanujan) formed one of the most celebrated collaborations in mathematics. Ramanujan, a self-taught mathematician from Tamil Nadu, sent Hardy a letter in 1913 containing extraordinary formulas for series, continued fractions, and modular forms — many without proofs, many subsequently verified by extremely difficult analysis. Hardy brought him to Cambridge. Ramanujan died in 1920, aged thirty-two.

Hardy's other great contribution was the transformation of British number theory. He trained a generation of analysts who made the Hardy-Littlewood-Ramanujan tradition one of the defining forces in twentieth-century mathematics.

## Chaos and dynamical systems

[Henri Poincaré's](/mathematicians/poincare) late-nineteenth-century work on the three-body problem had already hinted at chaos. Stephen Smale in the 1960s formalised the theory of chaotic dynamical systems. Mitchell Feigenbaum discovered in 1978 the universality constant $\delta \approx 4.669$ in the period-doubling route to chaos — a number that appears in the same form regardless of the specific dynamical rule, one of the most surprising universality results in mathematics.

## Mathematical finance

Fischer Black, Myron Scholes, and Robert Merton developed the Black-Scholes model in 1973: given an asset following geometric Brownian motion, a European option can be priced using a PDE derived via Itô's lemma. The formula $C = S_0 N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$ (where $d_1, d_2$ depend on the parameters of the model) made the theory of derivatives pricing mathematically rigorous. Scholes and Merton received the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economics (Black had died in 1995).

## The Langlands programme and Fermat's Last Theorem

In 1994, [Andrew Wiles](/mathematicians/hardy) proved Fermat's Last Theorem — a problem open for 358 years — by proving a special case of the **Taniyama-Shimura conjecture**: every elliptic curve over $\mathbb{Q}$ is modular. This was part of the Langlands programme: a vast web of conjectured connections between automorphic forms, Galois representations, and L-functions that unifies number theory, representation theory, and algebraic geometry.

## Perelman and the Poincaré conjecture

In 2003, Grigori Perelman posted proofs of the Poincaré conjecture (every simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere) and the full geometrisation conjecture of Thurston. Perelman was awarded the Fields Medal (declined) and the $1,000,000 Clay Millennium Prize (also declined). He had already left academic mathematics.

The mathematics you are studying is part of this living tradition. The theorems you prove in tutorials have lives that stretch back centuries and extend forward into open questions at the research frontier. When you understand Cauchy's theorem, you are joining a conversation with the mathematician himself. When you use Kolmogorov's axioms, you are standing on foundations that were built within living memory.
