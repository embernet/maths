In the summer of 1665, Cambridge University closed because of the plague. [Isaac Newton](/mathematicians/newton), aged twenty-two, retreated to his family home at Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire and over the next eighteen months invented the calculus, the law of universal gravitation, and the foundations of optics. It is perhaps the most productive period of solitary work in the history of science.

Newton called his version of calculus the "method of fluxions." A "fluent" was a flowing quantity; its "fluxion" was its rate of change. The key insight was the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: differentiation and integration are inverse operations. Newton used his methods extensively in his *Principia Mathematica* (1687), which derived Kepler's laws of planetary motion from his law of gravitation and the calculus.

## Leibniz and the notation battle

[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](/mathematicians/leibniz) developed calculus independently in the 1670s, motivated by quite different problems (tangents to curves, areas). His notation — $dy/dx$ for the derivative, $\int$ for the integral — was vastly superior to Newton's and is still used universally today. Leibniz also invented binary arithmetic (the basis of all digital computing) and built a mechanical calculator.

The priority dispute between Newton and Leibniz — who invented calculus first? — became one of the ugliest controversies in the history of mathematics. Newton's supporters in the Royal Society (with Newton himself orchestrating behind the scenes) accused Leibniz of plagiarism. Leibniz died in 1716 in dispute, virtually friendless. The consequence was that British mathematicians used Newton's less convenient notation for over a century, while continental Europeans adopted Leibniz's notation and advanced rapidly beyond the British. Mathematics is not immune to nationalism.

(Historical verdict: both invented calculus independently. Leibniz's notation was better. The Royal Society's investigation was a travesty.)

## Euler: the most prolific mathematician

[Leonhard Euler](/mathematicians/euler) (1707–1783) dominated eighteenth-century mathematics. He is associated with the symbols we use today: $e$ for the base of natural logarithms, $i$ for $\sqrt{-1}$, $\pi$ for the circle constant, $\Sigma$ for sums, and $f(x)$ for a function. (He was the first to *popularise* these — $\pi$ had been introduced by William Jones in 1706; Euler's consistent use from 1737 onwards standardised it.)

Euler's most famous single result is $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ — Euler's identity — a consequence of his formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ (proved using power series). He proved that $\sum_{n=1}^\infty 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6$, settling the Basel problem that had stumped mathematicians for decades. He solved the Königsberg bridge problem, founding graph theory in a four-page paper in 1736.

Euler continued publishing after losing his sight entirely in 1766 — dictating to assistants and working with mental calculation. His collected works fill over 70 volumes. He had thirteen children and continued working even while nursing a baby.

## Bernoulli, d'Alembert, Lagrange

The eighteenth century also produced [Daniel Bernoulli](/mathematicians/bernoulli) (fluid dynamics and the equation that bears his name), [d'Alembert](/mathematicians/laplace) (who solved the wave equation in 1747), and [Lagrange](/mathematicians/lagrange) (who reformulated Newtonian mechanics in a purely algebraic framework, eliminating geometric intuition in favour of coordinate invariance).

By 1800, calculus had reshaped astronomy, physics, and engineering. But it was built on intuitive foundations — infinitesimals, "limits," "approaching" — that no one had made rigorous. The nineteenth century would have to repair that.
