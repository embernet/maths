Newton and Leibniz invented calculus. But when nineteenth-century mathematicians examined its foundations carefully, they found them deeply unsatisfactory. What exactly is an infinitesimal? What does "approaches" mean? What *is* a real number? The calculus worked spectacularly, producing correct answers in physics, astronomy, and engineering. But it was, foundationally, a mess.

The rigour revolution of the nineteenth century did not change the results of calculus. It provided them with proper proofs.

## Cauchy and the epsilon-delta definition

[Augustin-Louis Cauchy](/mathematicians/cauchy) (1789–1857), working in Paris in the 1820s, introduced the formal definition of a limit and rebuilt the calculus on top of it. His *Cours d'analyse* (1821) and *Résumé des leçons* (1823) contain the first rigorous treatments of continuity, convergence, and the integral. Cauchy's definition of the derivative and integral are, with minor modifications, the definitions used in analysis textbooks today.

Cauchy was enormously productive — he published 789 papers — but he was also fiercely royalist and religious, causing him to lose his university positions multiple times during France's successive revolutions. He fled to Turin after the 1830 revolution rather than swear allegiance to the new government.

The ε-δ formalism is often called "Cauchy's" but the exact form in which it is usually stated — with the universal quantifier over $\varepsilon$ and the existential quantifier over $\delta$ — was fully articulated by [Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) in his Berlin lectures from the 1860s.

## Bolzano: the forgotten pioneer

[Bernard Bolzano](/mathematicians/bolzano) (1781–1848), a Bohemian priest working in Prague, proved the Bolzano-Weierstrass theorem and gave the first rigorous proof of the Intermediate Value Theorem — decades before Cauchy, and working in isolation in a language (German) less read than French. Placed under house arrest by the Austrian government for his philosophical writings (which had nothing to do with mathematics), he continued mathematical work that was not recognised in his lifetime and had to be rediscovered later.

## Weierstrass and the pathological function

[Karl Weierstrass](/mathematicians/weierstrass) spent fifteen years as a secondary school teacher, publishing papers in provincial journals, before his mathematical genius was recognised and he was invited to Berlin at age forty. There he became the most influential analysis teacher of the century.

Weierstrass constructed a continuous function that is nowhere differentiable — a curve you can draw without lifting your pen but which has no tangent line at any point. This function:

$$W(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a^n \cos(b^n \pi x), \quad 0 < a < 1,\ ab > 1 + \frac{3\pi}{2},$$

shocked the mathematical community. It demonstrated that intuition about continuous functions — that they must be differentiable "almost everywhere" — was simply wrong.

## Riemann and the real integral

[Bernhard Riemann](/mathematicians/riemann) formulated the integral rigorously using upper and lower sums (the Riemann integral you met in core mathematics), and proved his spectacular Riemann Hypothesis (still unproved today) about the distribution of prime numbers in his single number-theory paper (1859).

## Dedekind, Cantor, and the structure of infinity

[Richard Dedekind](/mathematicians/dedekind) and [Georg Cantor](/mathematicians/cantor) completed the construction of the real numbers. Dedekind's "cuts" (1872) built $\mathbb{R}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$ by specifying which rationals lie to the left and right of each real number. Cantor's approach used equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences.

Cantor then did something more extraordinary: he proved that infinities have different sizes. The natural numbers and the rational numbers are both **countably infinite** (there is a bijection between them). But the real numbers are **uncountably infinite**: no bijection between $\mathbb{N}$ and $\mathbb{R}$ exists. The proof — Cantor's diagonal argument — is one of the most beautiful in mathematics.

Cantor was ferociously attacked by Leopold Kronecker ("a corrupter of youth") and others. He suffered multiple mental breakdowns, dying in a sanatorium in 1918. He is now recognised as one of the most original mathematical thinkers in history; his theory of transfinite cardinals is a foundation of modern mathematics.
