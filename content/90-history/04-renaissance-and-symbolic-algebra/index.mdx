The fourteenth and fifteenth centuries saw the slow spread of Hindu-Arabic numerals and Arabic algebra through European universities. By the sixteenth century, Italian mathematicians were competing fiercely to solve polynomial equations of degree three and four — the cubic and quartic. The resulting drama involves theft of credit, public challenges, and the first glimpse of imaginary numbers.

## The cubic formula

Niccolò Fontana, known as **Tartaglia** (c.1500–1557), found a method to solve certain cubic equations. He shared his technique in confidence with [Gerolamo Cardano](/mathematicians/cardano), who published it in his *Ars Magna* (1545) — the most important algebra book of the sixteenth century — with attribution to Tartaglia but in a more general form. Tartaglia was furious; Cardano maintained the attribution was fair. The ensuing dispute was bitter and public.

The cubic formula is remarkable not just for its existence but for its use of imaginary numbers. Even when the three roots are all real, the formula requires intermediate calculations involving $\sqrt{-1}$. Cardano was the first to write down expressions like $\sqrt{-15}$ as part of a calculation, calling such quantities "as subtle as they are useless." The "useless" imaginary numbers he dismissed would become, four centuries later, indispensable in quantum mechanics.

Cardano's pupil Lodovico Ferrari extended the method to quartic (degree-four) equations. Both solutions appear in the *Ars Magna*.

## Viète and symbolic algebra

François Viète (1540–1603) made the decisive step toward modern symbolic algebra: he introduced letters to represent not just unknowns but also given quantities (parameters). Before Viète, an algebraic equation was a verbal statement with one unknown. After Viète, equations could be written with multiple letters representing various known and unknown quantities — the first step toward the purely symbolic algebra of today.

## Descartes and coordinate geometry

[René Descartes](/mathematicians/descartes) (1596–1650) published his *La Géométrie* in 1637 as an appendix to a philosophical work, introducing what we now call Cartesian coordinates. The insight — that a point in the plane corresponds to a pair of numbers $(x, y)$, and that a geometric curve corresponds to an equation in $x$ and $y$ — fused algebra and geometry for the first time. Any geometric question became, in principle, an algebraic one, and vice versa.

[Pierre de Fermat](/mathematicians/fermat) (c.1607–1665) independently arrived at the same idea and in many respects went further analytically. He also stated the theorem that would bear his name: in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's *Arithmetica*, he wrote that the equation $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no solution in positive integers for $n \ge 3$, adding that he had "a truly marvellous proof which this margin is too narrow to contain." The proof was not found for 358 years; Andrew Wiles finally proved **Fermat's Last Theorem** in 1994.

## Why the cubic matters

The algebraic solution of the cubic turned out to be important not just practically but conceptually: it demonstrated that algebra could go beyond what geometry could accomplish, and it introduced imaginary numbers as mathematically necessary. Two centuries later, Galois would prove that the cubic formula is the deepest formula of its kind: there is no analogous formula for degree five and above. The drama of Italian algebra was the prologue to one of the most beautiful theories in mathematics.
