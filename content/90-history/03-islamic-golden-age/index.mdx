After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, the centre of mathematical activity shifted to the Islamic world. Baghdad under the Abbasid caliphate became the intellectual capital of the medieval world. The caliph al-Mamun established the **House of Wisdom** (Bayt al-Hikma) in the early ninth century: a great library and translation centre where Greek works — Euclid, Archimedes, Ptolemy, Diophantus — were translated into Arabic and then extended.

## Al-Khwarizmi and the birth of algebra

[Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi](/mathematicians/al-khwarizmi) (c.780–850 CE) worked at the House of Wisdom and wrote two books that shaped the next millennium of mathematics. His *Al-Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala* ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing") gave algebra its name: *al-jabr* (completion — moving a term to the other side of an equation) and *al-muqabala* (balancing — cancelling like terms) are the two basic operations of equation-solving. The word "algebra" derives directly from *al-jabr*.

His second influential work described the Hindu-Arabic numeral system — with the revolutionary concept of zero as a placeholder — and introduced it to the Islamic scholarly world. His own name, Latinised as "Algoritmi" or "Algorismus," gave the world the word "algorithm."

## Indian origins of the numeral system

The numerals that al-Khwarizmi described originated in India, not in Arabia. Indian mathematicians — particularly Brahmagupta (628 CE) and his predecessors — developed the decimal place-value system and established the arithmetic of zero. This system was transmitted westward through Islamic scholars before reaching Europe through translations of al-Khwarizmi's work and the Fibonacci sequence of contacts with Arabic mathematics. The story of the "Hindu-Arabic" numerals is a story of mathematical ideas travelling the world.

## Other contributions

Islamic mathematics extended Greek trigonometry (developing sine, cosine, and tangent as functions rather than geometric line segments), developed combinatorics and the binomial theorem, made advances in number theory and spherical geometry, and produced highly accurate astronomical tables. Al-Haytham (c.965–1040 CE) proved results about geometry that anticipate the calculus.

## Fibonacci and the European transmission

[Leonardo Fibonacci](/mathematicians/fibonacci) (c.1170–1250) grew up in North Africa, where his father worked as a customs officer and Leonardo was educated in the Arabic mathematical tradition. His 1202 *Liber Abaci* ("Book of Calculation") introduced the Hindu-Arabic numeral system to European readers with practical explanations of commercial arithmetic. The famous Fibonacci sequence appears in this book as a puzzle about rabbit breeding.

Fibonacci's sequence $1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, \ldots$ (each term the sum of the two before) has remarkable properties: the ratio of consecutive terms converges to the golden ratio $\phi = (1+\sqrt{5})/2$; the terms appear in the spiral arrangements of sunflower seeds and pine cones; and the generating function can be computed explicitly using polynomial methods from foundational topics. It is a beautiful example of a simple recursive rule whose consequences are rich and surprising.

The transmission of Arabic and Indian mathematics to Europe via works like the *Liber Abaci* triggered a flowering of European mathematics that would, over the next four centuries, produce the calculus.
