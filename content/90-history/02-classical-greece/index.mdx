Ancient Greek mathematics at its height was less a single tradition than a network of competing schools across the Mediterranean world. Thales proved geometric theorems; Pythagoras and his followers built a numerological cosmology; Plato elevated geometry to the queen of sciences; and Euclid, working in Alexandria under the Ptolemies, organised the whole inheritance into the *Elements* — the most enduring textbook in human history.

What distinguished Greek mathematics from its Babylonian and Egyptian predecessors was not the results (Babylonian mathematicians knew Pythagorean triples a thousand years earlier) but the *method*: deductive proof from axioms. A theorem was not true because it had been verified in examples, or because a clever method consistently gave the right answer. It was true because it could be derived, step by step, by logic alone from a set of explicitly stated initial assumptions. This standard of certainty was, and remains, unique to mathematics.

## The conic sections

Menaechmus (fl. 350 BCE) discovered the conic sections — ellipse, parabola, and hyperbola — while trying to solve the problem of "doubling the cube" (constructing $\sqrt[3]{2}$ by ruler and compass). Apollonius of Perga (c.262–190 BCE) gave them their modern names and derived their properties systematically in his eight-book *Conics*. These curves would reappear two thousand years later in Kepler's laws of planetary motion and the parabolic trajectories of projectiles.

## Number theory in the Elements

Books VII–IX of the *Elements* contain substantial number theory: the Euclidean algorithm, the proof that there are infinitely many primes (one of the most elegant proofs in all of mathematics: assume finitely many, multiply them together and add 1, arrive at a contradiction), and the characterisation of even perfect numbers. This mathematical programme — proving results about integers by purely logical methods, without appeal to measurement — is the ancestor of modern number theory.

## Plato's influence

[Plato](/mathematicians/pythagoras) (referencing here the Pythagorean tradition Plato drew from) insisted that knowledge gained through the senses is unreliable; only mathematical knowledge, derived by reason from first principles, is certain. The regular polyhedra — tetrahedron, cube, octahedron, dodecahedron, icosahedron — became known as the "Platonic solids" after his use of them in cosmological speculation. Mathematics, for Plato and his followers, was not merely a practical tool but access to a realm of permanent, perfect truth.

This Platonist view of mathematics — that mathematical objects (numbers, shapes, proofs) exist independently of human minds and are discovered rather than invented — remains influential and controversial. Most mathematicians, when asked, report some form of mathematical Platonism in their gut feelings about what they are doing.

## The legacy

Greek mathematics gave Western intellectual culture its template for rigorous argument. When Euclid says "let it be assumed that through any two points there passes exactly one straight line," he is setting up an axiomatic system of the kind that would not be generalised until the nineteenth century. The tension between the five postulates — particularly the parallel postulate — would occupy mathematicians for over two thousand years, and its resolution would transform not just geometry but the entire conception of what mathematics is and what it is about.
