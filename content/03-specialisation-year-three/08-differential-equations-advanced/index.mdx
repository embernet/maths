Year Two differential equations gave you techniques: separation of variables, Fourier series, the three canonical PDEs. Year Three asks the harder questions. When do solutions exist and are they unique? Can you write an explicit solution for an arbitrary forcing function? What if the equation itself is the question — if you are trying to find a function that minimises some integral?

## Existence and uniqueness for ODEs

The **Picard-Lindelöf theorem**: given the ODE $y' = f(t, y)$ with initial condition $y(t_0) = y_0$, if $f$ is continuous and Lipschitz in $y$ (meaning $|f(t,y_1) - f(t,y_2)| \le L|y_1 - y_2|$ for some constant $L$), then there exists a unique local solution.

The proof constructs the solution via the **Picard iteration**: $y_0(t) = y_0$; $y_{n+1}(t) = y_0 + \int_{t_0}^t f(s, y_n(s))\, ds$. This is a contraction mapping on the space of continuous functions with the sup norm, and the Banach fixed-point theorem gives convergence to the unique solution.

**Gronwall's lemma**: if $u(t) \le \alpha(t) + \int_{t_0}^t \beta(s) u(s)\, ds$, then $u(t) \le \alpha(t) + \int_{t_0}^t \alpha(s)\beta(s) e^{\int_s^t \beta(r)dr}\, ds$. This fundamental inequality bounds the "growth of error" and is used to prove continuous dependence on initial conditions.

## Green's functions

For a linear ODE or PDE $Ly = f$ (where $L$ is a linear differential operator), the **Green's function** $G(x, \xi)$ is the solution when the forcing is a unit impulse: $LG(\cdot, \xi) = \delta(\cdot - \xi)$. The solution for a general forcing $f$ is then:

$$y(x) = \int G(x, \xi)\, f(\xi)\, d\xi.$$

Green's function represents $L^{-1}$ as an integral operator — analogous to writing a matrix inverse as $A^{-1} = $ adjugate / determinant.

**Example**: for $-y'' = f(x)$ on $[0,1]$ with $y(0) = y(1) = 0$, the Green's function is:

$$G(x, \xi) = \begin{cases} x(1-\xi) & 0 \le x \le \xi \\ \xi(1-x) & \xi \le x \le 1 \end{cases}.$$

The physical interpretation: $G(x, \xi)$ is the deflection of a string at position $x$ due to a unit point load at position $\xi$.

The **maximum principle** for harmonic functions (solutions of $\nabla^2 u = 0$): a harmonic function on a bounded domain with non-constant boundary values attains its maximum and minimum on the boundary. Immediate consequences: uniqueness of solutions to the Dirichlet problem; stability of solutions with respect to boundary perturbations.

## Sturm-Liouville theory

Many PDEs, when solved by separation of variables, produce an **eigenvalue problem**:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[p(x) y'\right] + q(x) y + \lambda w(x) y = 0, \quad a \le x \le b,$$

with boundary conditions at $a$ and $b$. This is a **Sturm-Liouville problem**. Key properties:
- Eigenvalues $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \lambda_3 < \cdots \to +\infty$ are real and form a discrete sequence.
- Eigenfunctions $\phi_n$ corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal in the $L^2$ sense with weight $w$: $\int_a^b \phi_m(x)\phi_n(x) w(x)\, dx = 0$ for $m \ne n$.
- The eigenfunctions form a complete orthonormal basis for $L^2_w([a,b])$ — any square-integrable function can be expanded in terms of them.

Fourier series are the special case $p = w = 1$, $q = 0$. Bessel functions, Legendre polynomials, and Hermite polynomials are all eigenfunctions of Sturm-Liouville operators.

## The calculus of variations

The **calculus of variations** asks: among all functions $y : [a,b] \to \mathbb{R}$ satisfying given boundary conditions, which one minimises a functional

$$J[y] = \int_a^b F(x, y, y')\, dx?$$

The necessary condition is the **Euler-Lagrange equation**:

$$\frac{\partial F}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial F}{\partial y'} = 0.$$

This is the "derivative of $J$" set to zero, in the same way we set $f'(x) = 0$ to find minima of ordinary functions.

**Brachistochrone**: which curve between two points allows a bead to slide (under gravity, frictionlessly) in minimum time? The Euler-Lagrange equation gives the **cycloid** — a result first found by the Bernoulli brothers in a famous challenge problem.

**Geodesics**: shortest paths on a surface. The Euler-Lagrange equation on a Riemannian manifold gives the geodesic equations; these become Einstein's equations in general relativity (extremising the spacetime action).

**Lagrangian mechanics**: Newton's $F = ma$ can be recast as: a system evolves to minimise the **action** $S = \int (T - V)\, dt$ (kinetic minus potential energy). This formulation — Hamilton's principle — extends naturally to quantum mechanics and field theory.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [Robotics and control systems](/applications/robotics-and-control) use the Euler-Lagrange equations from this course to derive the equations of motion for robot arms, and Green's functions appear in the impulse-response formulation of control design. [Bridge and structural engineering](/applications/bridge-and-structural-engineering) applies the calculus of variations to find the shape that minimises deflection under load — the catenary curve for cables and the Euler-Bernoulli beam equation both arise as Euler-Lagrange equations. [Airfoil and aerodynamics design](/applications/airfoil-and-aerodynamics) uses the maximum principle for harmonic functions (from the Green's functions section) to prove uniqueness of the potential-flow solution around a wing profile, underpinning the mathematical rigour behind computational aerodynamics.

The calculus of variations connects classical mechanics, geometry, and the deepest formulations of modern physics. It is one of the places where mathematics and physics become, for a while, the same subject.
