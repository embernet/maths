What does "continuous" really mean? In metric spaces, you said: $f$ is continuous at $x$ if $f(y) \to f(x)$ as $y \to x$ in the metric. But this presupposes a metric. Topology asks: what is the minimal structure needed to define continuity, convergence, and related notions — without any notion of distance?

The answer is the concept of **open sets**. Define continuity using open sets, and you have a framework that works for spaces where no metric is natural (infinite-dimensional function spaces, algebraic varieties, or the space of all topologies on a given set).

## Topological spaces

A **topology** on a set $X$ is a collection $\tau$ of subsets of $X$ (called **open sets**) satisfying:
1. $\emptyset$ and $X$ are in $\tau$.
2. Any union of sets in $\tau$ is in $\tau$.
3. Any *finite* intersection of sets in $\tau$ is in $\tau$.

The pair $(X, \tau)$ is a **topological space**.

Examples:
- **Metric topology**: open balls generate the topology of any metric space.
- **Discrete topology**: $\tau = 2^X$ (every subset is open).
- **Indiscrete topology**: $\tau = \{\emptyset, X\}$ (only the trivial open sets).
- **Zariski topology**: used in algebraic geometry, where closed sets are algebraic varieties.

A function $f : X \to Y$ is **continuous** if $f^{-1}(U)$ is open in $X$ for every open $U \subseteq Y$. This is the topological definition of continuity; it agrees with the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition when $X$ and $Y$ are metric spaces.

A **homeomorphism** is a continuous bijection with continuous inverse — the topological analogue of an isomorphism. Two homeomorphic spaces are "the same" topologically: you cannot distinguish them using only continuous maps.

## Compactness

A topological space $K$ is **compact** if every open cover has a finite sub-cover. In metric spaces (where Heine-Borel applies), this is equivalent to closed-and-bounded for subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$. In general topological spaces, compactness is the "right" abstraction.

**Tychonoff's theorem**: the product of any collection of compact topological spaces is compact. This is one of the most powerful theorems in topology, and surprisingly, its proof for infinite products is equivalent (in ZF set theory) to the Axiom of Choice.

Compact Hausdorff spaces behave especially well: continuous functions on them are bounded and attain their extrema; compact subsets are closed; the topology is uniquely determined by the convergent sequences.

## Hausdorff spaces and separation axioms

A **Hausdorff space** is one where any two distinct points can be separated by disjoint open sets. Metric spaces are always Hausdorff. Many non-metric topological spaces are not Hausdorff, and their properties can be pathological. Most spaces arising in analysis and geometry are Hausdorff.

The separation axioms ($T_0, T_1, T_2, T_3, T_4, \ldots$) form a hierarchy of increasingly strong separation properties. $T_2$ is the Hausdorff condition; $T_4$ (normal spaces) allows Urysohn's lemma: existence of continuous functions separating disjoint closed sets.

## Connectedness

A topological space is **connected** if it cannot be written as the union of two disjoint non-empty open sets. The real line $\mathbb{R}$ is connected (the Intermediate Value Theorem is equivalent to this). The rationals $\mathbb{Q}$ are totally disconnected: every connected subset has at most one point.

**Path-connectedness** (stronger): $X$ is path-connected if any two points can be joined by a continuous path. Path-connected implies connected; the converse fails.

## Real-world applications

Where this turns up in the world: [Robotics and control systems](/applications/robotics-and-control) use the topology of configuration spaces — the space of all possible robot arm positions — to plan collision-free paths; holes in that space correspond to unreachable configurations or unavoidable obstacles. [Computer graphics and 3D modelling](/applications/computer-graphics-3d) rely on topological invariants to classify and manipulate meshes: detecting whether a surface is a sphere, a torus, or something more complex determines how textures wrap and how mesh surgery can proceed. [Protein folding](/applications/protein-folding-alphafold) depends on the topological structure of the energy landscape — persistent homology, a computational offspring of algebraic topology, maps the shape of that landscape and identifies the barriers between folding states.

## The fundamental group: a glimpse of algebraic topology

[Poincaré](/mathematicians/poincare) founded algebraic topology around 1900 by attaching algebraic invariants to topological spaces. The **fundamental group** $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ records the "essentially different" loops based at $x_0$: two loops are equivalent if one can be continuously deformed into the other.

$\pi_1(\mathbb{R}^2 \setminus \{0\}) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ — you can wind around the origin any integer number of times. $\pi_1(S^2) = 0$ — every loop on a sphere can be shrunk to a point.

Algebraic topology extends this to homology and cohomology groups, which classify spaces by their "holes" in various dimensions. These tools are used in data analysis (persistent homology), physics (topological insulators), and pure mathematics (the Poincaré conjecture, proved by Perelman in 2003, characterises the 3-sphere by its fundamental group).
