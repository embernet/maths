## The application

Every time a smartphone shows your location, it has solved a system of simultaneous equations using signals from at least four satellites, each broadcasting its position and the precise time. The mathematics involves linear algebra, least-squares optimisation, and — to achieve metre-level accuracy — corrections derived from Einstein's theory of general relativity.

## The maths behind it

Each satellite broadcasts its position $(x_i, y_i, z_i)$ and the time $t_i$ of transmission. Your receiver records the arrival time $T$. The signal travels at the speed of light $c$, so the distance to satellite $i$ is approximately $r_i = c(T - t_i)$. Your position $(x, y, z)$ must satisfy

$$r_i^2 = (x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2 + (z - z_i)^2.$$

With four satellites you have four equations and four unknowns (including an unknown receiver clock offset). With more satellites — modern receivers use twelve or more — the system is **over-determined**: more equations than unknowns. No exact solution exists (every measurement has noise), so you instead minimise the sum of squared residuals. This is the **method of least squares**, developed by [Gauss](/mathematicians/gauss) and Legendre at the turn of the nineteenth century.

In matrix form, $A\mathbf{x} \approx \mathbf{b}$ where $A$ encodes the linearised geometry and $\mathbf{b}$ encodes the measured pseudoranges. The least-squares solution is $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}$. Ill-conditioning (which occurs when satellites cluster in one part of the sky) inflates errors — monitored via the *dilution of precision* (DOP) statistic, which is the square root of $\text{tr}(A^T A)^{-1}$.

**Relativistic corrections**: without correction, clocks on GPS satellites run faster by about 38 microseconds per day (7 microseconds from special relativity due to satellite velocity, offset by 45 microseconds from general relativity due to weaker gravity at altitude). A 38-microsecond error translates to a position error of over 11 km per day. General-relativistic corrections are therefore baked into the satellite clock rates — one of the few places where general relativity appears as an engineering necessity rather than a curiosity.

## A concrete example

A civilian GPS receiver achieves accuracy of roughly 3–5 metres horizontally in good conditions. Military and survey-grade receivers using carrier-phase tracking reach centimetric accuracy. The European Galileo system and augmentation services such as WAAS (Wide Area Augmentation System) push civilian accuracy below 1 metre by broadcasting differential corrections from ground stations.

## History of the application

The first GPS satellite was launched in 1978; the system became fully operational in 1993. The US Department of Defense initially degraded public signals through "Selective Availability" (removed in 2000), after which civilian accuracy improved tenfold overnight. Modern GNSS (Global Navigation Satellite Systems) now include Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo, and China's BeiDou — each using the same over-determined system of pseudorange equations.
