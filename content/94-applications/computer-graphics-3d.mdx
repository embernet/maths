## The application

Every rendered scene in a film or video game involves the same sequence of mathematical operations: representing geometry as collections of triangles, transforming those triangles through a pipeline of matrix multiplications, and computing the colour of each pixel by solving the rendering equation (a Fredholm integral equation for light transport). The mathematics is linear algebra for transformations, quaternions for rotations, and differential geometry for surfaces and shading.

## The maths behind it

**Transformations and homogeneous coordinates**: a 3D point $(x,y,z)$ is represented as a 4D vector $(x,y,z,1)^T$. Translations, rotations, and perspective projections are all represented as $4 \times 4$ matrix multiplications, composable by matrix product. The full rendering pipeline — model transform, view transform, projection — is a sequence of matrix multiplications, performed in hardware by the GPU.

**Rotations and quaternions**: representing 3D rotations as $3 \times 3$ orthogonal matrices ($\det = +1$) is standard, but for animation and interpolation, **quaternions** are superior. A quaternion $q = w + xi + yj + zk$ (with $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$) of unit norm $|q| = 1$ represents a rotation of angle $2\theta$ about axis $\hat{n}$ as $q = \cos\theta + \sin\theta(n_x i + n_y j + n_z k)$. Spherical linear interpolation (**SLERP**) between two unit quaternions gives the smoothest rotation path, avoiding the gimbal lock of Euler angles. The quaternion group is the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^4$ — a topic in [topology](/03-specialist-areas/04-topology).

**Differential geometry and shading**: realistic lighting requires computing surface normals and curvatures. A smooth surface in 3D is described by a parametrisation $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$; the **first fundamental form** $(E\, du^2 + 2F\, du\, dv + G\, dv^2)$ encodes distances, and the **second fundamental form** encodes curvatures. Normal maps encode perturbations to the normal vector for high-frequency surface detail without increasing polygon count.

The **rendering equation** (Kajiya, 1986): the radiance $L$ at a point $x$ in direction $\omega_o$ is

$$L(x, \omega_o) = L_e(x, \omega_o) + \int_\Omega f_r(x, \omega_i, \omega_o) L(x', \omega_i) (\omega_i \cdot \mathbf{n})\, d\omega_i.$$

This recursive integral equation is solved by Monte Carlo ray tracing — generating random light paths and averaging them.

## A concrete example

Pixar's *Finding Dory* required roughly $100$ million CPU core-hours of rendering on their in-house RenderMan system. A single frame at 4K resolution has ~8 million pixels; for each, thousands of rays are traced and the rendering equation evaluated at each bounce, using the differential geometry of procedurally generated coral and ocean surfaces.

## History of the application

Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963) and GRAIL (1969) were the first interactive computer graphics systems. The rendering equation was formulated by Kajiya in 1986. Real-time rasterisation (used in video games) replaced true ray tracing until NVIDIA's RTX hardware (2018) made real-time ray tracing feasible, bringing production-quality rendering to interactive applications.
