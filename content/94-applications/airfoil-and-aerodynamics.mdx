## The application

The curved wing profile of every commercial aircraft descends from a mathematical insight: the flow around a complicated shape (an airfoil) can be obtained from the flow around a circle (which is analytically tractable) via a **conformal mapping** — an angle-preserving transformation given by a function of a complex variable. This connection between complex analysis and fluid dynamics was the cornerstone of aerodynamic design from the 1910s until computational fluid dynamics matured in the 1980s.

## The maths behind it

For two-dimensional, steady, inviscid, irrotational flow, the velocity field $\mathbf{u}$ satisfies both $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$ (incompressibility) and $\nabla \times \mathbf{u} = 0$ (irrotationality). Writing $\mathbf{u} = \nabla \phi$, the velocity potential $\phi$ satisfies **Laplace's equation** $\nabla^2 \phi = 0$.

The **complex potential** $w(z) = \phi(x,y) + i\psi(x,y)$ (where $\psi$ is the stream function satisfying $\nabla^2 \psi = 0$ and $\psi = \text{const}$ on streamlines) is an **analytic function** of $z = x + iy$. The Cauchy-Riemann equations ensure that $\phi$ and $\psi$ are harmonic conjugates.

For uniform flow past a cylinder of radius $R$ with circulation $\Gamma$, the complex potential is:

$$w(z) = U\!\left(z + \frac{R^2}{z}\right) + \frac{i\Gamma}{2\pi}\ln z.$$

The **Joukowski transformation** $z \mapsto \zeta + c^2/\zeta$ maps the circle $|z| = R$ to an airfoil shape. Crucially, because $w$ is analytic, the complex potential of the transformed flow is obtained by substituting — and the lift can be computed from the **Kutta-Joukowski theorem**: $L = \rho U \Gamma$ per unit span, where $\Gamma$ is the circulation. Lift comes entirely from circulation, and circulation is generated by the sharp trailing edge of the airfoil (the Kutta condition).

For compressible flow at higher speeds, the governing equations become nonlinear PDEs (the Euler equations), requiring numerical methods. Modern **computational fluid dynamics** (CFD) discretises the full Navier-Stokes equations on three-dimensional meshes using finite volume methods.

## A concrete example

The wing of an Airbus A380 generates roughly 560 tonnes of lift at cruise, with a wing area of 845 m². The lift coefficient $C_L \approx 0.45$ at cruise conditions (Mach 0.85, 35,000 ft) was optimised using thousands of CFD simulations, each solving the discretised Euler equations on a mesh of $10^7$–$10^8$ cells. The elegant complex-analysis approach gives the intuition; modern CFD provides the numbers.

## History of the application

Nikolai Joukowski derived the airfoil transformation in 1910; Ludwig Prandtl developed boundary layer theory (explaining drag) in 1904. Together these framed aerodynamics as an applied mathematical discipline. The shift to numerical methods began in the 1960s and accelerated with the availability of supercomputers. Today, complex-analysis methods remain in use because they provide the only analytical understanding of why wings generate lift.
