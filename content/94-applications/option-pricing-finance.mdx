## The application

A financial option is a contract that gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell an asset at a fixed price on a future date. Pricing such a contract fairly — so neither buyer nor seller has an unfair advantage — requires modelling the random future evolution of the asset price. The mathematics is stochastic calculus, and the pricing equation is a partial differential equation identical in form to the heat equation.

## The maths behind it

The **Black–Scholes model** assumes the asset price $S_t$ follows **geometric Brownian motion**:

$$dS = \mu S\, dt + \sigma S\, dW_t,$$

where $\mu$ is the drift, $\sigma$ the volatility, and $W_t$ a standard Brownian motion (Wiener process). The option value $V(S, t)$ depends on the current price $S$ and time $t$.

Applying **Itô's lemma** to $V(S_t, t)$ and constructing a risk-free portfolio (long the option, short $\partial V / \partial S$ units of the stock) eliminates the random term. Setting the portfolio's return equal to the risk-free rate gives the **Black–Scholes PDE**:

$$\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial S^2} + rS\frac{\partial V}{\partial S} - rV = 0.$$

Changing variables $S = e^x$, $\tau = T - t$ transforms this into the **heat equation** $\partial u / \partial \tau = \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \partial^2 u / \partial x^2$. The explicit Black–Scholes formula for a European call option with strike $K$ and maturity $T$ is:

$$C = S_0 N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2),$$

where $d_1 = [\ln(S_0/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T]/(\sigma\sqrt{T})$, $d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}$, and $N$ is the standard normal CDF.

## A concrete example

The global over-the-counter derivatives market has a notional value exceeding $600 trillion. A European equity option on Apple with strike \$200, maturity six months, current price \$195, volatility 25%, and risk-free rate 5%, is priced by the formula to within cents. Every bank's trading desk runs Black–Scholes and its extensions thousands of times per second.

## History of the application

Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published their pricing formula in 1973; Robert Merton published an extension simultaneously. The Chicago Board Options Exchange opened in the same year, turning options trading into a mainstream market. Scholes and Merton received the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economics (Black had died in 1995). The formula's derivation drew explicitly on [Kolmogorov's](/mathematicians/kolmogorov) theory of diffusion processes and the heat equation of [Fourier](/mathematicians/fourier).
