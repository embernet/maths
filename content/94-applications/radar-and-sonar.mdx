## The application

Radar (Radio Detection And Ranging) and sonar (Sound Navigation And Ranging) both work by transmitting a wave and detecting its reflection from a target. The time delay gives range; the Doppler shift gives velocity. Extracting these quantities from noisy measured signals is a signal-processing problem whose solution uses the Fourier transform, matched filtering, and statistical hypothesis testing.

## The maths behind it

A transmitted pulse $s(t)$ reflects from a target at range $R$ with radial velocity $v$ and returns as $r(t) \propto s(t - 2R/c)\, e^{j2\pi f_d t}$, where $c$ is the wave speed and $f_d = 2v f_0 / c$ is the **Doppler frequency shift**. The received signal also contains thermal noise $n(t)$, modelled as a zero-mean Gaussian random process.

**Matched filtering**: to detect a known signal $s(t)$ in additive Gaussian noise, the **matched filter** maximises the signal-to-noise ratio. Its impulse response is $h(t) = s^*(T - t)$ (the time-reversed complex conjugate of the signal), and the filter output is the cross-correlation of the received signal with $s$:

$$y(t) = \int r(\tau)\, s^*(t - \tau + T)\, d\tau.$$

In the frequency domain this is multiplication: $Y(f) = R(f) \cdot S^*(f) \cdot e^{j2\pi fT}$, where the product is computed via the **fast Fourier transform**. The matched filter output peaks at the correct delay when the signal is present, and the peak value is compared to a threshold to decide detection.

**Doppler processing**: in pulsed radar, the received pulses from a target have a phase that advances by $e^{j2\pi f_d T_r}$ pulse-to-pulse (where $T_r$ is the pulse repetition interval). Taking the DFT across a coherent dwell of $N$ pulses concentrates the target energy at frequency bin $k \approx N f_d T_r$, allowing simultaneous estimation of range and velocity — the **range-Doppler map**.

## A concrete example

A modern air traffic control radar (such as the Raytheon ASR-11) transmits pulses at around 1 GHz, rotating its antenna to scan the full airspace every 4–5 seconds. The digital signal processor performs matched filtering on thousands of range bins simultaneously, using FFT-based processing at hundreds of millions of complex operations per second, to track hundreds of aircraft simultaneously.

## History of the application

Radar was developed independently in Britain, Germany, the United States, and France in the 1930s, with Britain's Chain Home system first deployed operationally in 1937. The mathematical framework — matched filtering, optimal detection — was formalised by Van Vleck and Middleton in the 1940s and by Woodward (1953) who introduced the ambiguity function. Sonar has an earlier history: Paul Langevin used ultrasound to detect submarines in World War I (1917).
