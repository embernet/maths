## The application

Approximately 45% of global email traffic is spam — 162 billion messages per day. Every email service runs a spam classifier to keep most of it out of your inbox. The simplest and most instructive implementation is **naïve Bayes classification**: a direct application of Bayes' theorem that works surprisingly well despite its simplifying assumption.

## The maths behind it

**Naïve Bayes classifier**: let $C = 1$ (spam) or $C = 0$ (ham) and let $\mathbf{w} = (w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_d)$ be the word features present in an email (a binary vector: $w_i = 1$ if word $i$ appears). Bayes' theorem gives:

$$P(C = 1 \mid \mathbf{w}) = \frac{P(\mathbf{w} \mid C = 1)\, P(C = 1)}{P(\mathbf{w})}.$$

The "naïve" assumption is that word occurrences are **conditionally independent** given the class: $P(\mathbf{w} \mid C) = \prod_i P(w_i \mid C)$. Then:

$$P(C = 1 \mid \mathbf{w}) \propto P(C = 1) \prod_{i : w_i = 1} P(w_i = 1 \mid C = 1) \prod_{i : w_i = 0} P(w_i = 0 \mid C = 1).$$

The parameters $P(w_i = 1 \mid C)$ are estimated from a labelled training set by **maximum likelihood** (simply the fraction of class-$C$ emails containing word $i$, with Laplace smoothing to avoid zero probabilities for unseen words).

In log-space this becomes a **linear classifier**: $\log P(C=1 \mid \mathbf{w}) - \log P(C=0 \mid \mathbf{w}) = \log \frac{P(C=1)}{P(C=0)} + \sum_i w_i \log \frac{P(w_i=1 \mid C=1)}{P(w_i=1 \mid C=0)}$. Each word $i$ contributes a **weight** (its log-likelihood ratio) to the total score; words strongly associated with spam get large positive weights.

**Logistic regression** relaxes the independence assumption by directly modelling $P(C = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}^T \mathbf{x})$ (where $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function) and estimating $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ by maximum likelihood. Modern spam filters use much richer features and neural network classifiers, but naïve Bayes remains competitive and interpretable.

## A concrete example

A naïve Bayes spam filter trained on the SpamAssassin public corpus achieves roughly 97% accuracy. Gmail's spam detection (using deep learning) achieves over 99.9% precision (few false positives) with high recall — filtering billions of spam messages per day with an imperceptibly small false positive rate for legitimate mail.

## History of the application

Spam became a serious problem with the rise of commercial internet in the mid-1990s. Paul Graham's 2002 essay "A Plan for Spam" popularised the naïve Bayes approach and triggered a wave of adoption. It remains one of the best-known real-world demonstrations that a simple probability model can outperform elaborate heuristic rule systems.
