## The application

The UK Labour Force Survey interviews around 50,000 households per quarter to estimate national employment statistics — unemployment rate, average earnings, participation by age and region — for a population of 67 million. That a sample of 0.07% can accurately characterise the whole is not obvious; it follows from probability theory, and the margin of error can be calculated exactly. Survey statistics is one of the oldest and most practically significant applications of mathematical probability.

## The maths behind it

**Simple random sampling**: in a population of size $N$, a simple random sample of size $n$ gives an unbiased estimator of the population mean $\bar{Y}$: the sample mean $\hat{\bar{Y}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in s} y_i$. Its variance is:

$$\text{Var}(\hat{\bar{Y}}) = \frac{S^2}{n}\left(1 - \frac{n}{N}\right),$$

where $S^2 = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \bar{Y})^2$ is the population variance. The factor $(1 - n/N)$ is the **finite population correction** — the variance is reduced when the sample is a substantial fraction of the population.

**Stratification**: partition the population into $H$ strata (e.g., by region, age group) and sample each stratum independently. The **stratified estimator** $\hat{\bar{Y}}_{st} = \sum_h W_h \hat{\bar{Y}}_h$ (weighted average of stratum estimates) has lower variance than simple random sampling when strata are internally homogeneous.

**Ratio and regression estimation**: auxiliary variables correlated with the outcome (e.g., previous census data) can dramatically reduce variance. The regression estimator $\hat{\bar{Y}}_{reg} = \hat{\bar{Y}} + \hat{\beta}(\bar{X} - \hat{\bar{X}})$ adjusts the sample estimate by the known population mean of the auxiliary variable, achieving near-optimal efficiency when the regression relationship is tight.

**Complex sample designs** (multi-stage clustering, probability proportional to size) require specialised variance estimators such as Taylor linearisation or the jackknife/bootstrap replication methods. Ignoring design effects and treating a clustered survey as a simple random sample systematically understates uncertainty — a common error in media reporting of polls.

## A concrete example

The 2021 England and Wales Census achieved a response rate of 97%, covering all 24 million households. However, even a full census has undercoverage (hard-to-reach populations) and overcoverage (double-counting). The ONS estimates these using a post-census survey (the Census Coverage Survey) and applies a dual-system estimation procedure derived from capture-recapture theory — the same mathematics used to estimate wildlife populations from mark-recapture data.

## History of the application

Laplace used a form of ratio estimation to estimate the population of France in 1802 from partial registration data. The modern theory of probability-based sampling was developed by Jerzy Neyman in a 1934 paper that introduced stratified sampling and the concept of confidence intervals in the survey context. Random digit dialling and online panels have extended these methods to new modalities while preserving the same theoretical foundation.
